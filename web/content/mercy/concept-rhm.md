### The womb–mercy etymology

Lemma #5 is the structural revelation of the root. **`>aroHaAm` literally means "wombs"** — the same trilateral consonants ر-ح-م that name divine mercy also name the maternal organ. In classical Arabic lexicography (Lane, Ibn Manẓūr), this is not a coincidence: *raḥma* is etymologically derived from *raḥim* (womb), and the bond of *kinship* is also called *raḥim*. The root projects a single semantic field across:

1. The maternal/biological bond (womb → kinship → "blood relations")
2. The protective/nurturing emotion (mercy, compassion)
3. The supreme divine attribute (Allah as ar-Raḥmān, ar-Raḥīm)

Three of the 12 wombs-occurrences make the legal-kinship sense explicit:
- **4:1** — addresses humanity to "fear the Lord… and the wombs" (i.e. honor kinship ties)
- **47:22** — warns against "cutting your wombs" (severing kinship)
- **60:3** — "your wombs and your children will not benefit you on Judgment Day"

The other 9 occurrences are biological/embryological (e.g. **3:6**, **13:8**, **22:5**, **31:34**: God's knowledge of what is in the wombs).

This single lemma is why the entire root sits at the center of Quranic theology rather than being an abstract attribute — divine mercy is named after the most concrete, visceral bond a human knows.

**Inferred concept clusters** (from co-occurrence structure):

1. **Divine attribute cluster** — Allah + Lord + Knowing + (mercy). Stable formulaic pairing. About a third of mercy ayahs are essentially God-attribute statements.
2. **Salvation cluster** — forgiveness + faith + (mercy). Mercy as the pathway out of sin, conditional on faith. Roughly the next third.
3. **Sovereignty cluster** — will + people + (mercy). Mercy as discretionary divine grant, not entitlement. Smaller but theologically distinct.

These three clusters substantially overlap in many ayahs but the data clearly separates them. They give us a first hypothesis for what *mercy* structurally *is* in the Quran: an attribute (#1), a transactional path (#2), and a sovereign act (#3).

## Distribution patterns

### Meccan vs Medinan
- Meccan (early period): **226 occurrences (67%)** — overwhelmingly the period of theological foundation. The divine names (`r~aHoma`n`, `r~aHiym`) are concentrated here.
- Medinan (community period): **113 occurrences (33%)** — mercy persists but the mode shifts toward legal / inter-personal contexts (kinship, forgiveness within community, mercy as social conduct).

**Al-Fātiḥa stands alone** — the opening surah is densest in mercy by a factor of 2.5×. Of its 7 ayahs, 4 invoke the root: *bismi-llāhi r-raḥmāni r-raḥīm* (1:1), *ar-raḥmāni r-raḥīm* (1:3). The Quran's opening prayer is structurally a meditation on this single attribute.

**Surah Maryam** — second by density and largest by absolute count after Al-Baqara — is the prophet-narrative surah. Mercy is the recurring frame for how prophets are described and addressed.

## Illustrative ayahs — with the four-translator disagreement panel

After adding the translation layer, every occurrence in `data/concepts/rHm.json` carries a literal word-by-word gloss plus four full-sentence translations (Saheeh International, Pickthall, Khattab, Arberry). The disagreements between translators are themselves data — they mark places where English cannot agree on what the Arabic is doing.

**1:1 — the Bismillah, both divine names side by side**

| | |
|---|---|
| Arabic | بِسۡمِ ٱللَّهِ ٱلرَّحۡمَٰنِ ٱلرَّحِيمِ |
| Literal (al-Raḥmān only) | "the Most Gracious" |
| Saheeh | "In the name of Allah, the Entirely Merciful, the Especially Merciful" |
| Pickthall | "In the name of Allah, the Beneficent, the Merciful" |
| Khattab | "In the Name of Allah—the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful" |
| Arberry | "In the Name of God, the Merciful, the Compassionate" |

Four translators, eight different English words for the *same two Arabic words*. Khattab and Arberry literally swap which name gets "Compassionate." There is no settled English. The Arabic *raḥmān/raḥīm* pairing is doing structural work that English can only approximate.

**4:1 — wombs as ethical command**

| | |
|---|---|
| Arabic word | وَٱلۡأَرۡحَامَ ("and the wombs") |
| Literal | "and the wombs" |
| Saheeh | "…and the wombs" |
| Pickthall | "…and toward the wombs (that bare you)" |
| Khattab | "…and ˹honour˺ family ties" |
| Arberry | "…and the wombs" |

**Three of four preserve "wombs." Khattab alone replaces it with "family ties"** — and adds the bracketed "honour" that isn't in the Arabic at all. Khattab's choice is defensible (the verse does mean kinship obligation) but it *removes the visceral image*. Without the disagreement panel, you'd miss that this interpretive choice was made.

**60:3 — the same word, but everyone flattens it**

| | |
|---|---|
| Arabic word | أَرۡحَامُكُمۡ ("your wombs") |
| Literal | "your relatives" |
| Saheeh | "your relatives" |
| Pickthall | "your ties of kindred" |
| Khattab | "your relatives" |
| Arberry | "your blood-kindred" |

Same Arabic word as 4:1. Same root, same lemma. But here **all five English sources (including the literal gloss) avoid "wombs"** and shift to relatives/kindred. Why? Context: this verse is about Judgment Day, when biological kin won't help. English forces a choice between the physical and social senses of *raḥim* that Arabic does not. The fact that translators flip on the same word between 4:1 and 60:3 is itself a finding — a signal that the Arabic word always means *both* and the translator is selecting one.

**21:107 — the prophetic mission as mercy**

| | |
|---|---|
| Arabic | وَمَآ أَرۡسَلۡنَٰكَ إِلَّا رَحۡمَةࣰ لِّلۡعَٰلَمِينَ |
| Literal (key word) | "(as) a mercy" |
| Saheeh | "And We have not sent you, [O Muhammad], except as a mercy to the worlds" |
| Pickthall | "We sent thee not save as a mercy for the peoples" |
| Khattab | "We have sent you ˹O Prophet˺ only as a mercy for the whole world" |
| Arberry | "We have not sent thee, save as a mercy unto all beings" |

Stable: *raḥma* → "mercy" (all four agree). Variable: *al-ʿālamīn* → "the worlds" / "the peoples" / "the whole world" / "all beings." When translators converge, the meaning is well-bounded. When they diverge, that's where Arabic is doing something English can't.

### What the translation panel adds methodologically

1. **The wombs–mercy etymological insight survives translation.** "Wombs" is preserved by 4 of 5 sources in 4:1. The reading is not an artifact of pre-modern Arabic alone.
2. **Khattab is the most interpretive of the four.** He routinely smooths Arabic ambiguity into a single English meaning (4:1 wombs → family ties). Useful as a "what does this mean in plain modern English" reading, but should never be the sole source.
3. **Pickthall preserves Arabic structure most consistently** despite the archaic English. His "wombs (that bare you)" parenthetical is the model of literal-with-clarification.
4. **Saheeh and Arberry sit between** — modern enough to read, literal enough to trust on most words.
5. **The literal word-by-word gloss is itself context-sensitive** — the same Arabic أَرْحَام is rendered "the wombs" in 4:1 and "your relatives" in 60:3 by the same source. Flag this: even the "literal" layer is making interpretive choices. We should treat it as the lowest-bias gloss but not as ground truth. When in doubt, fall back to the lemma + root + POS.

The Arabic remains the only ground truth. The translations together give us a defensible English reading without committing to any single one.

## What the data tells us about "mercy"

A first-principles reading from the structure alone — not from commentary:

1. **Mercy is not soft.** It pairs with forgiveness, but also with judgment, will, sovereignty. The dominant verbal subject is God; the recipients are "whom He wills."
2. **Mercy is the central divine attribute by frequency among non-pronominal attributes.** Only "Allah" (the name itself) and "Lord" (rbb) co-occur more often than mercy pairs with itself across the corpus.
3. **Mercy is rooted in the most physical bond humans know.** The same root that names God's defining attribute also names the womb. This is a structural — not metaphorical — claim of the Arabic language that the Quran inherits and amplifies.
4. **Mercy frames the prophetic mission.** 21:107 is one of the few verses where the Prophet's role is given a one-word definition; the word is *raḥma*.
5. **Mercy concentrates at openings.** Al-Fātiḥa, Bismillah, Maryam (which opens the prophet-narratives) — mercy is the entry-attribute through which other content is approached.

## Schema reflection — what to keep, what to add

This was the proof-of-workflow run for the knowledge graph. What the current schema captured well, and what we should add before scaling to all 1,642 roots.

**Worked well:**
- **Root → Lemma → Occurrence → Ayah → Surah** is a natural and complete spine. Every node has clear identity, every edge has clear semantics.
- **Co-occurrence at the ayah level** is the right unit. It produces interpretable clusters (mercy ↔ forgiveness ↔ Lord) without spurious noise. Surah-level would be too broad; word-window would be too narrow.
- **POS distribution per lemma** distinguishes attribute-mercy (ADJ-heavy) from action-mercy (V) from object-mercy (N).

**Done since first draft:**
- ✅ **Translation alignment per ayah.** All 4 sentence translations (Saheeh, Pickthall, Khattab, Arberry) plus literal word-by-word from quran.com now attached to every occurrence. Disagreements between translators preserved as research signal.

**Still to add for the next concept:**

1. **Surrounding-ayah context.** Show the ayah ±1 window. Some occurrences are mid-argument and need the lead-in.
2. **Co-occurrence at the *lemma* level**, not just root. Knowing that `raHomap` (mercy-as-noun) co-occurs heavily with `magofirap` (forgiveness-as-noun) is more informative than just rHm ↔ gfr.
3. **Concept clusters as first-class objects.** The 3 clusters I inferred above (attribute / salvation / sovereignty) should be derivable structurally — e.g. by finding co-occurrence sub-graphs among the top-10 co-roots. This is the next graph operation.
4. **Asbāb al-nuzūl + revelation order** as ayah-level metadata. Several occurrences above only fully make sense in their historical context (e.g. 60:3 and 47:22 on kinship).
5. **Cross-root etymology links.** ر-ح-م and ر-ح-ب share two letters and a related semantic field (mercy / spaciousness). The graph should support n-letter root similarity edges so semantic fields can extend across roots, not stop at the root boundary.
6. **Translator-divergence flag at ayah level.** When the four translators disagree substantially on a verse, flag it as a "high-divergence" ayah — these are first-priority candidates for direct Arabic study.

**Open question:** is "mercy" one concept or three? The data supports a single root-anchored concept with three behavioral clusters. The graph should probably model both: one Concept node anchored on the root, with sub-clusters as views or sub-graphs. We don't need to commit to that until we've done 3–5 more roots and seen whether the same clustering pattern holds.
