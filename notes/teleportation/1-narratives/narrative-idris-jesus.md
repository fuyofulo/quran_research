# Narrative: Idrīs and Jesus — the Quran's two human-vertical-transport cases

> A paired reading of the Quran's two named-prophet vertical-translation passages: the bare three-word `wa rafaʿnāhu makānan ʿaliyyā` for Idrīs (19:57; cf. 21:85-86), and the dual-operation `mutawaffīka wa rāfiʿuka ilayya` for Jesus (3:55; cf. 4:157-158, 5:117). Both events use the same root — **rfE** (raise) — and both are **one-way**: neither prophet is described in the Quran as descending afterward. Yet on every other axis they diverge sharply. Idrīs goes to a **place** (`makānan ʿaliyyā`); Jesus goes to **God Himself** (`ilayya / ilayh`). Idrīs is just *raised*; Jesus is *taken-then-raised*. Idrīs gets one half-verse; Jesus gets a polemical four-verse argument. The Quran reserves vertical-permanent translation for exactly two humans, and the asymmetry between the two cases is the chief data of this analysis.

This file ties together the work in `concept-rfE.md` (the verb), `concept-wfy.md` (the tawaffā operation), and `0-structural/teleportation-divergence.md` (translator behavior on these verses) — and adds what they do not cover: the structural why of Jesus's added tawaffā, the connection to the broader vertical-cosmology, and the negative-space inventory for the pair.

---

## 1. Headline finding

The Quran says exactly two named human beings have been raised vertically off the earth: **Idrīs** and **Jesus**. Both events are reported with the verb `rafaʿa` (root rfE). Both are one-way in the canonical text — neither prophet is described as returning. The two cases share *one verb* and *one direction* and **differ on every other axis**:

| Axis | Idrīs | Jesus |
|---|---|---|
| Verb(s) | `rafaʿa` only | `tawaffā` + `rafaʿa` (3:55); `rafaʿa` alone (4:158) |
| Destination | `makānan ʿaliyyā` — "a lofty place" | `ilayya` / `ilayh` — "to Me / to Him" |
| Verses | 1.5 (19:56-57; 21:85-86 names without raising) | 4 (3:55; 4:157-158; 5:117) |
| Polemical context | None — bare statement | Direct (4:157 denies crucifixion claim) |
| Tawaffā paired? | No | Yes (3:55, 5:117) |
| Death-status | Unspecified | Disputed; no translator commits |
| Return in Quranic text | None | None |
| Tradition fills the gap with | Identification with Enoch | The eschatological return |

The pair forms the **trans-cosmic register of rfE** identified in `concept-rfE.md` §C: three verses (19:57, 3:55, 4:158) where rfE behaves differently from its 26 other occurrences — the destination is not a topographical place or a social rank but a directional-divine location, the subject is a specific named prophet, and there is no descent reported.

The two cases are the *only* cases in the Quran of a HUMAN-AS-PATIENT undergoing one-way vertical transport. Angels go up and come down. Souls go up at death and return for resurrection. The Quran descends, rain descends, scriptures descend. Adam falls (`hbT`) and Noah's ark settles. Muhammad is taken horizontally by night and is back by morning. **Only Idrīs and Jesus are taken up and not brought back.**

## 2. The Idrīs passages

### 19:56-57

> وَٱذۡكُرۡ فِي ٱلۡكِتَٰبِ إِدۡرِيسَۚ إِنَّهُۥ كَانَ صِدِّيقࣰا نَّبِيࣰّا
> وَرَفَعۡنَٰهُ مَكَانًا عَلِيࣰّا

**Saheeh:** "And mention in the Book, Idrees. Indeed, he was a man of truth and a prophet. **And We raised him to a high station.**"

**Pickthall:** "And make mention in the Scripture of Idris. Lo! he was a saint, a prophet; **and We raised him to high station.**"

**Khattab:** "And mention in the Book ˹O Prophet, the story of˺ Enoch. He was surely a man of truth and a prophet. **And We elevated him to an honourable status.**"

**Arberry:** "And mention in the Book Idris; he was a true man, a Prophet. **We raised him up to a high place.**"

### 21:85-86

> وَإِسۡمَٰعِيلَ وَإِدۡرِيسَ وَذَا ٱلۡكِفۡلِۖ كُلࣱّ مِّنَ ٱلصَّٰبِرِينَ
> وَأَدۡخَلۡنَٰهُمۡ فِي رَحۡمَتِنَآۖ إِنَّهُم مِّنَ ٱلصَّٰلِحِينَ

**Saheeh:** "And [mention] Ishmael and Idrees and Dhul-Kifl; all were of the patient. And We admitted them into Our mercy. Indeed, they were of the righteous."

This is **Idrīs's only other Quranic mention**. He is named in a list of three (Ishmael, Idrīs, Dhū al-Kifl), characterized by `ṣabr` (patience/endurance), and admitted into divine mercy. **No raising is mentioned here.** The Idrīs corpus in the Quran is therefore *one and a half ayahs total*: the half-verse 19:57 carrying the rfE event, and 21:85 placing him in a virtue-list. He is the most lightly-attested major prophet figure in the Quran.

### Identification

The post-Quranic tradition identifies Idrīs with the biblical **Enoch** (Genesis 5:21-24, the seventh patriarch from Adam, of whom "God took him"). Khattab makes this explicit by replacing "Idrīs" with "Enoch" in his rendering — the only one of the four. The Hebrew text says `wa-ʾēnenû kī lāqaḥ ʾōtō ʾĕlōhīm` — "and he was no more, for God took him" — using `lāqaḥ` (took), the same conceptual move as the Quranic `rafaʿnāhu`. The tradition's identification of Idrīs = Enoch is grounded on the shared "vertical translation without death" pattern. The Quran does not assert this identification; it gives a name (Idrīs) and a verb (rafaʿa) and lets the reader supply the cross-tradition link.

### The "lofty place" question

The phrase **`makānan ʿaliyyā`** is the entire crux. Translation divergence (cf. `concept-rfE.md` §"The 19:57 puzzle"):

- Saheeh: "a high station"
- Pickthall: "high station"
- Khattab: "an honourable status" — outlier
- Arberry: "a high place"

`makān` is morphologically derived from `kawn` (being, location) and is **strongly spatial** elsewhere in the Quran: 19:16 `makānan sharqiyyā` ("an eastern place," Maryam's withdrawal), 19:22 `makānan qaṣiyyā` ("a far place," the birthplace of Jesus), 11:93 `makānatikum` ("your place/position"). The same surah uses the same construction *immediately above* (19:22) and *immediately below* (19:57) — a horizontal `makān` for Maryam, a vertical `makān` for Idrīs. The parallel is conspicuous.

`ʿaliyyā` ("lofty, exalted, elevated") is from the same root as `al-ʿAlī` ("the Most High"), one of God's names (cf. 2:255, Āyat al-Kursī: `wa huwa al-ʿaliyyu al-ʿaẓīm`). When applied to a place, it can mean physically elevated or in-honor exalted — Arabic does not make this distinction lexically.

**The data-grounded reading:** `makānan ʿaliyyā` is a literal-spatial construction, structurally parallel to `makānan qaṣiyyā` at 19:22. Maryam withdrew **horizontally** to a far place; Idrīs was raised **vertically** to a lofty place. The two are mirror images. Khattab's "honourable status" is a theological softening that decouples the verse from its own surah's syntactic neighborhood. The Quran does not say *where* the lofty place is; it says only that there is one and that Idrīs is in it.

## 3. The Jesus passages

### 3:55

> إِذۡ قَالَ ٱللَّهُ يَٰعِيسَىٰٓ إِنِّي مُتَوَفِّيكَ وَرَافِعُكَ إِلَيَّ وَمُطَهِّرُكَ مِنَ ٱلَّذِينَ كَفَرُواْ وَجَاعِلُ ٱلَّذِينَ ٱتَّبَعُوكَ فَوۡقَ ٱلَّذِينَ كَفَرُوٓاْ إِلَىٰ يَوۡمِ ٱلۡقِيَٰمَةِۖ ثُمَّ إِلَيَّ مَرۡجِعُكُمۡ فَأَحۡكُمُ بَيۡنَكُمۡ فِيمَا كُنتُمۡ فِيهِ تَخۡتَلِفُونَ

**Saheeh:** "[Mention] when Allah said, 'O Jesus, **indeed I will take you and raise you to Myself and purify you from those who disbelieve** and make those who follow you superior to those who disbelieve until the Day of Resurrection...'"

**Pickthall:** "...O Jesus! Lo! **I am gathering thee and causing thee to ascend unto Me, and am cleansing thee** of those who disbelieve..."

**Khattab:** "...'O Jesus! **I will take you and raise you up to Myself. I will deliver you** from those who disbelieve...'"

**Arberry:** "...'Jesus, **I will take thee to Me and will raise thee to Me and I will purify thee** of those who believe not...'"

### 4:157-158

> وَقَوۡلِهِمۡ إِنَّا قَتَلۡنَا ٱلۡمَسِيحَ عِيسَى ٱبۡنَ مَرۡيَمَ رَسُولَ ٱللَّهِ وَمَا قَتَلُوهُ وَمَا صَلَبُوهُ وَلَٰكِن شُبِّهَ لَهُمۡۚ وَإِنَّ ٱلَّذِينَ ٱخۡتَلَفُواْ فِيهِ لَفِي شَكࣲّ مِّنۡهُۚ مَا لَهُم بِهِۦ مِنۡ عِلۡمٍ إِلَّا ٱتِّبَاعَ ٱلظَّنِّۚ وَمَا قَتَلُوهُ يَقِينَۢا
> بَل رَّفَعَهُ ٱللَّهُ إِلَيۡهِۚ وَكَانَ ٱللَّهُ عَزِيزًا حَكِيمࣰا

**Saheeh (4:157-158):** "And [for] their saying, 'Indeed, we have killed the Messiah, Jesus, the son of Mary, the messenger of Allah.' **And they did not kill him, nor did they crucify him; but [another] was made to resemble him to them.** And indeed, those who differ over it are in doubt about it... **Rather, Allah raised him to Himself.** And ever is Allah Exalted in Might and Wise."

**Pickthall (4:158):** "**But Allah took him up unto Himself.** Allah was ever Mighty, Wise."

**Khattab (4:158):** "Rather, Allah raised him up to Himself."

**Arberry (4:158):** "God raised him up to Him; God is All-mighty, All-wise."

### 5:117

> فَلَمَّا تَوَفَّيۡتَنِي كُنتَ أَنتَ ٱلرَّقِيبَ عَلَيۡهِمۡ

Jesus, addressing God on Judgment Day: "...And I was a witness over them as long as I was among them; **but when You took me up [tawaffaytanī], You were the Observer over them...**"

This third Jesus-verse closes the loop on the *tawaffā*-of-Jesus question. Jesus himself uses the verb `tawaffaytanī` to mark the moment when his earthly witness-role ended. The verb-form is past-tense, first-person-direct-object — "when You tawaffā-ed me" — and the setting is eschatological (Judgment Day). The statement is consistent with both the death-and-soul-collection reading and the take-up-alive reading; it specifies only that *some* tawaffā-event has occurred which terminated Jesus's worldly-witness-tenure.

### The dual operation of 3:55

3:55 stacks **three sequential operations** on Jesus, all expressed as Form-V participles taking Jesus as object:

1. `mutawaffīka` — "taking-you-fully" (root wfy, Form V active participle, hapax in this morphological form per `concept-wfy.md` lemma table)
2. `rāfiʿuka ilayya` — "raising-you to Me" (root rfE, active participle)
3. `muṭahhiruka mina alladhīna kafarū` — "purifying-you from those who disbelieved" (root Thr, Form II active participle)

Three operations, one subject (God), one object (Jesus), three different verbal roots. The grammatical layering is a *temporal stack* in classical Arabic syntax — the three participles are in coordinate sequence after `innī` ("indeed I am"). Whether the stack reads as **strict sequence** (first take, then raise, then purify) or as **simultaneous bundle** (the three are aspects of one event) is the load-bearing exegetical question.

The Idrīs verse is **bare**. The Jesus verses are **argumentative** — they exist within a textual machinery whose target is a specific Christian claim. This is the structural difference and accounts for almost everything else: Idrīs's raising has no rhetorical work to do, so the Quran does not elaborate; Jesus's raising must explicitly displace the death-and-resurrection sequence of the gospel narrative, so the Quran stages a four-verse argument with `bal` ("rather"), `mā qatalūhu` ("they did not kill him"), `shubbiha lahum` ("[another] was made to resemble"), and the corrective `bal rafaʿahu Allāhu ilayh`.

## 5. The tawaffā puzzle of 3:55 — depth read

The verb `tawaffā` (root wfy, Form V) is the Quran's **clinical operative term for soul-collection at death** (cf. `concept-wfy.md` headline finding). In its 24 Form-V occurrences, it overwhelmingly denotes the death-event: God or the angel of death `tawaffā`-s the soul. The single locus classicus is 39:42:

> `Allāhu yatawaffā al-anfusa ḥīna mawtihā wa-llatī lam tamut fī manāmihā` — "Allah takes-fully the souls at the time of their death, and those that have not died, in their sleep."

Sleep and death are the **same operation** with the difference being whether the soul is withheld (death) or released (waking). 32:11 specifies the angel of death as agent (`yatawaffākum malaku al-mawti`).

Apply this directly to 3:55 and the verse reads: "I will take you in death and raise you to Me." This is grammatically licensed and lexically standard.

But the translators do not render it that way. Look at the divergence panel from `concept-wfy.md` §"Translation Divergence":

| Translator | 3:55 `mutawaffīka` |
|---|---|
| Saheeh | "I will take you" |
| Pickthall | "I am gathering thee" |
| Khattab | "I will take you" |
| Arberry | "I will take thee to Me" |

**Not one** translator commits to "cause to die." All four use deliberately ambiguous English verbs of taking. Pickthall's "gathering" is the most innovative — he picks an English verb that approximates wfy's "fullness-of-receiving" semantics without committing to death. The translation tradition has *systematically declined* the standard reading on this single verse.

### Sequence vs reorder

Two readings of the 3:55 stack `mutawaffīka + rāfiʿuka + muṭahhiruka`:

**Reading A — strict sequence.** Take, then raise, then purify. On this reading, `tawaffā` is the death-event (or some non-death taking), `rafʿ` is the subsequent ascension, and `ṭahr` is the post-ascension purification. This is the natural reading of the syntactic stack and is supported by the Form-V → active-participle → active-participle pattern.

**Reading B — non-sequential / theological reorder.** The three operations are *aspects* of a single event, not steps. On this reading the order in the verse is rhetorical, not chronological. Some classical commentators read the stack as `rāfiʿuka → muṭahhiruka → mutawaffīka` chronologically — first raise, then purify, then `tawaffā` at the eschaton (or at the post-return death). This is not what the verse says in order, but Arabic syntax does not strictly require coordinated participles to be temporally sequenced.

### Four classical exegetical readings

Per the rabbit-hole catalog in `concept-wfy.md`:

(a) **Sleep-then-ascent** (attributed to Ibn ʿAbbās). `tawaffā` = sleep — the partial-tawaffā pattern from 39:42. Jesus is put to sleep, then raised bodily.

(b) **Death-then-ascent** (al-Ṭabarī's preference). `tawaffā` = a temporary death; the soul is taken; the body or soul (or both) is raised.

(c) **Take-up-without-death** (Ibn Kathīr, majority Sunni). `tawaffā` = the taking-fully without death — the verb's general "complete-fully-receiving" sense applied to a person rather than a soul. Jesus is taken up alive.

(d) **End-of-mission, not body** (al-Manār, modernist). `tawaffā` = the completion of Jesus's earthly *prophetic mission*, not the body. The "raising" is the elevation of his teaching/status.

**All four are philologically defensible.** The verb's polysemy (death, sleep, take-fully, complete) is real and grammatically licensed in the Quran. The four-way exegetical split is not a sign of textual ambiguity in the modern sense — it is a sign that the verb does what it does, and the readers are forced to choose.

The translators evade this choice. **All four (Saheeh, Pickthall, Khattab, Arberry) preserve the ambiguity.** None commits to (a), (b), (c), or (d). The translation tradition is more conservative than the exegetical tradition. The evasion is the tribute to the verb's load.

## 6. The 4:157-158 crucifixion question

> وَمَا قَتَلُوهُ وَمَا صَلَبُوهُ وَلَٰكِن شُبِّهَ لَهُمۡ
> ... وَمَا قَتَلُوهُ يَقِينَۢا
> بَل رَّفَعَهُ ٱللَّهُ إِلَيۡهِ

"They did not kill him and did not crucify him, but [another] was made to resemble him for them... they did not kill him, certainly. Rather, Allah raised him to Himself."

Three propositions stacked:

1. **Negation of killing** — `mā qatalūhu` — flat denial. Repeated: `wa mā qatalūhu yaqīnan` ("certainly they did not kill him").
2. **Negation of crucifixion** — `wa mā ṣalabūhu` — flat denial of the specific historical method.
3. **The `shubbiha lahum` mechanism** — `wa lākin shubbiha lahum` — "but [it/another] was made to resemble [him] for them."

The verb `shubbiha` is a Form II passive perfect, 3sg masculine, of the root sh-b-h ("to resemble, be similar"). The subject is **suppressed**. The verse does not say what was made to resemble what — only that there was a resemblance-event in the perception of "them." Translation divergence:

- Saheeh: "but [another] was made to resemble him to them"
- Pickthall: "but it appeared so unto them"
- Khattab: "it was only made to appear so"
- Arberry: "only a likeness of that was shown to them"

Saheeh inserts `[another]` (a person was substituted). Pickthall, Khattab, and Arberry leave the subject open — a *something* was made to resemble — which could be a person, an event, or a perceptual illusion. The Arabic alone does not specify.

### Three classical readings of 4:157-158

**(i) Raised alive — substitution.** The most prevalent classical Sunni reading: someone other than Jesus was made to resemble him; that person was crucified; Jesus was raised alive without dying. This is the *substitute crucifixion* reading. It pairs naturally with reading (c) of `tawaffā` in §5: take-up-without-death.

**(ii) Raised alive — perceptual illusion.** Jesus was raised; *it appeared* to the killers that they had killed him; the appearance was the illusion, not a substituted body. The crucifixion they thought they performed was a non-event — they crucified empty air, or the cross was empty. Pickthall's "it appeared so unto them" leans this direction.

**(iii) Died-then-raised.** A minority classical reading: Jesus did die (briefly, on the cross or otherwise); the killers were correct that *something* died but wrong that they had killed God's messenger; God then raised him. This reading takes 3:55's `tawaffā` as death and threads it with 4:157's `mā qatalūhu` as a denial-of-agency rather than a denial-of-death (i.e., "they did not kill him — God did, by tawaffā").

All three are philologically licensed. The Quran's text is **calibrated to permit** all three readings — and the tradition's four-way exegetical split tracks the readings.

### What `bal rafaʿahu Allāhu ilayh` does

The adversative particle `bal` ("rather, but instead") in 4:158 sets `rafʿ` as the **substitute term for `qatl`**. The structure is:

- claim: "we killed him"
- counter: NOT killing, NOT crucifixion
- correction: `bal` — "rather" — `rafaʿahu Allāhu` — "Allah raised him."

`Rafʿ` is not just an additional fact; it is **the term that takes the rhetorical place of `qatl`**. Whatever the metaphysics of Jesus's terminus, the Quran insists that the operative verb is upward (rfE), not graveward (qatl, ṣalb, dafn). This is the structural point — and it is the one the Quranic text explicitly makes.

The terminus of 4:158 — `ilayh`, "to Him" — is the **same destination** as 3:55's `ilayya` ("to Me"). And it is the same destination as the angels-and-the-Spirit's ascent in 70:4: `taʿruju al-malāʾiku wa al-rūḥu **ilayh** fī yawmin kāna miqdāruhu khamsīna alfa sanah` ("the angels and the Spirit ascend to Him in a day whose measure is fifty thousand years"). Jesus's terminus is the same as the angels' terminus. The vertical-cosmology is consistent across the corpus.

## 7. Translator strategies

The translators' behavior on these four passages tracks the rules established in `0-structural/teleportation-divergence.md`:

### 19:57 — Khattab spiritualizes

Three of four translators preserve `makānan ʿaliyyā` as spatial (high station / high station / high place). Khattab alone reads it as honorific ("an honourable status"). This is the cleanest two-position dispute in the teleportation slice. Khattab's move is consistent with his broader translation strategy of *resolving* ambiguous Quranic spatial language into theological-honorific equivalents. The cost is that the surah's internal parallel structure (`makānan sharqiyyā` 19:16, `makānan qaṣiyyā` 19:22) collapses — Khattab is forced to render those *spatially* (he does) and 19:57 *honorifically*, breaking the within-surah pattern.

### 3:55 — all four evade tawaffā

As noted in §5: all four translators decline the standard "cause to die" reading of `tawaffā`. They converge on neutral "take" or, in Pickthall's case, "gather." The convergent evasion is the tribute. **The single most theologically explosive verb in the Quran is also the verb on which all four translators agree to be vague.**

Arberry is the lone outlier on 3:55 only on a *rhetorical* axis — he uses "to Me / to Me" twice in close succession (`I will take thee to Me and will raise thee to Me`) where the others vary the destination phrasing. Arberry's choice mimics the Arabic's `ilayya` more closely; the others smooth it. This is not a theological divergence; it is a stylistic one.

### 4:157 — outliers on `shubbiha lahum`

- **Saheeh** is the only translator to insert an explicit subject: `[another] was made to resemble him to them`. The bracketed `[another]` is interpretive — Saheeh commits to substitution-reading (i). The other three leave the subject open.
- **Pickthall:** "but it appeared so unto them" — drifts toward perceptual-illusion-reading (ii).
- **Khattab:** "it was only made to appear so" — same as Pickthall but cleaner.
- **Arberry:** "only a likeness of that was shown to them" — strict participial: a `likeness-of-that` (shibh) was shown.

### 4:158 — Pickthall's "took him up" outlier

Pickthall is the **lone outlier** on 4:158 with `took him up` instead of `raised`. This is the most aggressive interpretive move on rfE in the entire teleportation slice (per `concept-rfE.md` §Translation divergence). Pickthall's choice imports the verb `take` (which is the standard rendering of *tawaffā*) into a verse that does not contain *tawaffā* — implicitly fusing 3:55's two operations into 4:158's single one. This may be deliberate harmonization with 3:55, or it may be Pickthall's archaic English stylistic preference for "took up" as the natural English idiom for vertical translation. Either way it stands alone against three "raised" renderings.

### Pattern summary

The four passages span four different translator-outlier patterns: Khattab on 19:57 (spatial→honorific), Arberry on 3:55 (rhetorical mimicry), Saheeh on 4:157 (substitution-reading commitment), Pickthall on 4:158 ("took up"). **Each translator outliers on a different verse for a different reason.** This is consistent with the broader finding that on theologically-loaded teleportation verses, outlier rates rise and the outlying *direction* is translator-specific. There is no single "consensus" reading on the Idrīs/Jesus pair; there is consensus on the *bare facts* (something happened, vertical, one-way) and disagreement on every *qualification*.

|---|---|---|---|---|
| Idrīs | Up | rfE | One-way | 19:57 |
| Jesus | Up | rfE (+ wfy at 3:55) | One-way (Quranic; tradition adds return) | 3:55, 4:158 |
| Muhammad | Horizontal | sry | Round-trip (one night) | 17:1 |
| Angels | Both directions | nzl (down), Erj (up) | Bidirectional | 19:64, 32:5, 70:4, 97:4 |
| The Spirit | Both directions | nzl, Erj | Bidirectional | 70:4, 78:38, 16:102 |
| Souls (general) | Up at death | wfy (`tawaffā`) | Will return at resurrection | 32:11, 39:42 |
| Souls (sleep) | Up at night | wfy partial | Released back | 6:60, 39:42 |
| Quran | Down | nzl (and intensives) | One-way | 17:106, 2:23, 26:193 |
| Rain, provision | Down | nzl | One-way | 2:22 |
| Adam | Down | hbT | One-way (the fall) | 2:36, 7:24 |
| Mountain over Israel | Both directions | rfE up; descent implicit | Coercive | 2:63, 2:93, 4:154 |

Patterns visible in this grid:

1. **Idrīs and Jesus are the only HUMAN one-way ascents.** Every other up-going entity (angels, Spirit, souls) is bidirectional or returns. Every other down-going entity (Quran, rain, Adam) goes once. The human-one-way-up cell has exactly two occupants.

2. **Vertical motion is policed; horizontal motion is open.** Angels descend `bi-idhni rabbihim` (97:4 — "by the permission of their Lord"); jinn who attempt vertical ascent without permission are repelled by missile-stars (37:7-10, 67:5, 72:8). But horizontal extraordinary motion (sry, the Night Journey) does not invoke this gatekeeping language. The vertical axis is a *guarded* axis; the horizontal is not. Cross-link: `narrative-angels-and-jinn.md`.

3. **Vertical motion correlates with non-return for humans.** Muhammad (horizontal, sry) goes by night and is back by morning (17:1). Idrīs and Jesus (vertical, rfE) do not return in the canonical text. This is the architecture: vertical = exit-from-earthly-bounds = no return; horizontal = extraordinary-intra-earthly = round-trip.

4. **Vertical motion correlates with time-distortion.** Per `concept-rfE.md` §"Connection to the time investigation": angelic ascent at 32:5 (1,000-year day) and 70:4 (50,000-year day) couples vertical traversal with crossing-into-different-time-scales. Horizontal sry preserves earthly time. The structural prediction is that Idrīs and Jesus are not just "elsewhere in space" but "elsewhere on the time-axis" — a prediction the Quran does not refute and the tradition implicitly affirms (Jesus alive in the tradition because he is in a different time-frame, not just a different place).

5. **Reverse direction is reserved.** The Quran has `nzl` (descent) for revelation, angels, rain, provision, and Adam-the-fall — but **never of a prophet returning from a raised position**. Idrīs does not come back. Jesus does not come back *in the Quran*. The descent-of-Jesus tradition is post-Quranic and uses `nzl` (the hadith for the eschatological return reads `inna ʿīsā ibn maryam nāzilun fīkum`). The Quranic vocabulary for Jesus's return is *unspecified* — it is the gap the tradition fills.

## 9. Why does Jesus have tawaffā but Idrīs does not?

The structural question. Both are raised — only one is paired with `tawaffā`.

### The polemical answer

3:55 sits inside Surah Āl ʿImrān, a heavily Christological surah (the entire Maryam-Yaḥyā-ʿĪsā cycle 3:33-63). The verse is *responsive*. 4:155-159, where 4:158 sits, is *more directly* polemical: 4:157 explicitly cites the Christian-Jewish claim "we killed him" and denies it. The Jesus-passages are arguing against a specific historical claim; the Idrīs-passages are not arguing against anything.

The Christian narrative includes a death-and-resurrection sequence. To displace this, the Quran needs to address *both* the death-claim (countered by `mā qatalūhu`) and the post-death-event-claim (countered by `bal rafaʿahu`). The dual-operation `mutawaffīka + rāfiʿuka` is structurally calibrated for this dual displacement: it acknowledges *some kind of taking-fully* (which can absorb whatever death-language the listener brings) and pairs it with the upward-translation that displaces resurrection.

Idrīs has no Christian (or Jewish) competing narrative around him. His Quranic mention is sparse precisely because there is no rhetorical work to do. The Quran can say `wa rafaʿnāhu makānan ʿaliyyā` and stop.

### The asymmetry as a methodological signal

This is one of the cleanest examples in the Quran of **the rhetorical context shaping the verbal stack**. The verb-set used on a prophet correlates with the polemical context the verse-set inhabits. Idrīs (no polemic) gets the bare verb. Jesus (active polemic) gets the dual operation plus the `bal`-correction plus the `shubbiha`-mechanism plus the `mā qatalūhu` flat denial plus the eschatological `mā dumtu fīhim ... fa-lammā tawaffaytanī` framing of 5:117.

The methodological prediction is: where the Quran gives a *bare* verb without elaboration, the rhetorical context is consensual; where it gives a *stack* of verbs, the rhetorical context is contested. This is testable across other prophet-cases (Abraham gets bare verbs for his rescue-from-fire at 21:69; Moses gets a stack of verbs for the Red Sea crossing because there is a competing Egyptian narrative). The Idrīs/Jesus pair is the cleanest case because the same root (rfE) is used in both, and the difference between the two stacks is exactly the polemical context.

### The cost

The cost of pairing `tawaffā` with `rafʿ` is exactly the four-way exegetical dispute documented in §5. Once `tawaffā` enters the Jesus-passage, the Quran has to either commit to a single reading of the verb (it does not) or accept that the verse will be readable in multiple ways (it is). The Quran's strategy is to *not commit* — and the cost of non-commitment is fourteen centuries of philological-theological argument. This is the price of rhetorical precision: the verse defeats its specific polemical target (the death-and-resurrection narrative) by being *under-determined* on the metaphysics. It is not under-determined by accident; it is under-determined by design.

## 10. Connection to other teleportation phenomena

### Cross-link to `narrative-isra-miraj.md`

Muhammad's night journey (17:1) uses `sry` (horizontal). 53:1-18 shows him a vision but never names a verb of vertical motion **for him**. The post-Quranic *miʿrāj* tradition fuses 17:1 with 53 into a horizontal-then-vertical composite event — using `Erj`-derived vocabulary that the Quran itself reserves for **angels and the Spirit**, never for Muhammad. The contrast with Jesus is sharp: Jesus is given an explicit `rafʿ ilayh` (4:158) — vertical, to-God; Muhammad is given an explicit `asrā bi-ʿabdihi` (17:1) — horizontal, mosque-to-mosque. **The Quran makes a deliberate vocabulary distinction between the two prophets' translation events.** Jesus's mission ends with vertical departure; Muhammad's continues on the ground.

### Cross-link to `narrative-maryam-mobility.md`

3:55's Jesus is also Maryam's son, and the entire Christological argument of Surah Āl ʿImrān runs through Maryam (3:33-47, the annunciation; 3:45-47, "his name shall be the Messiah, ʿĪsā son of Maryam"; 3:48-50, his teaching; 3:51-54, the disciples; 3:55, the tawaffā-rafʿ). The Quran's strategy is to bracket Jesus's life with two extraordinary operations: birth without male agency (the Spirit-inblowing of 21:91, 66:12), and termination by `mutawaffīka + rāfiʿuka ilayya`. **Both operations involve the vertical axis** — the spirit comes down into Maryam (`nafakhnā fīhā min rūḥinā` — 21:91), and Jesus is taken up to God (`rāfiʿuka ilayya` — 3:55). Jesus is the only Quranic figure whose entry and exit from the world both transit the heaven-earth boundary. Maryam is the locus of the entry; Jesus's own raising is the locus of the exit.

### Cross-link to `narrative-angels-and-jinn.md`

The vertical axis is **policed**. Angels descend by permission. Jinn who try to ascend uninvited are repelled by missile-stars (37:7-10, 67:5, 72:8-9). Muhammad's vertical access is not described in the Quran. **Idrīs and Jesus pass through this policed axis without resistance** — there is no missile-fire, no permission-clause, no gatekeeping language at all. They go up because God raises them. The agency is divine, the patient is the prophet, and the gate is opened by the same operation that closes it for jinn. Cross-link: `narrative-angels-and-jinn.md` §"Asymmetric vertical traffic."

### Cross-link to `concept-wfy.md` — the routine tawaffā at 32:11

> `qul yatawaffākum malaku al-mawti alladhī wukkila bikum thumma ilā rabbikum turjaʿūn`
> "Say: the angel of death, who is in charge of you, will tawaffā you. Then to your Lord you will be returned."

The routine, every-human tawaffā. Compare to 3:55's `mutawaffīka` (Jesus). Three differences:

1. **Agent.** 32:11 — angel of death. 3:55 — God Himself ("I am tawaffā-ing you").
2. **Audience.** 32:11 — every human. 3:55 — Jesus, second-person singular.
3. **Sequel.** 32:11 — `thumma ilā rabbikum turjaʿūn` (then to your Lord you will be returned, eschatological resurrection). 3:55 — `wa rāfiʿuka ilayya` (and raising you to Me, immediate).

The structural difference: in the routine case, tawaffā precedes the long pause-then-resurrection; in Jesus's case, tawaffā is followed *immediately* by `rāfiʿuka`. This is the most concrete textual signal that something atypical is happening at 3:55: the standard tawaffā→intermediate-state→resurrection pattern is short-circuited, with `rafʿ` taking the place of the intermediate state. Jesus does not enter the standard barzakh-and-resurrection cycle; he is taken directly to God.

This is also why reading (a) (sleep-then-ascent, Ibn ʿAbbās) has classical traction: sleep is the *only* form of tawaffā that does not involve the intermediate barzakh state (per 39:42 — sleep is the soul "released back to a stated term"). If the 3:55 tawaffā is sleep-tawaffā, then `rāfiʿuka` follows naturally as the immediate post-sleep operation. Reading (a) preserves the structural shape of the verb; the other readings reshape it.

## 11. Negative space — what the text does not say

1. **No when.** Neither 19:57 (Idrīs) nor 3:55, 4:158 (Jesus) gives a temporal anchor. The Idrīs verse is bare narrative-frame ("And mention in the Book"). The Jesus verses are the historical-Jesus past and the polemical-present, but the *moment of raising* is not dated. There is no "when he was thirty-three" or "after the crucifixion-attempt." The events float free of chronology.

2. **No how.** The Quran does not describe the mechanism. There is no vehicle (no Burāq, no chariot, no cloud), no procedure, no companions, no preparation, no signs, no theophany. `Rafaʿnāhu` and `rafaʿahu`. Two perfect-tense verbs. The mechanism is suppressed; only the result is reported.

3. **No description of life-in-heaven.** The Quran does not narrate what Idrīs is doing in his lofty place or what Jesus is doing in God's nearness. We have no scenes of them praying, teaching, waiting, or interacting with others. The destination is named (`makānan ʿaliyyā`, `ilayya/ilayh`) and then the camera cuts.

4. **No return mechanism.** No descent verse for either. The Quranic vocabulary for descent (`nzl`, `hbT`) is not applied to Idrīs or Jesus. The eschatological-return-of-Jesus tradition is hadith-anchored. The text has no return.

5. **No specification of who or what was crucified.** 4:157 says `shubbiha lahum` ("[it/he] was made to resemble for them") with the subject suppressed. The Quran does not say a substitute died, or no one died, or an illusion appeared. It says *something* was made to resemble. The reader is left to choose between substitution, illusion, or some other mechanism.

6. **No definition of `tawaffā` in this context.** The verse uses the word and does not gloss it. The reader carries the gloss from elsewhere — and the elsewhere licenses death, sleep, and full-taking-without-death. The Quran does not narrow the range.

7. **No internal cross-reference.** 19:57 (Idrīs) and 3:55, 4:158 (Jesus) do not name each other. The Quran does not say "as We raised Idrīs, We raised Jesus" or "this raising is like that one." The pair is a *reader-reconstructed* pair — visible only when you sort all the rfE-verses and see the trans-cosmic register clustering. The Quran scatters the cases and lets the reader assemble them.

8. **No description of Idrīs's mission.** The text says he was `ṣiddīq nabī` (a true man, a prophet) and `min al-ṣābirīn` (of the patient). What he taught, who he taught, where he taught — silent. The most undocumented prophet in the Quran has the cleanest one-way-up event.

9. **No commentary on the raising.** No "and this is a sign for those who reflect," no `āyatun li-l-mutafakkirīn`, no `inna fī dhālika la-ʿibratan`. Idrīs's raising and Jesus's raising are reported and left undeveloped. The Quran does not interpret the events for the reader.

10. **No alternative-fate denial for Idrīs.** Whereas Jesus's raising is set against the alternative claim "they killed him" (`mā qatalūhu`), Idrīs's raising is not set against anything. There is no "they did not bury him" or "his body was not found." The Quran asserts; it does not argue. The Idrīs-raising has no opponents.

## 12. Verses for direct study + rabbit holes

### Verses for direct study

| [s:a] | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| 19:56-57 | The bare Idrīs-raising. Three words for the event itself. Read against the surah's own `makānan` constructions (19:16, 19:22). |
| 21:85-86 | Idrīs's only other Quranic mention. Patience-list, divine-mercy admission. *No raising mentioned* — the asymmetry within the Idrīs corpus. |
| 3:55 | The dual operation `mutawaffīka + rāfiʿuka + muṭahhiruka`. The most theologically loaded teleportation verse in the corpus. |
| 4:157-158 | The polemical correction. `mā qatalūhu wa mā ṣalabūhu wa lākin shubbiha lahum ... bal rafaʿahu Allāhu ilayh`. The crucifixion-question locus. |
| 5:117 | Jesus's first-person `fa-lammā tawaffaytanī`. The third Jesus-verb closing the loop on the tawaffā question. |
| 32:11 | The routine tawaffā by the angel of death. The structural baseline against which 3:55 is atypical. |
| 39:42 | The sleep-and-death tawaffā. Establishes the verb's full semantic range. The Rosetta Stone for reading 3:55. |

### Rabbit holes

1. **Why is Idrīs's raising tucked inside Surah Maryam (19)?** The surah is overwhelmingly about Maryam, Jesus, Yaḥyā, and Zachariah — and yet Idrīs gets his half-verse here, alongside Abraham (19:41-50), Moses (19:51-53), and Ishmael (19:54-55). The structural placement is curious: 19:56-57 (Idrīs) sits between Ishmael and a closing prophetic genealogy. Is the Idrīs-raising rhetorically positioned to *foreshadow* Jesus's raising in the same surah's logic? The Quran's surah-architecture sometimes embeds parallels of this kind. Worth a structural analysis.

2. **What does `ʿaliyyā` modify — `makān` or Idrīs?** Arabic accusatives can attach as adverbial-of-state. The phrase could read `makānan ʿaliyyā` ("a place [that is] lofty") or "[as] lofty in place" (a state-adverbial). All four translators take the former. The latter would shift the locus of `ʿaliyyā` from the place to Idrīs — "We raised him, [he] being lofty as to place." A small grammatical move with a slightly different theological texture. Worth checking against classical grammarians.

3. **Why does the Quran's `tawaffā` of Jesus echo 39:42's sleep more than 32:11's death?** 32:11 has the angel of death as agent and `thumma ilā rabbikum turjaʿūn` as sequel (return for resurrection). 39:42 has the partial-tawaffā of sleep (release-back) and the full-tawaffā of death (withhold). 3:55 has *no agent named other than God* (no angel of death) and *no resurrection-pause sequel* (rafʿ is immediate). This is structurally closer to the 39:42 sleep-pattern than to the 32:11 death-pattern. The classical reading (a) — sleep-then-ascent — is the structurally cleanest fit. Worth tracing the shared formulas.

4. **Why does Pickthall harmonize `rafaʿahu` to "took him up" at 4:158?** This is the single most aggressive rfE-rendering in his entire translation. He uses "raise" elsewhere; only on 4:158 does he switch to "took up" — importing the standard wfy-rendering into a verse that has no wfy. Hypothesis: Pickthall is attempting to fuse 3:55 and 4:158 lexically because he reads them as the same event from different angles. If so, he is making an interpretive decision that the other three translators decline to make. Worth a Pickthall-specific divergence study.

5. **Where else does `bal` correct a death-claim with a raising-claim?** The structure `mā qatalūhu ... bal rafaʿahu` (4:157-158) is rare. Other `bal`-corrections in the Quran tend to be much shorter and within-verse. A search for the pattern "*verb-of-death-or-killing* + `bal` + *verb-of-raising*" might surface other cases — or confirm that 4:157-158 is unique. The structural-uniqueness would strengthen the polemical-reading.

6. **Does `shubbiha lahum` have other Quranic uses?** The verb `shubbiha` (Form II passive perfect) at 4:157 is rare. The root sh-b-h has other forms (`mutashābih` for ambiguous verses, 3:7; `tashābahat qulūbuhum` for hearts that resemble each other, 2:118). But the specific form `shubbiha` with a suppressed subject and `lahum` recipient — searching the morphology corpus for this pattern would identify whether this is a hapax or a recurring pattern. If hapax, it strengthens the verse's structural uniqueness.

7. **What is the "lofty place" in pre-Islamic and contemporaneous traditions?** Enoch in 1 Enoch (the Book of the Watchers) tours the heavens and is shown the "places of the holy ones" — a cosmography of heavenly locations. The Slavonic 2 Enoch elaborates "ten heavens" through which Enoch ascends. The Christian fourth-heaven tradition appears in 2 Corinthians 12:2 (Paul's "third heaven"). The Quran says only `makānan ʿaliyyā` — naming nothing. The deliberate sparseness against a culturally available cosmography is itself a finding. Worth a comparative-religions cross-reference.
