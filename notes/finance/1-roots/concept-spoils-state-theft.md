# Spoils, State Revenue & Theft — Root-Cluster Analysis

## Headline Finding (100 words)

The Quran uses three distinct spoils vocabularies — anfāl (unearned surplus), ghanīma (earned in battle), fayʾ (restored without fighting) — each with a different ownership rationale. The khums verse (8:41) specifies a five-recipient distribution that partly overlaps with the zakat list (9:60) but diverges crucially: khums gives to Prophet's kin; zakat does not. Jizya gets exactly one verse (9:29), barely a legal foundation. Theft gets two treatment tracks: a deterrence law (5:38–39) and a narrative laboratory in the Joseph cycle where the accusation is false and guilt is deflected through ancestral smear. State finance is principle-only: no rate tables, no enforcement apparatus.

## The Three Spoils Vocabularies

### 1. Anfāl — Surah 8:1

> يَسۡـَٔلُونَكَ عَنِ ٱلۡأَنفَالِۖ قُلِ ٱلۡأَنفَالُ لِلَّهِ وَٱلرَّسُولِۖ فَٱتَّقُواْ ٱللَّهَ وَأَصۡلِحُواْ ذَاتَ بَيۡنِكُمۡ
>
> Saheeh: "They ask you about the bounties [of war]. Say, 'The [decision concerning] bounties is for Allah and the Messenger.' So fear Allah and amend that which is between you."
> Pickthall: "The spoils of war belong to Allah and the messenger."
> Khattab: "Their distribution is decided by Allah and His Messenger."
> Arberry: "The spoils belong to God and the Messenger."

Anfāl derives from the root idea of "surplus" or "superfluous gift" — what accrues beyond the necessary. The question the community poses ("they ask you about al-anfāl") suggests the spoils issue was contentious immediately after Badr [8:1 is the surah's opening]. The answer is radical: ownership is not distributed, it is referred upward. "The anfāl belong to Allah and the Messenger" — no further distribution formula follows in this verse; the response is a sovereignty statement, not an allocation table.

The second nfl lemma — nāfila (17:79, 21:72) — means "an additional gift above what is owed." This sematic range (surplus, bonus, supererogatory) illuminates anfāl: it is wealth that the community did not strictly earn through its own labour. It falls outside normal exchange.

### 2. Ghanīma — 8:41

> ۞وَٱعۡلَمُوٓاْ أَنَّمَا غَنِمۡتُم مِّن شَيۡءࣲ فَأَنَّ لِلَّهِ خُمُسَهُۥ وَلِلرَّسُولِ وَلِذِي ٱلۡقُرۡبَىٰ وَٱلۡيَتَٰمَىٰ وَٱلۡمَسَٰكِينِ وَٱبۡنِ ٱلسَّبِيلِ
>
> Saheeh: "And know that anything you obtain of war booty — then indeed, for Allah is one fifth of it and for the Messenger and for [his] near relatives and the orphans, the needy, and the [stranded] traveler."
> Khattab: "Know that whatever spoils you take, one-fifth is for Allah and the Messenger, his close relatives, orphans, the poor, and needy travellers."

Ghanīma is the transactional term for combat-acquired wealth: you exerted effort (horse, camel, sword), and the verb ganima ("to gain as spoils") is active, second-person. Unlike anfāl, the distribution formula is explicit: one-fifth (khums) goes to five designated parties; the implied four-fifths flow to the fighters who earned it. The verb in 8:41 is "know" (i'lamū) — an epistemological command. This is a legal decree, not a theological reflection.

The khums recipients: Allah / Messenger / relatives of the Prophet (dhū al-qurbā) / orphans (yatāmā) / the needy (masākīn) / the wayfarer (ibn al-sabīl).

### 3. Fayʾ — 59:7

> مَّآ أَفَآءَ ٱللَّهُ عَلَىٰ رَسُولِهِۦ مِنۡ أَهۡلِ ٱلۡقُرَىٰ فَلِلَّهِ وَلِلرَّسُولِ وَلِذِي ٱلۡقُرۡبَىٰ وَٱلۡيَتَٰمَىٰ وَٱلۡمَسَٰكِينِ وَٱبۡنِ ٱلسَّبِيلِ كَيۡ لَا يَكُونَ دُولَةَۢ بَيۡنَ ٱلۡأَغۡنِيَآءِ
>
> Saheeh: "And what Allah restored to His Messenger from the people of the towns — it is for Allah and for the Messenger and for [his] near relatives and orphans and the [stranded] traveler — so that it will not be a perpetual distribution among the rich."
> Arberry: "Whatsoever spoils of war God has given to His Messenger from the people of the cities belongs to God, and His Messenger, and the near kinsman, orphans, the needy and the traveller, so that it be not a thing taken in turns among the rich of you."

The decisive distinguishing clause for fayʾ is 59:6: "you did not spur for it any horses or camels" — no fighting occurred. Fayʾ is wealth that "returns" (the root means to come back, revert) to the Muslim community without military effort; the paradigm case is the Banu Nadir's property in Medina, ceded when they departed. The allocation in 59:7 covers the entire one-fifth (100%) to the same five parties as khums; there is no four-fifths remainder for fighters because fighters played no role.

The added rationale in 59:7 is unique: the distribution is motivated explicitly so that wealth does not become "a dawla (rotation) among the rich alone." This is a data-grounded principle of circulation: state revenue must prevent wealth concentration, not enable it.

## The Khums vs. Zakat Comparison

**Khums recipients (8:41):** Allah/Messenger — dhū al-qurbā (Prophet's kin) — yatāmā (orphans) — masākīn (needy) — ibn al-sabīl (wayfarer) [5 categories]

**Zakat recipients (9:60):** fuqarāʾ (poor) — masākīn (needy) — ʿāmilīn ʿalayhā (administrators) — muʾallafa qulūbuhum (new converts / hearts to be won) — fī al-riqāb (freeing captives) — ghārimīn (indebted) — fī sabīl Allāh (the cause of Allah) — ibn al-sabīl (wayfarer) [8 categories]

Shared: masākīn, ibn al-sabīl (2 of 5 khums categories).
Khums-only: Allah/Messenger (as direct principals), Prophet's kin (explicitly family-based). No category for "freeing captives," no category for administrators, no "new converts."
Zakat-only: fuqarāʾ (a separate poverty category from masākīn), ʿāmilīn (collection staff), muʾallafa qulūbuhum (political bridge-building), riqāb (manumission), ghārimīn (debt relief), fī sabīl Allāh (military/religious work).

Interpretive observation: Zakat is a recurring civilian welfare system; khums/fayʾ is a one-time war-revenue allocation. The differences reflect the different contexts: khums rewards military participants (four-fifths for them) and directs state revenue toward kin-and-vulnerable; zakat distributes ongoing taxation toward systemic poverty, debt, and institutional maintenance. Both lists include the wayfarer — the transient, unattached poor — as a consistent priority.

## Jizya — 9:29 Only

> قَٰتِلُواْ ٱلَّذِينَ لَا يُؤۡمِنُونَ بِٱللَّهِ… حَتَّىٰ يُعۡطُواْ ٱلۡجِزۡيَةَ عَن يَدࣲ وَهُمۡ صَٰغِرُونَ
>
> Saheeh: "[Fight] until they give the jizyah willingly while they are humbled."
> Pickthall: "until they pay the tribute readily, being brought low."
> Khattab: "until they pay the tax, willingly submitting, fully humbled."

The Arabic of the key phrase: *ʿan yadin* — literally "from a hand," conventionally rendered "willingly" or "out of hand." Classical commentators disagree sharply: does *ʿan yad* mean "from their own hand" (directly, not by proxy), "from a position of strength/plenty," or "willingly/submissively"? Saheeh interpolates "willingly"; Arberry reads "out of hand." Translation divergence here is extremely high — which signals genuine semantic ambiguity in the source.

*Wa-hum ṣāghirūn* — "while they are subdued/humbled" — is unambiguous in its root (ṣ-gh-r = small, diminished). The state required is visible social subordination, not just payment.

What 9:29 does NOT specify: the amount of jizya, the assessment criteria (wealth, household, or per capita), any exemptions, any collection mechanism, any consequence for non-payment beyond the war permission that opened the verse. The entire Quranic foundation for a recurring tax on non-Muslim residents of the Islamic polity is a single prepositional phrase — *ḥattā yuʿṭū al-jizyata ʿan yadin*. Everything else that historical jizya law became was fiqh elaboration on these four words.

## Theft — 5:38 and the Joseph Countercase

### The Legal Verse — 5:38

> وَٱلسَّارِقُ وَٱلسَّارِقَةُ فَٱقۡطَعُوٓاْ أَيۡدِيَهُمَا جَزَآءَۢ بِمَا كَسَبَا نَكَٰلࣰ ا مِّنَ ٱللَّهِ
>
> Saheeh: "[As for] the thief, the male and the female, amputate their hands in recompense for what they committed as a deterrent punishment from Allah."
> Arberry: "cut off the hands of both, as a recompense for what they have earned, and a punishment exemplary from God."

The verse structure: (crime classification) → (mandatory response) → (framing 1: *jazāʾ bimā kasabā*) → (framing 2: *nakālan min Allāh*). Two framings operate simultaneously: *jazāʾ bimā kasabā* ("recompense for what they earned") deploys the exact vocabulary of eschatological judgment — the same root-pair (jzy + ksb) used throughout the Quran for divine recompense on the Day of Judgment. Criminal punishment is linguistically rendered as an earthly instantiation of divine accounting. *Nakālan min Allāh* ("an exemplary punishment from Allah") is the deterrence frame — it is not merely about this thief, it is about preventing future theft.

5:39 immediately follows:

*"But whoever repents after his wrongdoing and reforms, indeed, Allah will turn to him in forgiveness. Indeed, Allah is Forgiving and Merciful."*

The sequence: crime → mandatory punishment → repentance opens. The punishment is not the end of the story; restoration is available. Notably, 5:39 does not cancel the punishment (it does not say "if they repent, do not cut their hands"). Repentance operates at a different register — it governs the divine accounting, not the earthly legal outcome.

### The Joseph Cycle — 12:70-81

The conceptual structure here is the inverse of 5:38. In 12:70, Yusuf himself places the king's cup in Benjamin's bag and then has a herald accuse the caravan of theft [12:70]. The brothers protest in 12:73: "By Allah, you know well that we did not come to spread mischief, nor are we thieves (*sāriqīn*)." In 12:77, they deflect further: "If he has stolen, a brother of his stole before" — a reference to Joseph's alleged theft in his own childhood (a story not elaborated in the Quran but apparently known to the audience).

The structural features:
- The accusation is staged by Yusuf, not based on observed crime.
- The brothers respond with a character claim ("we are not thieves"), not with evidence about the specific event.
- Their counter-move is ancestral guilt attribution: the accused's character is read through lineage.
- Yusuf inwardly rejects this ("You are in a worse case") but does not reveal the setup.

This narrative raises the question the prompt poses: does the Quran frame theft as an ACT or a PERSON? The legal verse (5:38) treats it as an act — the grammatical subject is "the thief, male and female" (dual active participle), not "the person who has a history of theft." But the Joseph brothers' deflection constructs theft as a familial trait: "a brother of his stole before." The narrative shows this logic as the brothers' bad-faith reasoning, used to distance themselves from shame. The Quran does not validate it; Yusuf's inner response condemns them for it.

The fourth occurrence of srq outside legal/narrative contexts: 15:18 — "except one who eavesdrops (istāraqa al-samʿ)" — and 60:12, theft listed among the pledges women take at the bay'a (pledge of allegiance). These extend theft-vocabulary beyond property crime: eavesdropping is "stealing hearing." The semantic field includes unauthorized appropriation in a general sense.

## Three-Layer Spoils Logic (Interpretive)

The terminological distinction encodes an ownership theory:

| Term | Who earns it | Whose it is initially | Distribution rationale |
|------|-------------|----------------------|----------------------|
| Anfāl | Undefined (contested after Badr) | Allah and Messenger | Sovereignty — not distributed by formula |
| Ghanīma | Fighters (explicit military effort) | Fighters (4/5) + 5 state parties (1/5) | Earned share + community tithe |
| Fayʾ | No one (no fighting) | Entirely state/community | Circulation — prevent rich monopoly |

The deeper logic: the less human effort, the more completely the wealth belongs to the divine/state allocation. Full fighting → four-fifths to fighters. No fighting → zero to fighters, all to state parties. The one-fifth (khums) is the constant across both: a standing 20% state levy on war-acquired wealth, regardless of how it was acquired.

## Negative Space — What Is Missing

A comprehensive state finance system would require: tax rates and thresholds; assessment mechanisms (census, wealth survey); collection bureaucracy; penalty for non-compliance; appeals processes; budget categories and expenditure rules; deficit and debt frameworks; monetary policy.

The Quran provides: two distribution lists (khums, fayʾ/zakat), one tax category without a rate (jizya), one criminal penalty (theft), and a distributional principle (wealth should not circulate only among the rich). This is a framework of principles and priorities, not an administrative manual. Every institution of Islamic state finance that actually functioned historically — the bayt al-māl (treasury), the dīwān (tax registers), the kharāj (land tax), the full jizya rate schedule — was elaborated by jurists and administrators working from these minimal Quranic anchors.

The absence of any governance apparatus is consistent across the entire Finance investigation: the Quran specifies ends (orphans fed, wayfarers helped, wealth distributed) and occasionally means (cut the thief's hand, give one-fifth to these parties), but never mechanisms.

## Rabbit Holes for Synthesis

1. **Khums kin-priority vs. zakat kin-exclusion.** Zakat explicitly prohibits giving to Prophet's family (in Islamic jurisprudence, based on hadith, not the Quran). Khums gives them an explicit share. If both streams fund the state, the two systems pull in opposite directions on the question of prophetic-family entitlement.

2. **"Dawla" as economic principle.** The word *dawlatan* in 59:7 (circulation / rotation) is the same root as later Arabic for "state" (dawla). The verse's principle — wealth must not circulate only among the rich — may be the closest the Quran comes to an articulated distributive justice theory.

3. **Theft + bay'a in 60:12.** Theft appears in a list alongside shirk and infanticide as pledges women make on entering the community. This places theft in a community-covenant frame, not merely a criminal-penalty frame. Is it property crime, or breach of social covenant?

4. **The Joseph narrative and legal epistemology.** 12:70–73 raises the question of how one knows a theft occurred. The cup is planted; the herald calls out the accusation without investigation; the brothers protest their character, not the evidence. This is a narrative about the difference between appearance, character, and truth in legal proceedings — arguably more sophisticated than the flat legal command of 5:38.
