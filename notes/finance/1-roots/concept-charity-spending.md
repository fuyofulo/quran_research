# Charity & Spending — Root Cluster Analysis
## roots: Sdq (ص د ق) · zkw (ز ك و) · nfq (ن ف ق) · Hbb (ح ب ب) · bxl (ب خ ل) · sAl (س أ ل)

---

## Headline Finding (100 words)

The Quran constructs a complete moral economy of wealth through six interlocking roots. Zakat (zkw, 32 noun occurrences) and ṣalāh co-occur in exactly 28 of 56 zakat ayahs (50%), asserting that worship and redistribution are a single indivisible act. Infāq (nfq spending-sense, 68 occurrences) far outnumbers the munāfiqūn sense (37), yet the two share the same root and the same surah clusters — hypocrisy is defined in part as spending-without-sincerity (9:54). The cure for hubb al-māl (love of wealth, 89:20; 100:8) is not renunciation but spending from what you love (3:92). The sāʾil's claim is ḥaqq — a right, not a gift (51:19). Zakat rate: deliberately absent from the text.

## 3. The Ṣalāh–Zakat Formula

Data-grounded: of 56 ayahs containing zkw, 28 also contain the root Slw (ṣalāh = prayer), a co-occurrence share of exactly 50.0%. This is the highest structural co-occurrence among zkw roots, tied only with ātā (to give, 29 ayahs — which is mechanically expected since "to give" zakat is the standard construction).

The formula "aqīmū al-ṣalāh wa-ātū al-zakāh" appears explicitly at [2:43], [2:83], [2:110], [2:177], [2:277], [5:12], [5:55], [9:5], [9:11], [9:18], [9:71], [22:41], [24:56], [98:5], and many more. The pairing is not accidental emphasis — it appears in Meccan and Medinan contexts alike (Medinan: 34 ayahs; Meccan: 25 ayahs, including early surahs such as 19:31, 19:55, 21:73, 23:4, 41:7).

Interpretive: the formula asserts a structural equivalence between vertical worship (ṣalāh) and horizontal redistribution (zakāh). Neither completes the believer's obligation without the other. This is not merely liturgical shorthand — 9:67 makes the negative case explicit: the hypocrites "close their hands" (yaqbiḍūna aydiyahum) as a defining attribute, bracketed between "enjoin evil" and "forget Allah."

## 4. Ṣadaqa vs. Zakat — Are They Synonymous?

The lemma data resolves a common confusion. Zakāh (zakawʿp) is an institution-noun: it names the obligatory levy, specified by its recipients (9:60 uses ṣadaqāt for what is functionally the zakat pool) and coupled to ṣalāh throughout. Ṣadaqa(t) is a broader term for voluntary charitable giving. The two have different morphological homes within the Quran:

- zakawʿp is always substantive (32N, 0V) — it IS the institution
- Sadaqat occurs sometimes as obligatory alms (9:58–60, 9:79, 9:103–104) and sometimes as general giving (2:264, 2:271, 2:276)

**9:60 is the decisive case.** The verse begins: "The ṣadaqāt are only for [eight categories]…" (إِنَّمَا الصَّدَقَاتُ) — using ṣadaqāt to describe what Islamic jurisprudence later codified as the zakat distribution list. This is not a contradiction. It shows that ṣadaqa is the broader semantic umbrella; zakāh is a formalized, institutionalized subset. Ṣadaqa can refer to zakāh; zakāh cannot refer to optional ṣadaqa.

## 5. nfq Polysemy: Is Hypocrisy a Form of False Spending?

The geographic distribution of infāq and munāfiqūn within nfq's 86 ayahs:

**Surah 2:** 19 infāq occurrences, 1 munāfiq occurrence — almost entirely economic
**Surah 9:** 8 infāq, 11 munāfiq — both senses dense in the same surah
**Surah 63 (al-Munāfiqūn):** 2 infāq, 4 munāfiq — the surah named for hypocrisy contains commands to spend

The decisive textual link is [9:54]:

> وَمَا مَنَعَهُمۡ أَن تُقۡبَلَ مِنۡهُمۡ نَفَقَٰتُهُمۡ إِلَّآ أَنَّهُمۡ كَفَرُواْ بِٱللَّهِ وَبِرَسُولِهِۦ وَلَا يَأۡتُونَ ٱلصَّلَوٰةَ إِلَّا وَهُمۡ كُسَالَىٰ وَلَا يُنفِقُونَ إِلَّا وَهُمۡ كَٰرِهُونَ
>
> Saheeh: "And what prevents their expenditures from being accepted from them but that they have disbelieved in Allah and in His Messenger and that they come not to prayer except while they are lazy and that they do not spend except while they are unwilling."
>
> Khattab: "What prevented their donations from being accepted is that they have lost faith in Allah and His Messenger, they never come to prayer except half-heartedly, and they never donate except resentfully."

The word nafaqātihim (their expenditures) — the very noun-form of infāq — is the subject: the munāfiqūn DO spend, but they spend reluctantly (kārahūn). Their expenditure is real but their sincerity is absent. The Quran thus frames nifāq not as total non-spending but as structurally corrupted spending — giving without the inner orientation that makes giving valid.

[63:7] makes this explicit from the other direction: the munāfiqūn's defining strategy is "do not spend (lā tunfiqū) on those with the Messenger until they scatter." Hypocrisy weaponizes withholding. And [63:10] immediately pivots to command genuine spending before death makes the opportunity moot.

Interpretive conclusion: the shared root is semantically load-bearing. Nifāq and infāq are conceptually twinned because the Quran frames authentic giving as the antithesis of hypocrisy. A munāfiq is, in part, someone whose spending is performative or coerced rather than volitional. The polysemy is not etymological accident.

## 6. The Hubb al-Māl Motif — Love of Wealth

The Hbb root (95 occ, 9 lemmas) is primarily about divine love-and-being-loved ([3:31], [2:195]), but four passages address love of worldly wealth as the diagnostic spiritual problem:

| Ayah | Key phrase | Saheeh |
|------|------------|--------|
| 3:14 | حُبُّ الشَّهَوَات | "Beautified for people is the love of that which they desire — of women and sons, heaped-up sums of gold and silver…" |
| 89:20 | تُحِبُّونَ الْمَالَ حُبًّا جَمًّا | "And you love wealth with immense love." |
| 100:8 | لِحُبِّ الْخَيۡرِ لَشَدِيدٌ | "And indeed he is, in love of wealth, intense." |
| 76:8 | عَلَى حُبِّهِ | "They give food in spite of love for it to the needy…" |

Note the spectrum: 3:14 names the love as real and beautified (zuyyina — passive, external seduction); 89:20 diagnoses it directly as excessive; 100:8 declares the human condition "intense" in this love; but 76:8 inverts the motif — the righteous give DESPITE their love (ʿalā ḥubbihī = "upon/in spite of their love for it").

The cure is NOT renunciation. The Quran nowhere commands the believer to stop loving wealth. The cure is [3:92]:

> لَن تَنَالُواْ ٱلۡبِرَّ حَتَّىٰ تُنفِقُواْ مِمَّا تُحِبُّونَۚ وَمَا تُنفِقُواْ مِن شَيۡءٍ فَإِنَّ ٱللَّهَ بِهِۦ عَلِيمٌ
>
> Saheeh: "Never will you attain the good [reward] until you spend [in the way of Allah] from that which you love. And whatever you spend — indeed, Allah is Knowing of it."
>
> Arberry: "You will not attain piety until you expend of what you love."

This verse explicitly requires spending mimmā tuḥibbūn — "from what you love." Love of wealth is not the problem; hoarding it is. The mechanism is to redirect the beloved object outward, not to extinguish the love. This distinguishes the Quranic cure from ascetic traditions that prescribe detachment.

[2:177] reinforces: giving wealth ʿalā ḥubbihī (in spite of / through the love of it) to the eight categories of recipients is listed as the first act of birr (righteousness) — before prayer and zakat in the verse's sequence.

## 7. Bukhl — Miserliness as Cosmic Self-Harm

Twelve occurrences, 7 ayahs, 6 surahs. All Medinan except [92:8] (Meccan). Two lemmas: the verb baxila (10 occ) and the noun bukhl (2 occ, both in the phrase "they command people to bukhl"). The pattern is sharp:

**Verse catalogue:**
- [3:180] — the collar verse: wealth withheld becomes a collar around the neck on the Day of Judgment
- [4:37] — the trio: they withhold, command others to withhold, and conceal what Allah gave them
- [9:76] — narrative: after Allah gave them from His bounty, they became stingy and turned away
- [47:37] — hypothetical: if Allah demanded all wealth, you would withhold and expose your resentment (ḍaghanakum)
- [47:38] — the self-harm argument: "whoever withholds, withholds only from his own soul" — God is Rich (al-Ghanī), you are the poor ones
- [57:24] — mirror of 4:37: same formula, adds that Allah is "the Rich, the Praiseworthy"
- [92:8] — Meccan: the paired alternatives — one who gives and fears Allah → easy path; one who withholds and deems himself self-sufficient (istaghna) → difficult path

Three structural arguments against bukhl emerge from the data:

1. **Eschatological**: what is withheld will be worn as a collar on the Day of Resurrection [3:180]. Material attachment materializes as punishment.

2. **Ontological self-harm**: "whoever withholds, withholds from himself" [47:38]. God is self-sufficient; the miser's hoarding benefits no one, least of all himself. Allah's need for human giving is zero.

3. **Social contagion**: bukhl is not merely individual — [4:37] and [57:24] both add "and they command people to bukhl." Miserliness is a teaching, a social norm that the miser propagates. Its condemnation is therefore communal, not only personal.

Co-occurrence with ghanī (rich/self-sufficient) at 3/7 ayahs (43%) makes the theological argument explicit: God is al-Ghanī; the miser who imagines he is wealthy enough to withhold is claiming a self-sufficiency that belongs to God alone.

## 8. The Sāʾil's Ḥaqq — Charity as Right, Not Gift

The noun sāʾil (petitioner/beggar) occurs only 7 times in the Quran. The finance-relevant occurrences:

| Ayah | Text | Key word |
|------|------|----------|
| 51:19 | "In their wealth was a right (ḥaqq) for the petitioner and the deprived" | ḥaqq — right/due |
| 70:24–25 | "…in their wealth a known right — for the petitioner and the deprived" | ḥaqq maʿlūm — known right |
| 93:10 | "And as for the petitioner, do not repel him" | prohibition on dismissal |
| 2:177 | gives to "those who ask" among the eight categories | sāʾilīn in birr list |

The critical feature of [51:19] and [70:24–25] is the word ḥaqq. The sāʾil does not receive charity as an act of the giver's generosity — the wealth already contains a right belonging to the asker. The giver is discharging an obligation, not performing a voluntary kindness. This is the Quran's most radical framing of redistribution: the wealth of the comfortable already includes a pre-existing claim by the destitute. Infāq is the act of returning what is owed.

The pairing sāʾil wa-maḥrūm (petitioner and deprived) is a fixed formula at both [51:19] and [70:25]. The maḥrūm (deprived/one who is denied) is typically glossed as the one who cannot or does not ask — the structurally excluded poor, not merely the one who begs. Together, the formula covers the full recipient spectrum: active petitioner and passive excluded.

[93:10]'s prohibition — "do not repel the petitioner" (lā tanhar) — is framed as symmetrically obligatory with [93:9]: "do not oppress the orphan." Both are duties grounded in the Prophet's own experience of orphanhood and destitution, making the sāʾil's right biographical as well as juridical.

## 9. Negative Space: The Missing Rate

Data-grounded observation: the word zakawʿp (institutionalized zakat) appears 32 times in the Quran. The Quran specifies the eight categories of recipients in precise detail [9:60]. It specifies the obligation's pairing with ṣalāh across 28 ayahs. It specifies purification as the theological ground (zakat comes from zakā = to be pure/to grow). What it does not specify anywhere is the rate — 2.5% on wealth above the niṣāb (minimum threshold), the number universally practiced, comes entirely from hadith corpus.

This is structurally consistent with the Quran's pattern in commercial law: it commands just measure and fair weights [83:1–3] without specifying units; it prohibits ribā without defining the threshold; it mandates ṣalāh without specifying four rakaʿāt for ẓuhr. The Quran establishes categories, principles, and moral architecture. The specification of quantity is consistently delegated to prophetic practice and juristic derivation.

For zakat specifically, the absence of a rate is not an oversight — it enables the institution to scale: the moral obligation is absolute, the magnitude is calibrated by time, economy, and circumstances. Fixing a rate in the text would have frozen the institution.

## 10. Synthesis Vectors for Phase 2

1. **The ṣalāh–zakāh dyad** warrants cross-examination with the PRAYER root cluster: how identical is the pairing formula? Is zakāh always second, ṣalāh always first? Does the order encode a hierarchy?

2. **Nifāq as corrupted infāq** links the Charity cluster to any investigation of sincerity and intention (niyya). Phase 2 should surface whether the nfq root's polysemy is the Quran's sharpest example of a root where the noun and verb forms are moral opposites.

3. **The 3:92 cure** (spend from what you love) sits in direct tension with any ascetic reading. If the WEALTH cluster found verses praising renunciation, this needs reconciliation.

4. **Ḥaqq-framing of charity** (51:19, 70:24–25) connects to the debt/contracts cluster. The claim of the poor is already pre-written into wealth — this is a property-rights statement, not merely an ethical recommendation.

5. **Bukhl's social contagion** (4:37, 57:24) — the miser as active propagandist for stinginess — connects to the HYPOCRISY cluster. Both the munāfiq and the bakhīl are defined partly by what they teach others, not only what they do themselves.
