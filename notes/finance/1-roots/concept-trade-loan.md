# Concept: Trade & Loan — tjr / byE / $ry / qrD

## Headline Finding

The four roots divide neatly into two planes: **literal commerce** (tjr, byE) and **spiritual transaction** (also tjr, byE, $ry, qrD). The decisive structural fact is that the Quran migrates its own commercial vocabulary upward: tijāra appears metaphorically in 5 of 8 ayahs where it occurs; $ry (shtarā) is grammatically ambidextrous — the same verb form serves both "buy" and "sell," allowing 9:111 to make Allah the buyer of souls while 2:90 shows humans "selling" themselves badly. qarḍ ḥasan is the most formally frozen formula in this cluster: 6/6 occurrences are identical in structure, all addressed to Allah as recipient, all promising multiplication — and it is distinct from charity (Sdq, nfq, zky) precisely because it names obligation on Allah's side, not merely generosity on the human side.

## Q1: tijāra — Literal vs. Metaphorical

Of the 9 occurrences:

**Literal commerce:** [2:282] (spot-cash transaction exempted from written contract), [9:24] (trade you fear will decline, listed alongside family/property as worldly distractions), [62:11] (the caravan that pulled people away from Friday prayer — twice in same verse).

**Metaphorical / God-exchange:** [2:16] (hypocrites' "commerce" that didn't profit — trading guidance for error), [4:29] ("consume not your wealth unlawfully, unless it be trade by mutual consent" — literal, but the verse is immediately about licit vs. illicit), [35:29] ("those who recite Allah's book hope for a commerce that will never perish"), [61:10] ("shall I guide you to a commerce that saves you from painful punishment?").

Count: **4–5 literal / 4–5 metaphorical** — essentially even split, but the metaphorical uses are the theologically load-bearing ones. The Meccan occurrence [35:29] is the only Meccan instance, and it is pure metaphor. The metaphorical uses frame tijāra as the ultimate investment frame: eternal returns vs. no-profit. This is data-grounded.

## Q2: bayʿ and the ribā Contrast (2:275)

The verse is structured as a quotation rebuttal:

**Panel 1 — 2:275:**
> إِنَّمَا ٱلْبَيْعُ مِثْلُ ٱلرِّبَوٰا۟ ۗ وَأَحَلَّ ٱللَّهُ ٱلْبَيْعَ وَحَرَّمَ ٱلرِّبَوٰا۟
> "They say: bayʿ is just like ribā — but Allah has permitted bayʿ and forbidden ribā."

Saheeh: "Trade is [just] like interest. But Allah has permitted trade and has forbidden interest."

The verse quotes the *opponents' claim* first (al-bayʿ mithl al-ribā) then overturns it. The rhetorical move is significant: the Quran does not refute their claim with an economic argument. It does not explain *why* bayʿ differs — it asserts the distinction as divine decree (aḥalla / ḥarrama). The interpretive layer (why they differ) must be reconstructed from surrounding verses and the broader cluster:

- [4:29] makes the operative criterion explicit: **tarāḍin minkum** ("mutual consent among you") — bayʿ is licit when both parties consent; the issue with ribā is that one party is structurally constrained (the borrower in need cannot freely consent to exponential cost).
- [2:254] and [14:31] frame bayʿ eschatologically: there will be "no bayʿ" on the Day of Judgment — meaning that the very human-transaction fabric dissolves at resurrection.
- The verb form in 2:275 uses **aḥalla** (Form IV permitting) for bayʿ and **ḥarrama** (Form II forbidding) for ribā — syntactically parallel, divine agency foregrounded on both sides.

**Interpretive conclusion** (marked as such): The Quran's argument is by assertion, not by economic principle. The distinguishing marker drawn from [4:29] is mutual consent (tarāḍ) — a consent-based ethics of exchange, not a risk-sharing or real-asset theory. This aligns with the broader qrD structure: qarḍ ḥasan is offered *freely*, which is why it can be "returned" with multiplication.

## Q3: $ry / shirāʾ Polysemy — The Ambidextrous Verb

**Panel 2 — 2:102 (both lemmas in same verse):**
> وَلَقَدْ عَلِمُوا۟ لَمَنِ ٱشْتَرَىٰهُ مَا لَهُۥ فِى ٱلْءَاخِرَةِ مِنْ خَلَـٰقٍ وَلَبِئْسَ مَا شَرَوْا۟ بِهِۦٓ أَنفُسَهُمْ
> "Whoever *bought* [the magic — ishtarā, VIII] would have no share in the Hereafter; evil indeed was what they *sold* [their souls for — sharū, I]."

This is the decisive polysemy verse. In a single ayah: ishtarā (VIII, "purchased") and sharā (I, "sold") — same root, same referent group, opposite transaction directions. The QAC correctly glosses them separately (literal_gloss: "buys it" vs. "they sold"). This is not a translation artifact — the Arabic uses two grammatically distinct forms.

**Distribution of the two lemmas:**
- ishtarā (VIII) × 21: predominantly means "to purchase/take" — used 15+ times for humans acquiring bad things (misguidance, worldly life, error, magic)
- $ara (I) × 4: used for "selling" — 2:102 (sold souls), 2:207 (gives/sells his soul to Allah — positive), 4:74 (those who sell the life of this world for the hereafter), 12:20 (sold Joseph for a cheap price)

**Pattern:** VIII-form (ishtarā) clusters overwhelmingly on the human side — humans buy bad exchanges. The I-form ($ara) appears in the sacrificial/positive sense at 2:207 (the believer who "sells" his soul seeking Allah's pleasure) and 4:74 (fighters who sell the present life for the next). The asymmetry is data-grounded: Form VIII is the heavy-use form for moral failure; Form I is the rarer, often redemptive sense.

**Panel 3 — 9:111 (Allah as buyer, $ry + byE combined):**
> إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ ٱشْتَرَىٰ مِنَ ٱلْمُؤْمِنِينَ أَنفُسَهُمْ وَأَمْوَٰلَهُم بِأَنَّ لَهُمُ ٱلْجَنَّةَ ... فَٱسْتَبْشِرُوا۟ بِبَيْعِكُمُ ٱلَّذِى بَايَعْتُم بِهِۦ
> "Allah has *purchased* [ishtarā] from the believers their souls and wealth — in exchange for Paradise... Rejoice in your *sale* [bayʿ] which you have *pledged* [bāyaʿtum]."

All three commercial roots converge in one verse: $ry (purchasing act), byE (the transaction itself), baAyaEo (the pledge-act). The word بَيْعِكُمُ here also echoes the political baya'a (bay'at al-ridwan, 48:10,18) — the formal pledge of allegiance. Commerce vocabulary and covenant vocabulary merge completely.

## Q4: qarḍ ḥasan — Why "Loan" Not "Charity"?

**Panel 4 — 2:245:**
> مَّن ذَا ٱلَّذِى يُقْرِضُ ٱللَّهَ قَرْضًا حَسَنًا فَيُضَٰعِفَهُۥ لَهُۥٓ أَضْعَافًا كَثِيرَةً
> "Who is it that would *loan* Allah a goodly loan, so He may *multiply* it for him many times over?"

All 6 "loan to Allah" occurrences (2:245, 5:12, 57:11, 57:18, 64:17, 73:20) follow this structure: verb aqraḍa (lend) + noun qarḍan (a loan) + adjective ḥasanan (goodly) + promise of multiplication (yudāʿifu). The formula is locked.

**Why "loan" not "charity"?** The vocabulary of charity — Sdq (sadaqa), nfq (infāq), zky (zakāt) — is all present in the broader cluster. Crucially:

1. **Charity vocabulary does not create obligation.** Sadaqa, infāq describe giving without return-right. Qarḍ creates a receivable — the lender is *owed* return.
2. **The Quran names Allah as debtor.** "Lend to Allah" places Allah in the structural position of the borrower, who *owes* repayment. This is theologically provocative — it is not metaphorical charity but a covenantal obligation framed in commercial law terms.
3. **57:18 places qarḍ ḥasan alongside Sdq (sadaqa) as a separate category:** "Those who give sadaqa, men and women, and *lend to Allah a goodly loan* — it will be multiplied for them." The and-conjunction (wa) separates them, confirming they are distinct acts.
4. **The multiplication is the structural return on the loan.** Sadaqa's return is blessing (barakah) language; qarḍ's return is explicitly a multiplication factor — aDʿāfan kathīra ("many times over"), or "it will be doubled." This preserves the loan-repayment structure.
5. **The formula qrD appears in the 18:17 cave passage** with an entirely different sense: the sun "passes by" (taqriḍu) the sleepers — a lateral movement meaning. The QAC assigns this a distinct lemma, confirming the financial formula is a specialist usage.

**Interpretive conclusion:** The "loan to Allah" frame creates a relational rather than a purely gift economy. Giving as a loan asserts: *Allah will honor this claim*. The vocabulary of loan activates the language of contractual entitlement, distinct from gift/charity. It implies that the giver is not erasing the debt but deferring it — with divine guarantee of repayment.

## Q5: Co-occurrences — Commerce and Remembrance

The structural finding from the co-occurrence tables confirms the research question's intuition:

**byE co-roots by share:** Alh (Allah root, 82%), Amn (faith, 64%), *kr (dhikr/remembrance, 36%), Slw (prayer, 36%), ywm (day/judgment, 36%).

**tjr co-roots by share:** Alh (75%), Slw (prayer, 25%), *kr (dhikr, 25%), lhw (lahw/distraction, 25%).

The pairing of tijāra + lahw is clinched at [24:37] and [62:11]. At 24:37 the men of God are defined negatively — "neither commerce nor sale diverts them from dhikr-Allah, prayer, and zakāt." Commerce and dhikr are antonyms that must be held in tension. At 62:11 the community literally abandons Friday khutba for a passing caravan — commerce wins the attention battle. The Quran's response: "what is with Allah is better than diversion (lahw) AND than commerce (tijāra)."

**Panel 5 — 24:37:**
> رِجَالٌ لَّا تُلْهِيهِمْ تِجَٰرَةٌ وَلَا بَيْعٌ عَن ذِكْرِ ٱللَّهِ وَإِقَامِ ٱلصَّلَوٰةِ وَإِيتَآءِ ٱلزَّكَوٰةِ يَخَافُونَ يَوْمًا
> "Men whom neither trade (tijāra) nor sale (bayʿ) distracts from the remembrance of Allah, prayer, or zakāt — they fear a Day..."

This verse is structurally important: tijāra and bayʿ appear together as paired synonyms, suggesting tijāra is the broader term (commerce in general) and bayʿ the specific act (the sale transaction). The verse also confirms that commerce is not condemned — the men are praised for not being *distracted* by it, not for avoiding it.

## Q6: Translator Divergence — Notable Gaps

- **2:275 (bayʿ/ribā):** Translators agree on rendering — all four give "trade" + "usury/interest." Low divergence.
- **9:111 ($ry + byE + baAyaEo):** Saheeh: "transaction...contracted." Pickthall: "bargain." Khattab: "exchange." Arberry: "bargain." The word *bāyaʿtum* (Form III verb) reads as both commercial pledge and political allegiance — translators divide: Saheeh's "contracted" emphasizes commercial law; Pickthall/Arberry's "bargain" emphasizes negotiation; none renders the political baya'a overtone explicitly.
- **2:90 ($ry):** literal_gloss says "sold" but translators diverge — Arberry has "disbelieving... for which they sold themselves." This is the Form I ($ara) reading — "they sold." QAC's literal_gloss confirms the "sell" reading, but context says they bought disbelief; the polysemy creates translator uncertainty about who is buyer and who is seller.
- **18:17 (taqriḍu — sun "passing"):** Saheeh: "passing away from them." Pickthall: "go past them." Arberry: "passing them by." All agree. The non-financial qarḍ use is unambiguous.

## Negative Space

1. **No word for "profit motive" or "price negotiation."** The word for profit, rbH (rabiḥa), appears only in [2:16] ("their commerce did not profit") — once, negated, in a metaphorical context. There is no positive Quranic word for legitimate profit-seeking.
2. **No terms for contract law specifics in tijāra.** The detailed contract law is byE + dyn (debt) territory [2:282]. The tijāra register stays abstract.
3. **qarḍ ḥasan never appears negated or failed.** All 6 uses are positive invitations. The formula "who will lend to Allah?" uses interrogative form but the expected answer is clearly affirmative. There is no "bad loan to Allah" scenario.
4. **$ry never appears in a neutral transaction.** Every occurrence of shirāʾ/shtarā is morally valenced — either a bad exchange (buying error, misguidance, worldly life) or a redemptive one (selling one's soul to Allah, buying Paradise). There is no commercial $ry for ordinary marketplace transactions.

## Rabbit Holes for Synthesis

1. The convergence of $ry + byE + qrD all framing the believer-Allah relationship as a commercial transaction across multiple surah contexts — is there a unified "divine economy" concept?
2. The rbH (profit) root at [2:16] is negated and metaphorical. The bwr (perish/fail) root at [35:29] is also negated ("a commerce that will never perish"). Is the Quran systematically using commercial failure vocabulary to frame spiritual loss?
3. tarāḍ (mutual consent, [4:29]) as the operative ethical criterion for legitimate exchange — how does this interact with ribā? Is ribā's prohibition structurally about structural coercion (absent tarāḍ)?
