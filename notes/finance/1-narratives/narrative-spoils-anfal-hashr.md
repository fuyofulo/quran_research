# Narrative: The Spoils Chapters — al-Anfāl and al-Ḥashr

## Headline Finding (100 words)

The Quran's spoils theology is not primarily a revenue law — it is a moral test structured as a redistribution mandate. Al-Anfāl opens by stripping ownership from individual fighters entirely ("the anfāl belong to Allah and the Messenger"), then allocates a fixed one-fifth (khums) to five social categories at 8:41. Al-Ḥashr intensifies this: fayʾ wealth obtained without battle goes 100% to the same five categories, with the explicit rationale stated in one word — لَا يَكُونَ دُولَةً (lā yakūna dūlatan): "so that it does not become a circulation [among the wealthy]." This is the Quran's fullest statement of political economy: wealth must flow, not pool.

---

## 1. al-Anfāl 8:1 — Ownership Reassigned Before Distribution

The surah opens mid-dispute. The post-Badr soldiers are arguing over the spoils. The question they bring to the Prophet is settled in the first sentence:

> **يَسۡـَٔلُونَكَ عَنِ ٱلۡأَنفَالِۖ قُلِ ٱلۡأَنفَالُ لِلَّهِ وَٱلرَّسُولِۖ فَٱتَّقُواْ ٱللَّهَ وَأَصۡلِحُواْ ذَاتَ بَيۡنِكُمۡۖ وَأَطِيعُواْ ٱللَّهَ وَرَسُولَهُۥٓ إِن كُنتُم مُّؤۡمِنِينَ**

| Translator | Rendering |
|-----------|-----------|
| Saheeh International | "They ask you, [O Muhammad], about the bounties [of war]. Say, 'The [decision concerning] bounties is for Allah and the Messenger.' So fear Allah and amend that which is between you and obey Allah and His Messenger, if you should be believers." |
| Pickthall | "They ask thee (O Muhammad) of the spoils of war. Say: The spoils of war belong to Allah and the messenger, so keep your duty to Allah, and adjust the matter of your difference, and obey Allah and His messenger, if ye are (true) believers." |
| Khattab | "They ask you ˹O Prophet˺ regarding the spoils of war. Say, 'Their distribution is decided by Allah and His Messenger. So be mindful of Allah, settle your affairs, and obey Allah and His Messenger if you are ˹true˺ believers.'" |
| Arberry | "They will question thee concerning the spoils. Say: 'The spoils belong to God and the Messenger; so fear you God, and set things right between you, and obey you God and His Messenger, if you are believers.'" |

Notice the verse structure: the property question is answered in one clause, then immediately subordinated to three moral imperatives — fear Allah, reconcile, obey. The spoils are not the point; they are the occasion for the test. The conditional ending — "if you are believers" — frames the entire wealth-question as a faith question. Greed over spoils is not merely a legal problem; it is a sign of deficient īmān.

Note also what 8:1 does NOT do: it does not distribute. It asserts ownership. Distribution comes forty ayahs later at 8:41. The gap between ownership-claim and distribution-formula is itself significant — the Quran separates the question "who owns this?" from the question "how does it flow?"

## 2. al-Anfāl 8:41 — The Khums: One Fifth with Five+ Recipients

> **وَٱعۡلَمُوٓاْ أَنَّمَا غَنِمۡتُم مِّن شَيۡءࣲ فَأَنَّ لِلَّهِ خُمُسَهُۥ وَلِلرَّسُولِ وَلِذِي ٱلۡقُرۡبَىٰ وَٱلۡيَتَٰمَىٰ وَٱلۡمَسَٰكِينِ وَٱبۡنِ ٱلسَّبِيلِ إِن كُنتُمۡ ءَامَنتُم بِٱللَّهِ…**

The operative word is **خُمُسَهُ** (khumusuhu) — one fifth. This is the Quran's only explicit percentage allocation for state-derived revenue. The recipients of this fifth:

1. **لِلَّهِ** — Allah (theologically prior; practically channeled through the Messenger in classical jurisprudence)
2. **وَلِلرَّسُولِ** — the Messenger
3. **وَلِذِي ٱلۡقُرۡبَىٰ** — near relatives (of the Messenger; the Banū Hāshim and Banū Muṭṭalib in classical fiqh)
4. **وَٱلۡيَتَٰمَىٰ** — orphans
5. **وَٱلۡمَسَٰكِينِ** — the poor/needy
6. **وَٱبۡنِ ٱلسَّبِيلِ** — the stranded traveler/wayfarer

The remaining four-fifths goes unstated in the verse. Classical jurists derive it as the fighters' share by the logic of exclusion — if one-fifth is specifically allocated, the rest belongs to those who earned it. The Quran does not say this; it says only what must be diverted away from the fighters. The negative carve-out is the legal form, not a positive entitlement grant.

The verse closes — again — with a faith-conditional: "if you believe in Allah and what We sent down upon Our servant on the Day of Criterion, the day the two parties met [at Badr]." The allocation is tethered to the specific moment of revelation and to belief itself. Hoarding from the khums would be not just unjust but faithless.

## 3. The Fayʾ Distinction: al-Ḥashr 59:6-7

Al-Ḥashr 59:6 introduces a second category of conflict-derived wealth:

> (59:6): "And what Allah has given as fayʾ to His Messenger from them — for which you spurred neither horse nor camel; but Allah gives His messengers authority over whom He wills, and Allah is over all things competent."

**Ghanīma** (8:41) = spoils from active battle. Fighters expended effort. Fighters receive 4/5.  
**Fayʾ** (59:6-7) = property that reverted without battle (the Banū Naḍīr case: they abandoned their properties under siege before fighting broke out). Because no fighters expended effort, there is no fighters' share. The entire amount flows to the allocated parties.

The juristic distinction is clean and consequential: the distribution formula changes with the mode of acquisition. Effort creates entitlement; absence of effort removes it. This is a form of labor theory embedded in the allocation logic — not developed theoretically, but operative structurally.

## 4. al-Ḥashr 59:7 — The Centerpiece: لَا يَكُونَ دُولَةً

> **مَّآ أَفَآءَ ٱللَّهُ عَلَىٰ رَسُولِهِۦ مِنۡ أَهۡلِ ٱلۡقُرَىٰ فَلِلَّهِ وَلِلرَّسُولِ وَلِذِي ٱلۡقُرۡبَىٰ وَٱلۡيَتَٰمَىٰ وَٱلۡمَسَٰكِينِ وَٱبۡنِ ٱلسَّبِيلِ كَيۡ لَا يَكُونَ دُولَةَۢ بَيۡنَ ٱلۡأَغۡنِيَآءِ مِنكُمۡۚ وَمَآ ءَاتَىٰكُمُ ٱلرَّسُولُ فَخُذُوهُ وَمَا نَهَىٰكُمۡ عَنۡهُ فَٱنتَهُواْۚ وَٱتَّقُواْ ٱللَّهَۖ إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ شَدِيدُ ٱلۡعِقَابِ**

| Translator | Key phrase (كَيۡ لَا يَكُونَ دُولَةً) | Full rendering |
|-----------|--------------------------------------|----------------|
| Saheeh International | "so that it will not be a perpetual distribution among the rich from among you" | "…it is for Allah and for the Messenger and for [his] near relatives and orphans and the [stranded] traveler — so that it will not be a perpetual distribution among the rich from among you…" |
| Pickthall | "that it become not a commodity between the rich among you" | "…it is for Allah and His messenger and for the near of kin and the orphans and the needy and the wayfarer, that it become not a commodity between the rich among you…" |
| Khattab | "so that wealth may not merely circulate among your rich" | "…they are for Allah and the Messenger, his close relatives, orphans, the poor, and ˹needy˺ travellers so that wealth may not merely circulate among your rich…" |
| Arberry | "so that it be not a thing taken in turns among the rich of you" | "…belongs to God, and His Messenger, and the near kinsman, orphans, the needy and the traveller, so that it be not a thing taken in turns among the rich of you…" |

### The word دُولَة (dūla)

The root د-و-ل (d-w-l) carries the sense of rotation, alternation, passing from hand to hand. A دَوْلَة is a turn, a cycle, a circulation — the same root gives modern Arabic its word for "state" (dawla), but here the meaning is older: wealth that revolves among a closed circle, returning perpetually to the same hands.

The grammatical construction is purposive: **كَيۡ لَا يَكُونَ** — "in order that it NOT be." The distribution mandate is explicitly justified by its anti-concentration purpose. The Quran does not merely command distribution and leave the reader to infer why; it states the reason. This is the fullest explicit rationale for any economic rule in the Quran.

The critique is structural, not individual. The text does not say "so that the rich do not hoard." It says "so that it does not become a dūla among the wealthy" — the problem is the circuit itself, the self-reinforcing loop by which wealth in motion returns only to those who already hold wealth. Mandated distribution breaks the circuit.

## 5. al-Ḥashr 59:8-9 — The Recipients Elaborated

**59:8 — The Poor Emigrants (al-Fuqarāʾ al-Muhājirīn):**

"For the poor emigrants who were expelled from their homes and their properties, seeking bounty from Allah and [His] approval and supporting Allah and His Messenger — those are the truthful." [59:8]

These are people defined by what they lost: homes (دِيَٰرِهِمۡ) and property (أَمۡوَٰلِهِمۡ). Their claim on the fayʾ is moral — they sacrificed material position for faith. The verse closes with a verdict: **أُوْلَٰٓئِكَ هُمُ ٱلصَّٰدِقُونَ** — "those are the truthful ones." Receiving distribution is not charity; it is just recognition of real loss.

**59:9 — The Anṣār: Radical Altruism from Scarcity:**

"And those who settled in al-Madīna and [adopted] the faith before them — they love those who emigrated to them and find not any want in their breasts of what the emigrants were given, but give preference to them over themselves even though they are in privation." [59:9]

Three moral moves in this verse: (a) the Anṣār love the newcomers; (b) they feel no resentment at what the newcomers receive; (c) they give preference even in their own scarcity — the word **خَصَاصَة** means pressing need, want. The Quran here names as the highest economic virtue the willingness to prefer others' consumption over one's own when one's own supply is already insufficient. This is not abstract altruism; it is concrete material sacrifice. The verse marks such people: **وَمَن يُوقَ شُحَّ نَفۡسِهِۦ فَأُوْلَٰٓئِكَ هُمُ ٱلۡمُفۡلِحُونَ** — "whoever is protected from the stinginess of his soul — those are the successful."

---

## 6. al-Ḥashr 59:10 — Distribution Across Time

"And those who came after them, saying: Our Lord, forgive us and our brothers who preceded us in faith…" [59:10]

The recipients of the fayʾ distribution, having been defined by social location (emigrants: 59:8) and by relational generosity (Anṣār: 59:9), are here extended temporally. The third category is all who come afterward, unified by their prayer for those who came before. The community of beneficiaries is not fixed at the moment of the conquest; it extends forward into all subsequent generations of believers.

This temporal expansion is economically significant: the redistribution mandate is not a one-time settlement of a specific dispute but a standing instruction — the principle that wealth must not pool among the wealthy applies as long as the community exists.

## 7. The Three-Revenue Comparison

| Category | Source | Rate diverted | Recipients |
|----------|--------|--------------|-----------|
| **Ghanīma** (8:41) | Active battle spoils | 1/5 (20%) | Allah, Messenger, Messenger-relatives, orphans, poor, wayfarer |
| **Fayʾ** (59:7) | Non-battle acquisition | 100% | Allah, Messenger, Messenger-relatives, orphans, poor, wayfarer |
| **Zakāt** (9:60) | Ordinary wealth (annual) | Rate unspecified in Quran | Poor, needy, collection-administrators, hearts-to-be-reconciled, slaves to be freed, debtors, in the way of Allah, wayfarer |

The spoils lists (ghanīma and fayʾ) are identical in their five named categories. The zakāt list is structurally different: it includes administrators (those who collect the tax), "hearts to be reconciled" (a political-integration function), slave-freeing (manumission), and debtors — a broader institutional reach. The spoils lists lack these institutional categories; they are more kinship-and-community focused (Messenger-relatives appear in spoils lists but not the zakāt list).

The inference: different revenue streams carry different distribution profiles tuned to their context. Spoils distribution is an ad hoc emergency response to sudden wealth; zakāt is a regularized social-welfare mechanism with administrative infrastructure implied.

**Negative space note**: no tax rate on ordinary wealth is specified in the Quran (zakāt percentages are in hadith/fiqh, not the Quran text). The Quran specifies one and only one explicit percentage — the one-fifth khums — and that is for the exceptional case of battle spoils. Ordinary wealth has no Quranic rate, only a direction of flow (toward the categories named in 9:60).

## 8. al-Anfāl 8:69 — Moral Permission as Closure

"So consume what you have taken of war booty as lawful and good, and fear Allah." [8:69]

After the dispute (8:1), the allocation (8:41), and extended battle narrative, 8:69 issues a moral green-light: what remains after the khums is distributed is halāl for the fighters to consume. The word **طَيِّبًا** (tayyiban — good, wholesome) pairs with halāl here, as it does at 2:168, 5:88, and elsewhere in the food-permission formulas. The fighters' 4/5 is declared not just legally permissible but morally wholesome — the distribution has cleansed the wealth.

## 9. Structural Pattern: Wealth as Moral Test

The arc across 8:1 → 8:41 → 59:6-10 is consistent: wealth acquired through conflict arrives as a moral test before it arrives as a legal problem. The faith-conditionals at 8:1 ("if you are believers") and 8:41 ("if you believe") frame the entire distribution architecture within the question of belief. The Quran treats the post-Badr wealth dispute not as a property case to be adjudicated but as a lesson in the relationship between faith and material attachment.

The distribution formulas resolve the test: once ownership is re-attributed to Allah (8:1), a fixed allocation is specified (8:41), and the rationale is declared (59:7 — lest it become a dūla), the wealth is liberated into a lawful circuit. The fighter may consume his 4/5 (8:69) only after the moral architecture has been established.

---

## 10. Negative Space: What Is Absent

- **No permanent state treasury.** The Quran handles spoils as events arise; there is no abstract theory of state revenue or annual budgeting.
- **No enforcement mechanism specified.** The khums system requires a Messenger or authority to collect and distribute; the Quran does not specify who administers this after the Messenger's death. Classical fiqh fills this gap; the Quran leaves it open.
- **No tax on ongoing wealth.** The only Quran-specified rate (20%) applies to the exceptional case of battle spoils. Ordinary accumulated wealth has no Quranic rate.
- **No investment or growth frame.** The distribution vocabulary is entirely consumptive — wealth flows to those who need it now. There is no concept of endowment, investment, or institutional accumulation.
- **No punishment for non-compliance with khums specified here.** 8:1 and 8:41 threaten no specific penalty; the consequence is moral (deficient faith). 59:7 closes with "Allah is severe in punishment" but as a general warning, not a specified penalty for khums-evasion.

## Rabbit Holes for Synthesis

1. **Dūla as political concept**: the word دُولَة at 59:7 is the etymological root of the modern Arabic state (dawla). The Quran uses it negatively — as a circulation to be prevented. The tension between "wealth that rotates [among the powerful]" and "the state" is buried in the same root.
2. **The Banū Hāshim exclusion from zakāt**: classical fiqh holds that the Messenger's relatives (ذِي ٱلۡقُرۡبَىٰ) who receive a share of khums/fayʾ are EXCLUDED from receiving zakāt, on the reasoning that they have their own revenue stream. The Quran does not say this; it is a juristic inference from the two lists having different compositions.
3. **The Anṣār iʾthār (preference) as economic principle**: the term **يُؤۡثِرُونَ** (giving preference) at 59:9 describes voluntary redistribution beyond legal mandate — giving from scarcity. This is a Quranic economic virtue above and beyond the legal khums/fayʾ system, and it has no legal compulsion attached to it.
