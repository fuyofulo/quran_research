# Narrative: The Inheritance Verses — an-Nisāʾ 4:7–14, 4:33, 4:176

## Headline Finding

The inheritance passage of an-Nisāʾ is the most quantitatively precise legal text in the Quran: six distinct fractions (1/2, 1/3, 1/4, 1/6, 1/8, 2/3) assigned across eleven conditional branches in two verses (4:11–12), closing with a seventh pass in 4:176. The architectonic move is 4:7's prior declaration — women receive an obligatory share (*naṣīban mafrūḍan*) — which overturns pre-Islamic Arabian custom that excluded women from inheritance entirely. The "after bequest and debt" formula appears four times in 4:11–12 alone, embedding a strict priority queue into the arithmetic: DEBT first, BEQUEST second, FRACTIONAL SHARES third. Classical exegetes then read the 2:1 male/female asymmetry through the male's financial obligations (mahr, household maintenance), but that justification is not present in the text of these verses; it is imported from elsewhere. The text says only what it says.

---

## 1. The Founding Principle: 4:7

**Arabic:**

> لِّلرِّجَالِ نَصِيبٌ مِّمَّا تَرَكَ الْوَالِدَانِ وَالْأَقْرَبُونَ وَلِلنِّسَاءِ نَصِيبٌ مِّمَّا تَرَكَ الْوَالِدَانِ وَالْأَقْرَبُونَ مِمَّا قَلَّ مِنْهُ أَوْ كَثُرَ ۚ نَصِيبًا مَّفْرُوضًا

| Translator | Rendering |
|---|---|
| Saheeh International | "For men is a share of what the parents and close relatives leave, and for women is a share of what the parents and close relatives leave, be it little or much — an obligatory share." |
| Pickthall | "Unto the men belongeth a share of that which parents and near kindred leave, and unto the women a share … a legal share." |
| Khattab | "For men there is a share … and for women there is a share … ˹These are˺ obligatory shares." |
| Arberry | "To the men a share … and to the women a share … a share apportioned." |

The Arabic closes with the phrase **نَصِيبًا مَّفْرُوضًا** (*naṣīban mafrūḍan*): a share that is imposed, prescribed, mandatory. The root *f-r-ḍ* (فرض) is the same root used for obligatory religious duties. This is the hinge: before specifying a single fraction, the text asserts the principle — both sexes inherit, from little or much, non-negotiably.

**Historical context (data-grounded):** Pre-Islamic Arabian custom allocated inheritance to adult male warriors who could defend tribal property. Women and children were effectively dependents, not heirs. 4:7 does not argue the point; it legislates the reversal as divine command.

---

## 2. The Bystanders Provision: 4:8

> وَإِذَا حَضَرَ الْقِسْمَةَ أُولُو الْقُرْبَىٰ وَالْيَتَامَىٰ وَالْمَسَاكِينُ فَارْزُقُوهُم مِّنْهُ وَقُولُوا لَهُمْ قَوْلًا مَّعْرُوفًا

[4:8] When the distribution is witnessed by relatives not in the inheritance list, orphans, and the needy, they are to receive *something* from the estate (*farzuqūhum minhu* — provide for them from it) and be spoken to graciously (*qawlan maʿrūfan*). This is a moral expectation, not an enforceable fraction. It operates in the moral register alongside the legal one: even non-heirs gain moral claim on the estate's moment of distribution. No amounts are given.

---

## 3. The Protective Inversion: 4:9

[4:9] "Let those who if they left behind weak offspring would fear for them — let them fear Allah and speak words of justice." The verse inverts the perspective: the administrator of another person's estate should imagine themselves in that person's place. If you would want your own children's inheritance protected, protect others' children's inheritance. This is theological motivation to comply with the legal frame just given.

---

## 4. The Harshest Financial Verdict: 4:10

**Arabic (quoted in full — the most violent finance-verdict in the text):**

> إِنَّ الَّذِينَ يَأْكُلُونَ أَمْوَالَ الْيَتَامَىٰ ظُلْمًا إِنَّمَا يَأْكُلُونَ فِي بُطُونِهِمْ نَارًا ۖ وَسَيَصْلَوْنَ سَعِيرًا

| Translator | Rendering |
|---|---|
| Saheeh International | "Indeed, those who devour the property of orphans unjustly are only consuming into their bellies fire. And they will be burned in a Blaze." |
| Pickthall | "Lo! Those who devour the wealth of orphans wrongfully, they do but swallow fire into their bellies, and they will be exposed to burning flame." |
| Khattab | "Indeed, those who unjustly consume orphans' wealth ˹in fact˺ consume nothing but fire into their bellies. And they will be burned in a blazing Hell." |
| Arberry | "Those who devour the property of orphans unjustly, devour Fire in their bellies, and shall assuredly roast in a Blaze." |

The word-picture is precise: the action of *eating* (*yaʾkulūna*) wrong wealth transforms into *eating fire* in the belly. The same verb — consume — applies to both the wrongful act and its eschatological consequence. This is not metaphor as ornament; it is punishment isomorphic to the crime.

## 5. The Fraction Table: 4:11–12 and 4:176

### 4:11 — Children and Parents

| Heir | Condition | Fraction |
|---|---|---|
| Male children (sons) | Always | 2× the female share |
| Daughters (2 or more) | No son present | 2/3 of estate (shared equally) |
| One daughter only | No son present | 1/2 of estate |
| Each parent (father, mother) | Deceased left children | 1/6 each |
| Mother only (father also inherits) | No children; parents are sole heirs | 1/3 |
| Mother | No children but siblings exist | 1/6 |

**Note on sons:** The text does not state a son's fraction as a number. It states: *lil-dhakari mithlu ḥaẓẓi al-unthayayn* — "the male gets the like of the share of two females." The son's share is derived by reference to daughters, not stated independently. If there is one son and one daughter, the estate divides into thirds: son gets 2/3, daughter 1/3. If there is one son and two daughters, it divides into fourths: son 2/4, each daughter 1/4.

### 4:12 — Spouses and Kalāla (sibling case A)

| Heir | Condition | Fraction |
|---|---|---|
| Husband inheriting from wife | Wife had no children | 1/2 |
| Husband inheriting from wife | Wife had children | 1/4 |
| Wife (or wives) inheriting from husband | Husband had no children | 1/4 |
| Wife (or wives) inheriting from husband | Husband had children | 1/8 |
| One brother or sister (maternal half-siblings per classical reading) | Kalāla: no ascendants or descendants | 1/6 each |
| Multiple siblings (maternal) | Kalāla; more than two | Share 1/3 equally |

**"After bequest and debt" formula — appearances:**
1. After husband's share when wife had no children [4:12a]: *min baʿdi waṣiyyatin yūṣīna bihā aw dayn*
2. After husband's share when wife had children [4:12a]: same phrase
3. After wife's share when husband had no children [4:12b]: *min baʿdi waṣiyyatin tūṣūna bihā aw dayn*
4. After siblings' share in kalāla case [4:12c]: *min baʿdi waṣiyyatin yūṣā bihā aw daynin*

The formula appears at each branch point, not once at the start. This is structurally significant: it attaches the priority rule to every individual fraction, ensuring no branch can be read as bypassing the queue.

### 4:176 — Kalāla Case B (full siblings)

| Heir | Condition | Fraction |
|---|---|---|
| One sister (full sibling) | Deceased: no children, no parents | 1/2 |
| Brother inherits entirely from sister | Sister: no children | All (no fraction given) |
| Two sisters (full) | No children, no parents | 2/3 (shared) |
| Mixed brothers and sisters (full) | No children, no parents | 2:1 male/female ratio |

The classical legal tradition distinguishes 4:12's sibling clause (maternal half-siblings, *akhun li-umm*) from 4:176's sibling clause (full siblings, *akhun shaqīq*). The fractions differ accordingly: maternal siblings share at most 1/3 total; full siblings can reach 2/3 for two sisters, and are governed by the 2:1 rule when mixed.

## 6. The "After Bequest and Debt" Priority Queue

The formula *min baʿdi waṣiyyatin … aw dayn* ("after any bequest … or debt") establishes three-tier priority:

1. **Debt (dayn):** Must be cleared first. No fraction is payable until all creditors are settled.
2. **Bequest (waṣiyya):** The voluntary testamentary disposition. Classical fiqh capped it at 1/3 of the remaining estate — this cap is *not stated in the verse itself* but is derived by jurists from the fractions: if you could bequest more than 1/3, the obligatory fractions would be unworkable for large estates. The verse's implicit logic thus constrains the bequest, though the ceiling is not written.
3. **Fractional shares:** Only after (1) and (2) are the Quranic fractions distributed.

The formula's four appearances in two verses constitute a legal *refrain* — each recurrence seals a new branch against circumvention.

## 7. The 2:1 Male/Female Asymmetry

**The verse's phrasing [4:11]:**

> لِلذَّكَرِ مِثْلُ حَظِّ الْأُنْثَيَيْنِ

*lil-dhakari mithlu ḥaẓẓi al-unthayayn* — "for the male, the like of the share of two females."

This formulation does three things:
- It makes the male share **derivative** of the female baseline, not the reverse.
- It does not state a reason.
- It covers children in 4:11 and is echoed verbatim for full siblings in 4:176.

**Data-grounded fact:** This is what the text says. The 2:1 ratio is stated as a divine command (*fareeḍatan min Allāh* — "an obligation from Allah"), not argued from any premise.

**Classical exegetical context (interpretive — marked as such):** Classical jurists — Shafi'i, Hanafi, Maliki, Hanbali — explain the asymmetry by noting that in the same legal system, a husband owes his wife *mahr* (bridal gift) and *nafaqa* (maintenance), while a wife owes neither to the husband. A daughter who inherits 1/2 retains her share entirely; a son who inherits 1× his sister's share will spend part of it on obligatory family expenses. Net benefit, classical argument goes, may be roughly equal. This argument is coherent but is reconstructed from *other* verses and *other* obligations — it is not present in 4:11 or 4:176 themselves. The text provides the ratio; the rationale is supplied by jurists working across the corpus.

**Negative space (data-grounded):** The verses do NOT mention mahr. They do NOT mention nafaqa. Any economic equalization argument is extra-textual.

## 8. The Mawālī Verse: 4:33

**Arabic:**

> وَلِكُلٍّ جَعَلْنَا مَوَالِيَ مِمَّا تَرَكَ الْوَالِدَانِ وَالْأَقْرَبُونَ ۚ وَالَّذِينَ عَقَدَتْ أَيْمَانُكُمْ فَآتُوهُمْ نَصِيبَهُمْ ۚ إِنَّ اللَّهَ كَانَ عَلَىٰ كُلِّ شَيْءٍ شَهِيدًا

| Translator | Rendering |
|---|---|
| Saheeh International | "And for all, We have made heirs to what is left by parents and relatives. And to those whom your oaths have bound [to you] — give them their share." |
| Pickthall | "And unto each We have appointed heirs of that which parents and near kindred leave; and as for those with whom your right hands have made a covenant, give them their due." |
| Khattab | "And We have appointed heirs to what has been left by parents and next of kin. As for those you have made a pledge to, give them their share." |
| Arberry | "To everyone We have appointed heirs of that which parents and kinsmen leave, and those with whom you have sworn compact. So give to them their share." |

The key term is **mawālī** (pl. of *mawlā*): a complex Arabic word covering clients, freed slaves, allies, and oath-bound associates. 4:33 sets up a two-tier system: (1) the Quranic fractional heirs from bloodline and marriage, and (2) those bound by *oath* (*ʿaqada aymānukum* — "whom your oaths have bound"), who also get their share. Classical readings differ on whether this second tier survived the later inheritance-by-blood consolidation or was abrogated — but the verse's plain meaning preserves a customary tier of oath-heirs alongside the bloodline system. The new system does not eliminate existing customary relationships; it absorbs them.

---

## 9. The Kalāla Verse: 4:176

[4:176] is traditionally numbered as one of the last, if not THE last, legal verse revealed in the Quran. The Prophet reportedly said he wished he had been asked about kalāla before this verse was revealed — signaling both its difficulty and its late arrival. The word *kalāla* itself (كَلَالَة) appears only twice in the Quran (4:12 and 4:176) and its precise Arabic definition was disputed even among early companions. Most settle on: a person who dies leaving neither ascendant (parent/grandparent) nor descendant (child/grandchild).

4:176 closes the surah on a legal note: *yubayyinu Allāhu lakum an taḍillū* — "Allah makes clear to you lest you go astray." The fear of *going astray* in inheritance matters is presented as equal in severity to going astray in theology. The kalāla case is the hardest edge case: nothing above, nothing below, only lateral kin. The verse handles it by applying the same 2:1 logic to full siblings that 4:11 applied to children.

---

## 10. Negative Space

What these verses do NOT specify:
- **How to distribute indivisible assets.** A house, a herd, a slave — the fractions assume liquid or divisible wealth. No mechanism for physical division is given.
- **Who administers the estate.** No executor role is described. 4:8 implies there is a "distribution" moment, but who organizes it is unspecified.
- **The 1/3 bequest cap.** Classical fiqh derived this ceiling from the fractional system's internal logic, but the ceiling is not stated.
- **The male's financial obligations** that classical exegetes use to justify the 2:1 ratio. *Mahr* and *nafaqa* are governed by other verses (e.g., 4:4, 4:34); they do not appear in the inheritance block.
- **What happens when the fractions exceed 100% of the estate** (the "ʿaul" problem, where e.g. a wife's 1/8, daughters' 2/3, and parents' 2×1/6 sum to more than 1). Classical jurists developed proportional reduction (*ʿaul*) to handle this — entirely extra-textual.
- **Estate disputes, courts, witnesses.** The verses command distribution; the apparatus for enforcement is absent.

---

## 11. Framing: The Legal Architecture

The inheritance block (4:7–14) has a recognizable structure:

1. **Principle** [4:7]: Both sexes inherit; it is mandatory.
2. **Moral frame** [4:8–9]: Non-heirs get moral provision; administrators must act protectively.
3. **Enforcement threat** [4:10]: Violators consume fire.
4. **Fractions — children and parents** [4:11]: Six conditional branches.
5. **Fractions — spouses and kalāla A** [4:12]: Five conditional branches plus the priority formula ×4.
6. **Closure** [4:13–14]: These are *ḥudūd Allāh* — Allah's limits. Compliance = paradise. Violation = hell.

The framing of inheritance law as *ḥudūd* (boundary limits) is architecturally significant. The same term governs fasting, marriage, and divorce rules. Inheritance is not presented as civil law that society may adjust — it is presented as a fixed divine boundary.

4:33 then inserts the oath-based customary tier, and 4:176 closes the surah with the hardest edge case, the *kalāla*.

The intellectual achievement of the passage is compression: eleven conditional branches, four fractions in 4:11–12 alone, a priority queue, a moral frame, an enforcement clause, and a founding principle — in roughly 250 words of Arabic.

## For Synthesis to Absorb

- The 2:1 asymmetry is texturally stated but rationale-free in the inheritance block. Any economic-equality argument requires cross-verse reasoning.
- The bequest cap (1/3) is the most practically important rule *derived* from this passage but *absent* from it — a clean example of how classical fiqh developed the implicit logic of the text.
- 4:7's *naṣīban mafrūḍan* uses the same *f-r-ḍ* root as obligatory prayer and fasting — this root is worth tracking across the finance investigation as a signal of absolute legal obligation versus conditional guidance.
- 4:10's fire-eating image is the most visceral economic punishment in the Quran. Compare with 9:34's gold-and-silver burning imagery (also inheritance-adjacent — hoarding) for the finance investigation's violence-of-wealth motif.
- The *ḥudūd Allāh* framing in 4:13–14 recurs in other legal blocks (divorce: 2:229–230, 65:1). This is a cross-investigation structural pattern — the Quran marks its fixed-limit rules with this phrase.
