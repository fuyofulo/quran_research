# Narrative: Maryam's ṣawm (19:26) and the identity-list (33:35)

## Headline finding

19:26 is the Quran's only unqualified use of the bare noun *ṣawm* — and in that single verse the faster is commanded to eat and drink. Maryam's fast is from speech, vowed to al-Raḥmān, self-imposed with no legislator, and functional: silence hands her defense to an infant who cannot lie. 33:35 places *ṣāʾimīn/ṣāʾimāt* eighth of ten paired qualities — flanked by giving before and guarding/remembering after — a settled disposition, not an event, rewarded only as part of the whole list. Neither occurrence is about hunger. Both are about what the abstention makes possible.

---

## 1. The philological fact

Every other occurrence of the root *Swm* in the Quran is the verbal noun *ṣiyām* (9x, all Medinan, all food-fasting: 2:183, 184–185, 187, 196; 4:92; 5:89, 95; 58:4), the verb *yaṣūm* (2x: 2:184, 185), or the participle *ṣāʾimīn/ṣāʾimāt* (33:35). Exactly once — 19:26 — the word is the bare masdar **صَوْمًا** (*ṣawman*), and it is the only Meccan occurrence in the set. This is not a stylistic variant; the lemma table confirms it is a distinct lemma (*Sawom*) appearing nowhere else in the corpus.

> **19:26** — فَكُلِي وَٱشۡرَبِي وَقَرِّي عَيۡنࣰ اۖ فَإِمَّا تَرَيِنَّ مِنَ ٱلۡبَشَرِ أَحَدࣰ ا فَقُولِيٓ إِنِّي نَذَرۡتُ لِلرَّحۡمَٰنِ صَوۡمࣰ ا فَلَنۡ أُكَلِّمَ ٱلۡيَوۡمَ إِنسِيًّا
> — Saheeh: "So eat and drink and be contented. And if you see from among humanity anyone, say, 'Indeed, I have vowed to the Most Merciful abstention, so I will not speak today to [any] man.'"
> — Khattab: "So eat and drink, and put your heart at ease... 'I have vowed silence to the Most Compassionate...'"
> — Arberry: "Eat therefore, and drink, and be comforted... 'I have vowed to the All-merciful a fast, and today I will not speak to any man.'"
> — Pickthall: "So eat and drink and be consoled... 'I have vowed a fast unto the Beneficent...'"

Read the sentence as one grammatical unit: *fa-kulī wa-shrabī wa-qarrī ʿaynan* — "so eat, and drink, and let your eye be cooled" — is the same imperative string, same speaker, same breath, as *innī nadhartu li-l-Raḥmāni ṣawman fa-lan ukallima al-yawma insiyyā* — "indeed I have vowed to al-Raḥmān a ṣawm, so I will not speak today to any human." Three of the four translators (Saheeh, Khattab, Pickthall) render *ṣawman* as "abstention," "silence," "a fast" — glossing it functionally as speech-abstention, because the text has just told her to eat. Only Arberry keeps the bare word "fast" and lets the contradiction with "eat and drink" stand starkly in English — closer to what the Arabic does: it hands the reader *ṣawm* with zero qualifier and lets the next clause — *fa-lan ukallima*, "I will not speak" — supply it. The Quran defines its own unqualified use of "fast" not by what is withheld from the body but by what is withheld from speech. This is data-grounded: it is the only place the word occurs without *ṣiyām*'s ritual food-frame, and the eating command sits inside the same verse.

## 2. A nadhr, addressed to al-Raḥmān, with a function

*Nadhartu* — "I have vowed" — is first-person, perfect tense, self-reported. Nobody commands this fast. Contrast the legislative fast: **2:183** opens *kutiba ʿalaykumu al-ṣiyām* — "fasting has been decreed/written upon you" — passive voice, plural addressee, external legislator. Maryam's fast has no *kutiba*, no imposing verb at all. She reports having already made the vow before the reader arrives at the sentence. This is the clearest structural split in the whole *Swm* dataset: the Quran has exactly one instance of *ṣawm* as personal vow and it sits opposite nine instances of *ṣiyām* as communal decree or legal remedy.

The addressee matters as much as the grammar. She does not vow to Allah in the generic sense, nor to *al-Rabb* (the term the angel uses of himself in 19:19, 21). She vows *li-l-Ra�ma̅n* — to the Most Merciful — the same name she invokes at the start of the episode, **19:18**: *innī aʿūdhu bi-l-Raḥmāni minka in kunta taqiyyā* — "I seek refuge in the Most Merciful from you, if you are God-fearing" — spoken to the angel she does not yet recognize. The name al-Raḥmān brackets the whole sequence: refuge from a stranger at the beginning, a vow at the end, both addressed to the same name, neither addressed to a lawgiver.

And the fast has a stated function that the legislative fasts never state for themselves. She is about to walk into her community carrying a child she cannot explain (19:27: *laqad jiʾti shayʾan fariyyā* — "you have done a monstrous thing"; 19:28: an explicit accusation against her lineage). She has no answer that will satisfy them — any answer she gives will sound like what it is: unbelievable. So she does not answer. She points. **19:29** — *fa-ashārat ilayhi* — "so she pointed to him" — and lets the infant speak in her place. The vow of silence is not devotional background; it is the specific mechanism that converts an unanswerable accusation into a miracle. This is interpretive but text-anchored: the vow is made in 19:26, the accusation arrives in 19:27, and the silence is the only tool she has when it does.

## 3. 19:16–26 as one movement, and what the silence produces

*Intabadhat* — "she withdrew" — occurs twice for Maryam: **19:16**, withdrawing from her family to a place east; **19:22**, withdrawing further, to a remote place, now pregnant and about to give birth. The second withdrawal is worse than the first — alone, in labor, at **19:23** she says *yā laytanī mittu qabla hādhā wa-kuntu nasyan mansiyyā* — "I wish I had died before this and been a thing forgotten." This is the low point of the passage, arguably of her life in the text.

The response to that low point is not instruction to endure. It is provision: a voice tells her a stream runs beneath her (19:24), a palm trunk will drop ripe dates if she shakes it (19:25), and only then — **19:26** — the command to eat, drink, be at ease, and the vow of silence. The fast is not what gets her through the ordeal; the ordeal is already answered by the stream and the dates, and the silence is set inside that provision, not instead of it.

The silence works. **19:29–30**: they ask *kayfa nukallimu man kāna fī al-mahdi ṣabiyyā* — "how can we speak to one who is in the cradle, a child?" — and the infant answers: *innī ʿabdu Allāhi ātāniya al-kitāba wa-jaʿalanī nabiyyā* — "I am the servant of Allah; He has given me the Scripture and made me a prophet." Her silence does not simply protect her from having to lie or explain — it produces the miracle. The abstention is instrumental, not endurance for its own sake.

## 4. 33:35 — the list of ten

**33:35** is a single sentence built from ten paired masculine/feminine terms, in this order:

1. al-muslimīn / al-muslimāt — those who submit
2. al-muʾminīn / al-muʾmināt — those who believe
3. al-qānitīn / al-qānitāt — those devoutly obedient
4. al-ṣādiqīn / al-ṣādiqāt — those truthful
5. al-ṣābirīn / al-ṣābirāt — those patient/enduring
6. al-khāshiʿīn / al-khāshiʿāt — those humble
7. al-mutaṣaddiqīn / al-mutaṣaddiqāt — those who give charity
8. **al-ṣāʾimīn / al-ṣāʾimāt — those who fast**
9. al-ḥāfiẓīna furūjahum / al-ḥāfiẓāt — those who guard their private parts
10. al-dhākirīna Allāha kathīran / al-dhākirāt — those who remember Allah often

Fasting sits at position 8 of 10. It is immediately preceded by *mutaṣaddiqīn* — givers of charity — and immediately followed by *ḥāfiẓū furūjihim*, guarding the private parts, which is itself followed by the closing item, abundant remembrance. Data-grounded: this is the Arabic word order, confirmed against the text directly. Fasting is flanked by an outward act of giving on one side and an inward discipline plus remembrance on the other — it sits at the hinge between what one does with wealth and what one does with the self.

Grammatically, every term in the list is a participle — an ongoing doer, not a completed act. *Ṣāʾim* is "one who fasts" as a standing description, the same morphological class as *muslim* or *muʾmin*, not a report of an event like *ṣāma* ("he fasted [on some day]"). The reward — *maghfiratan wa-ajran ʿaẓīmā*, "forgiveness and a great reward" — is a single grant at the end of the sentence, attached to the whole ten-item list, not distributed per item. There is no reward clause specific to fasting. It earns nothing alone; it counts as one strand of a woven identity.

The list culminates in *al-dhākirīna Allāha kathīran wa-l-dhākirāt* — those, male and female, who remember God much. Interpretively: the list does not end on submission (item 1, the largest category) or on obedience or on any outward marker — it ends on an interior, repeated, unmeasurable act. Whatever the nine preceding terms build toward, the text's own ordering makes that its terminus.

## 5. Synthesis

Put the two occurrences side by side. 19:26 is the Quran's one unqualified word for "fast," and it is an abstention from speech, explicitly co-present with eating. 33:35 is the Quran's one appearance of fasting as identity rather than event, and it is one settled disposition among ten, unrewarded on its own, opening onto charity and closing into remembrance. Neither verse locates fasting's value in the body's hunger. 19:26 locates it in what silence accomplishes in a specific crisis; 33:35 locates it in what a life built from ten interlocking habits accomplishes together.

For someone who fasted seven days from food and felt nothing: the text's own two non-legal appearances of the word never once frame the fast as an experience to be felt. Maryam's fast produces an outcome (the child speaks) because it was built for one — an unanswerable moment, a specific silence, a specific effect. The list's fast produces nothing alone because nothing in the list is designed to. Feeling nothing from hunger by itself is not a deviation from what these two verses describe — it may be closer to what they'd predict.

## For the fast

- Ask what the fast is *for*, concretely, before starting — Maryam's silence had a named target (an accusation she couldn't answer). Hunger without a target is just hunger.
- Consider what you are simultaneously *doing* during the fast, not only what you're avoiding — she eats and drinks; the abstention was never total.
- Don't expect the fast alone to carry weight it isn't structured to carry — 33:35 never rewards fasting by itself. Pair it with what flanks it: something given, something remembered.
- If the goal is nearness, note both non-legal appearances of *ṣawm* are addressed to al-Raḥmān by name, not a generic deity — relational, not transactional.
