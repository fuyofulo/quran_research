# Root: Swm (ص و م) — fasting

## Headline finding (100 words)

The Quran's grammar of fasting is dominated by the masdar *ṣiyām* (9/14) — fasting as an abstract, legislated act, not a repeated verbal command (the verb *yaṣum* fires only twice, both in the founding passage, and never again). Two distinct syntactic postures exist: **decree** (2:183's *kutiba ʿalaykum*, unconditional, one occurrence) and **fallback** (five kaffāra/hajj verses, all structured as *fa-man lam yajid fa-ṣiyāmu…* — "whoever cannot find [the primary remedy], then fasting of…"). The verb takes *time* as its object (2:185: "fast it," the month), never food. Fasting-as-identity (*ṣāʾim*) occurs exactly once — buried eighth in a list of ten — and is Medinan-only as a legal category; the sole Meccan use (19:26) is a private vow of silence, not food.

Twelve of fourteen occurrences are nominal; only two are verbal, and both belong to the single passage that establishes the obligation (2:184–185). After that, the Quran never again *commands* fasting with a verb — it refers back to fasting as an established legal category, using the masdar *ṣiyām* inside conditional and legislative clauses (2:187, 2:196, 4:92, 5:89, 5:95, 58:4). The imperative is spent early and once; everything downstream *reasons with* an already-established noun. The text does not repeatedly exhort "fast" — it legislates around a fast already assumed to be happening.

The active participle *ṣāʾim* — the faster as a *person*, a standing identity rather than an act — appears exactly once, at 33:35, eighth of ten paired qualities (muslim, mu'min, qānit, ṣādiq, ṣābir, khāshiʿ, mutaṣaddiq, **ṣāʾim**, ḥāfiẓ al-furūj, dhākir). Data-grounded: no other verse anywhere in the corpus describes a person *as* a faster. Interpretively, this is a striking absence — the Quran has a rich vocabulary of standing identity-labels for believers (the patient ones, the truthful ones, the humble ones), and fasting gets exactly one entry, unelaborated, mid-list (translation divergence there is a middling 0.6424 — translators aren't fighting over this verse). The Quran legislates fasting heavily as an *act* (9 masdar occurrences) but almost not at all as an *identity* (1 unelaborated participle).

## Two postures: decree vs. fallback

**Decree posture — 2:183.** *كُتِبَ عَلَيۡكُمُ ٱلصِّيَامُ* — "*kutiba ʿalaykumu l-ṣiyām*," a passive verb (root ktb, "was written") with a direct prepositional object ("upon you"). No condition, no substitution clause. This is the only occurrence of ṣawm framed as an unconditional legislative imposition, addressed to "those who believe" as a class, with a stated purpose attached (*laʿallakum tattaqūn*).

**Fallback posture — 4:92, 5:89, 58:4 (and structurally 2:196).** Three of the five kaffāra verses use the *identical formula*: *فَمَن لَّمۡ يَجِدۡ فَصِيَامُ...* — "*fa-man lam yajid fa-ṣiyāmu*..." — "then whoever does not find [the primary remedy — a slave to free, a payment to make], then fasting of [N]." Fasting is grammatically the **apodosis of a conditional clause triggered by another party's inability**. 2:196 repeats this exact structure twice in one ayah (once for the ḥajj ransom-triad, once for the substitute offering). 5:95 varies slightly — *"aw ʿadlu dhālika ṣiyāman"* ("or the equivalent of that, as fasting") — a third rung in a disjunctive triad (sacrifice / feeding / fasting) rather than a strict *lam yajid* clause, but structurally identical in function: fasting is the last-listed, not the first-offered, option.

Named precisely, not blurred: **decree** (unconditional, addressed directly to the community, one occurrence) versus **substitution-apodosis** (conditional, triggered by another act's failure, six occurrences across five verses). Ten of fourteen total occurrences sit downstream of one or the other posture, and substitution outnumbers decree 6:1 among the legal (non-Ramadan) material.

## What the verb does: fasting takes time as its object, not food

*yaṣum* fires twice, both in 2:184–185, and both are instructive. 2:184: *"wa-an taṣūmū khayrun lakum"* — "and that you fast is better for you" — the verb here has no object at all, only the fasting-act named comparatively. 2:185 is the sharper case: *فَمَن شَهِدَ مِنكُمُ ٱلشَّهۡرَ فَلۡيَصُمۡهُ* — "whoever among you witnesses the month, **let him fast it**." The pronoun *-hu* attaches directly to the verb and refers back grammatically to *al-shahr* (the month) two words earlier. This is data-grounded and unambiguous: the one place the Quran's fasting-verb takes a direct object, the object is **a period of time**, not food, not the body, not an emotion.

I checked whether *ṣawm* is ever given a food-object anywhere in the 14 occurrences — it is not. The root *TEm* (food/feeding) co-occurs with *Swm* in 4 ayahs, but always in a *different clause*: the *fidya* (ransom of feeding a poor person, 2:184) or the alternative remedy in a triad (2:196, 5:95) — never what is grammatically fasted. What is prohibited during a fast (eating, drinking, marital relations) is specified separately in 2:187 by naming what becomes *permitted again at night* — the fast's boundary is defined by its edges (dawn to night), not by giving the verb "fast" an object of "food." The single grammatical object the fasting-verb ever takes, in the entire Quran, is a month.

## The Meccan/Medinan split

13 of 14 occurrences are Medinan; the sole Meccan occurrence is 19:26, where *ṣawm* is a private vow of silence Maryam makes to God, unconnected to food, calendar, or communal law: *"innī nadhartu li-l-raḥmāni ṣawman fa-lan ukallima l-yawma insiyyā"* — "I have vowed to the Most Merciful a ṣawm, so I will not speak today to any human." Data-grounded: this is the only occurrence where *ṣawm* is bare, unqualified by "days" or a calendar reference, and it is not about food at all — she is explicitly told to eat and drink (*fa-kulī wa-shrabī*) two words earlier.

Interpretively: within the Quran's own compositional order, *ṣawm*-as-institution (dated, communal, legally administered, with substitution ladders and penalty clauses) belongs entirely to the Medinan phase — when the community had the political structure to legislate collectively. In Mecca, the root's only appearance is an individual's private restraint (root sense: to hold still), applied to speech rather than food, vowed by one person with no communal apparatus attached. This doesn't mean fasting-from-food was absent from pre-Medinan practice generally (the text is silent on that); it means the *root Swm itself* isn't used for a communal food-fast until Medina. As a legal category, fasting is a Medinan development; as a root-concept — restraint from something, vowed to God — it predates that and needs no state apparatus at all.

## Negative space (verified, not assumed)

I checked the full 132-root co-occurrence list for `Swm` before asserting any absence:

- **No reward-root, checked specifically.** *Ajr* (reward) and *gfr* (forgiveness) each share exactly **one** ayah with *Swm* — 33:35 — and there the reward/forgiveness is promised to the entire ten-item list collectively ("Allah has prepared for them forgiveness and a great reward"), not to fasting specifically. In no Ramadan verse, no kaffāra verse, and no hajj-substitute verse is any reward or forgiveness promised *for the fast itself*. The only stated purpose anywhere in the ṣawm-passages is 2:183's *laʿallakum tattaqūn*, echoed once more at the close of the whole passage in 2:187's *laʿallahum yattaqūn* — a purpose clause, not a reward clause.
- **Hunger is never named.** The root *jwE* (hunger) does not appear in the co-occurrence list at all — zero shared ayahs with *Swm* anywhere in the corpus. The text describes the fast's temporal boundary (dawn to night) and what becomes permitted after it, but never once names or dwells on the sensation of hunger.
- **Intention is never named.** The root *nwy* (intention/niyya) likewise does not appear anywhere in the co-occurrence list. There is no Quranic instruction to formulate an intention before a fast; every instruction that exists is behavioral and temporal (when to start/stop eating, what invalidates the fast, what substitutes for a missed day).
- **Only one spiritual outcome is named, stated exactly twice, never elaborated.** *Taqwā* (root wqy) co-occurs with *Swm* in 3 ayahs — 2:183, 2:187 (both purpose-clauses) and one further instance. No fasting verse anywhere describes a felt spiritual state resulting from the practice; the taqwā clause states a *hoped-for orientation* (*laʿalla* = "perhaps"), not a guaranteed or described experience.

## For the fast

- The verb's only direct object in the whole Quran is *time* — a month. The discipline the text legislates is about occupying a period, not enduring a hunger it never names. Where attention goes matters more than what's endured.
- No reward or forgiveness is ever attached to fasting alone in the text — only the hoped-for orientation toward taqwā, stated twice, framed with "perhaps." Don't expect the text to promise an outcome it doesn't promise.
- The Quran never prescribes an intention formula for a fast. If niyya matters to a fast, that's inherited practice, not something this root's occurrences instruct.
- *Ṣāʾim* — "one who fasts" as an identity — occurs exactly once, unelaborated, eighth of ten items in a list. The text does not build fasting into a standing identity the way it does patience or truthfulness. Treat the act as legislated; don't over-invest in the identity.
- The founding decree (2:183, unconditional) and every restitutive occurrence downstream (fallback, conditional) are grammatically different acts. What you're doing in Ramadan is not the same syntactic posture as what a kaffāra fast repairs — one is address, the other is remedy.
