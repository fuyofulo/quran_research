# Sbr (ص ب ر) and the Quran's vocabulary of hunger

## Headline finding

*Ṣabr* is not "patience" in the English passive-waiting sense — the lemma table is dominated by
imperatives (58 verbal forms, mostly commands: iṣbir) and active participles (one-who-restrains),
and 3:200 places it in a triad with *rābiṭū*, "tie/garrison," from the same root as tying a horse at
a frontier post. It means active self-restraint, not endurance-by-default. Ṣabr's paired instrument
in 2:45 and 2:153 is prayer, not fasting — fasting is nowhere named alongside it. Hunger (*jūʿ*, 5
occurrences) is uniformly negative in the Quran: a test, a punishment, or a horror whose *absence*
is the blessing. It is never itself praised. Feeding others is praised roughly 3x as often, across
5x as many surahs, as fasting is.

---

## 1. Ṣabr is not "patience"

**Data-grounded.** 103 occurrences, 93 ayahs, 45 surahs, 8 lemmas. POS: 62 verbs, 35 nouns, 6
adjectives — a root that is more verb than noun, more command than description.

| Lemma | Count | Form | What it is doing |
|---|---|---|---|
| *ṣabara* | 58 | V | overwhelmingly imperative (iṣbir/iṣbirū) — "restrain yourself," a command |
| *ṣābir* | 20 | N/ADJ | active participle, "one who restrains" — always plural, "the restrainers" |
| *ṣabr* | 15 | N | the masdar itself — instrument (2:45, 2:153), verdict ("fa-ṣabrun jamīl," 12:18, 12:83) |
| *ṣabbār* | 4 | ADJ | intensive, "one who constantly restrains" — every instance paired with *shakūr* (grateful): 14:5, 31:31, 34:19, 42:33 |
| *iṣṭabir* | 3 | V | reflexive Form VIII, "impose restraint on yourself" — 19:65, 20:132, 54:27, all commands to persist against something pulling you off |
| *ṣābirū* | 1 | V | Form III, competitive — "outdo each other in restraint" (3:200) |
| *ṣābirah/ṣābirāt* | 2 | N/ADJ | feminine forms, 8:66, 33:35 |

The verb is 58/103 occurrences and the imperative dominates that count. This is a root whose most
common grammatical shape is a command to *do something to yourself*, not a description of a state
you're already in. The dominant classical gloss "patience" smuggles in passivity that the Arabic
resists.

**Interpretive but well-supported.** The Yūsuf story is the clearest test case. Jacob's response to
losing Yūsuf and then Benjamin is *fa-ṣabrun jamīl* (12:18, 12:83) — "beautiful ṣabr" — immediately
after he is shown fabricated evidence of tragedy. This is not waiting for something to end; it is the
active suppression of a visible reaction while grief continues underneath (12:84 has him weep in
private after). The Khiḍr story (18:60-82) uses ṣabr 8 times in 22 verses — every instance is about
Moses's *failure* to restrain himself from objecting, breaking a condition, reacting. That is the root's
working definition inside its longest narrative deployment: the capacity to hold a reaction in, not
the capacity to wait something out.

The decisive verse for the "binding/restraining" semantic field:

> **3:200** — يَٰٓأَيُّهَا ٱلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُواْ ٱصۡبِرُواْ وَصَابِرُواْ وَرَابِطُواْ وَٱتَّقُواْ ٱللَّهَ لَعَلَّكُمۡ تُفۡلِحُونَ
> *Saheeh:* "persevere and endure and remain stationed and fear Allah..."
> *Pickthall:* "Endure, outdo all others in endurance, be ready..."

Three verbs in sequence: *iṣbirū* (restrain), *ṣābirū* (out-restrain each other, competitively), *rābiṭū*
(from *ribāṭ* — tie your horse at the frontier, garrison). The Quran itself places ṣabr in a triad with
a verb whose root sense is literally "to tie." Translators render *rābiṭū* as "be ready" or "remain
stationed," losing the binding image entirely — the same flattening that turns ṣabr into "patience."

**Translation strain.** Compare renderings of the same phrase *istaʿīnū bi-l-ṣabri* across 2:45/2:153:
Saheeh and Khattab both say "patience," Pickthall says "steadfastness," and Khattab's 2:153 shifts
again to "seek comfort" — a markedly passive, almost therapeutic gloss that has no restraint sense
left in it at all. Four translators, three different registers, for one word. That spread is the
signal: English doesn't have a clean single-word equivalent for "commanded active restraint."

## 2. 2:45 and 2:153: ṣabr's actual partner is prayer, not fasting

> **2:153** — يَٰٓأَيُّهَا ٱلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُواْ ٱسۡتَعِينُواْ بِٱلصَّبۡرِ وَٱلصَّلَوٰةِۚ إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ مَعَ ٱلصَّٰبِرِينَ
> "Seek help through ṣabr and prayer. Indeed Allah is with the restrainers."

**Data-grounded.** 2:45 and 2:153 both pair the same two instruments: ṣabr and ṣalāh (*Slw*, the
prayer root — 7 ayahs shared with Sbr, its strongest co-occurring root after divine-name/pronoun
roots). Fasting (*Swm*) is not named in either verse, and does not appear anywhere in the Sbr
co-occurrence table at meaningful frequency. The pairing the text actually makes is restraint +
prayer, not restraint + fasting.

**Interpretive.** Classical exegesis (e.g. reading fasting as a species of ṣabr — the "month of ṣabr"
epithet applied to Ramadan in hadith literature) treats fasting as one instance of a broader
restraint-capacity that 2:45/2:153 are invoking. That is a defensible inference — fasting is,
structurally, an act of self-restraint, and ṣabr's semantic range (see §1) would comfortably include
it. But it is an inference. The text of 2:45 and 2:153 does not say fasting; it says prayer. Anyone
building a fast around "seeking help through ṣabr" is extending the verse, not quoting it.

## 3. 2:155 — hunger as affliction, not choice

> **2:155** — وَلَنَبۡلُوَنَّكُم بِشَيۡءࣲ مِّنَ ٱلۡخَوۡفِ وَٱلۡجُوعِ وَنَقۡصࣲ مِّنَ ٱلۡأَمۡوَٰلِ وَٱلۡأَنفُسِ وَٱلثَّمَرَٰتِۗ وَبَشِّرِ ٱلصَّٰبِرِينَ
> "We will surely test you with something of fear and hunger [*jūʿ*] and a loss of wealth, lives, and
> fruits — but give good tidings to the restrainers."

**Data-grounded.** This is the only ayah in the whole Quran where Sbr and *jwE* (hunger) share a
verse — 1 of 103 occurrences, per the co-occurrence table. Hunger here is God-sent, involuntary, one
item in a list of afflictions (fear, hunger, loss of property/life/crops), and the commanded response
is ṣabr — restraint against panic or complaint, not the hunger itself as virtue. This is structurally
opposite to fasting: in 2:183-185 hunger is never named, and the deprivation is chosen and scheduled;
here hunger is named explicitly, and it is unchosen and inflicted.

## 4. Is hunger ever positive? A full search of *jūʿ*

**Data-grounded — the complete list.** The root *jwE* occurs exactly 5 times in the Quran:

| Ref | Context | Valence |
|---|---|---|
| 2:155 | one of five afflictions in the test-verse | affliction |
| 16:112 | "the garment of hunger and fear" — punishment for ingratitude | punishment |
| 20:118 | Adam in paradise: "you will not be hungry there, nor exposed" | blessing = *absence* of hunger |
| 88:7 | Hellfire food that "neither nourishes nor avails against hunger" | hunger persists as curse, even while eating |
| 106:4 | Quraysh "fed against hunger" | blessing = hunger *relieved* |

There is no sixth sense. Hunger is never commanded, never chosen, never praised as a spiritual good
in its own right. It is either a test, a punishment, a curse, or something whose removal is the mercy.
**This is the significant negative finding the brief asked me to check for: the Quran never praises
hunger itself, anywhere, under any root.** *Ẓamaʾ* (thirst, root *ZmA*) is even thinner — 3
occurrences (9:120, 20:119, 24:39) — and follows the identical pattern: an affliction endured for
God's cause (9:120) or a threat removed as blessing (20:119, Adam again). Same shape, same
uniformly negative valence.

> **16:112** — فَأَذَٰقَهَا ٱللَّهُ لِبَاسَ ٱلۡجُوعِ وَٱلۡخَوۡفِ / **106:4** — ٱلَّذِيٓ أَطۡعَمَهُم مِّن جُوعࣲ وَءَامَنَهُم مِّنۡ خَوۡفِۭ
> "God made it taste the garment of hunger and fear" (punishment for ingratitude) vs. "He fed them
> against hunger, and secured them against fear" (the favor owed for worship).

**Interpretive, held carefully.** Fasting's hunger is scheduled and chosen; 16:112/106:4's hunger is
inflicted or graciously lifted, with no schedule and no exit chosen by the sufferer. The Quran never
puts fasting-hunger and punishment-hunger in the same verse to compare them — that they look
structurally opposite is my inference from placing them side by side, not a claim the text makes.
Where the text is silent, it is silent: it offers no theory connecting Ramadan's chosen hunger to the
imposed hunger of 2:155, 16:112, or 106:4. They simply never meet.

## 5. The feeding counterweight

**Data-grounded — counted directly.** Verses that praise feeding others, or condemn the failure to
do so, drawn from the `feeding-others` versepack group (32 verses total, most of them surrounding
context):

- **76:8-9** — feed food "in spite of love for it," for God's face alone, wanting no reward — direct praise (2 verses)
- **90:14** — "feeding on a day of severe hunger" as one of the acts that breaks the difficult pass — direct praise (1 verse)
- **69:34** — condemned for not urging the feeding of the poor (1 verse)
- **107:3** — condemned for not urging the feeding of the poor (1 verse)
- **89:18** — condemned for not encouraging one another to feed the poor (1 verse)

**Total: 6 verses of feeding-praise (or its absence condemned), spread across 5 separate surahs**
(76, 90, 69, 107, 89 — four of them Meccan, independent of each other, not one repeated passage).

Fasting-praise, by contrast, is confined to 2 verses (2:183's stated purpose, 2:184's "fasting is
better for you") inside a single Medinan passage (2:183-185). Every other Swm occurrence (10 of 14)
is legislative — expiation, hajj substitution, timing — not praise.

**The count supports the hypothesis.** Feeding-praise outnumbers fasting-praise roughly 3:1 by verse
and recurs independently across 5x as many surahs as fasting-praise does. The emotional register
differs too: 76:9's "we feed you only for God's face, we want no reward nor thanks" is warmer and
more interior than anything said about fasting itself. Notably, 76:12 rewards "what they *ṣabarū*"
(endured/restrained) immediately after the feeding passage — ṣabr's reward there sits attached to a
verse about giving food away, not withholding it. If the Quran has an emotional center of gravity
around food, the data places it on generosity, not abstinence.

## For the fast

- Don't translate ṣabr as "patience" while fasting and expect a waiting-room feeling — the word
  commands active restraint, closer to gripping something shut than to sitting still.
- 2:45/2:153 pair ṣabr with prayer. If a fast is meant to "seek help," pray inside it — the text
  names that instrument, not the hunger itself.
- Hunger is not the Quran's spiritual currency. Nowhere is it praised. If a fast is producing hunger
  and nothing else, that hunger has no independent standing in the text — it is neutral at best,
  something to be *ended*, not sat with as an end.
- The verse count says: if a fast is meant to build the disposition the Quran actually rewards
  warmly, feed someone during it. 76:8-9 and 90:14 make giving food away — not abstaining — the act
  that gets the vivid praise.
