# What people actually say worked (and didn't) — fasting stories from the internet

**Headline:** Across dozens of first-person accounts — Muslim, convert, Jewish, Christian, and secular — the fasts people call transformative are never described as "I stopped eating." They're described by what got added into the emptied-out time: night prayer, a Qur'an-reading schedule, deleted social media, a reframing of *why*, or (for reverts) sheer isolation forcing direct address to God. The honest failure accounts converge on the same thing in reverse: intensity without sustainability, ritual without attention, hunger with nothing poured into the space it opened.

---

## 1. The pattern in what people credit

Reading across the accounts, almost nobody who describes a fast as meaningful credits the abstaining itself. They credit something they did *with* the emptied space.

A first-time faster on a 21-day church-sponsored challenge quit social media and food together, and explicitly names the substitution as the mechanism: "he replaced scrolling with reading books and Bible apps," redirecting Thursday hunger pangs "toward prayer and gratitude" — [Andy Bachert, Substack](https://andybachert.substack.com/p/experiencing-my-first-fast).

A Muslim blogger managing chronic illness during Ramadan says the thing that changed her fast wasn't discipline but a line from her imam: "Fasting isn't about you. It's about Allah" — which she says "reduced performance anxiety by redirecting focus from personal capability to divine purpose." She paired that reframe with adapted taraweeh (2–6 rakats sitting if needed) and a memorized forgiveness dua repeated through the month — [gentlyrooted, "Oh Sinner Rejoice"](https://gentlyrooted.substack.com/p/oh-sinner-rejoice).

A tahajjud account on MuslimMatters is the clearest single data point on "what got added." The author started tahajjud not from a rulebook but from need — "I needed to feel closer to Allah more than I needed sleep" — kept it inconsistent and small ("some nights I could only pray two rak'ahs before going back to bed"), and reports it became "a safe place" that made her "a more patient mother, a better wife, and a kinder friend." During the Gaza crisis specifically, she writes tahajjud "became a lifeline" when "sleep became impossible" — [MuslimMatters, "A Tahajjud Journey"](https://muslimmatters.org/2025/03/01/a-tahajjud-journey-to-inspire-your-own-a-ramadan-goal-thats-not-as-hard-as-it-seems/).

The social-media-fast accounts are the most concrete evidence for "addition, not subtraction" because the authors track the substitution explicitly. A writer who quit Instagram for four weeks during Ramadan says "the quietness" was the biggest change, read five books she "previously struggled to read a chapter" of, and started taking walks without her phone — [Bustle](https://www.bustle.com/wellness/what-i-really-learnt-giving-up-social-media-during-ramadan). Another who quit Instagram, Twitter, and Facebook says that by day 20, "without the endless buzz and voices of my feed, I was able to say things with intention" — [Vice](https://www.vice.com/en/article/why-i-decided-to-do-a-social-media-fast-this-ramadan/). Notably, both admit they couldn't sustain full abstinence and drifted to other distractions (Wikipedia, Netflix) — the removal alone didn't hold; it needed something to hold its place.

A Christian 40-day social media faster names a specific day: day 9, "in her car," she felt "deeply seen by the Lord" and her desire to engage with scripture "notably strengthened." Also notable — by day 41, back on the apps for 30–45 minutes, she felt "scattered and overstimulated," suggesting the clarity wasn't a permanent trait gained but a state tied to the practice — [embracingthechaosblog](https://embracingthechaosblog.com/2022/05/04/my-social-media-fast-recap/).

The one place the pattern gets complicated: a secular 7-day water-faster reports mental clarity arriving by day 3 with no added practice at all beyond "sitting with the discomfort" rather than distracting from it — [madhusavara.com](https://www.madhusavara.com/kitchen-blog/my-7-day-water-fast-experience-mental-clarity-inner-strength/). That's a genuine data point against a strong "addition" reading: for at least some people, the *removal itself*, undistracted, is what does the work — not stacking a new practice on top, but refusing to fill the gap with a substitute either. This is worth taking seriously rather than filing under noise.

## 2. The honest negative accounts — report, not resolved

These are the accounts closest to the commissioner's own experience: fasted, felt nothing, or felt like performance.

A Ramadan-burnout writer on WordPress describes the mechanism precisely: she "increased worship dramatically" during the month, then hit "spiritual burnout" in the final ten days, and names the actual failure mode — "mistaking Ramadan for a magic pill that instantly transforms character flaws." Her line: "increasing my worship and my quest for knowledge so dramatically meant a spiritual burnout was inevitable," and she uses a paracetamol analogy — two tablets heal, four harm — [bonsaisky.wordpress.com, "Ramadan Reality Check"](https://bonsaisky.wordpress.com/2015/06/15/ramadan-reality-check-burn-out-or-speed-up/).

A revert's blog post is the most nakedly honest account found: "I didn't feel connected to my Lord this Ramadan." She describes a month consumed by household labor — "covered in groundnut oil," making chin-chin from dusk until iftar — falling ill, and never finishing a tafsir series she'd started, spending the freed time on social media instead "as stress relief." She did add tahajjud (4:33 AM) and Qur'an reading (5:00 AM) in the last ten nights but says it came too late and too thin to change how the month felt. She closes not with resolution but with a request to herself: "be happy" on Eid anyway — [Aisha Safiyanu, "The Eid Paradox"](https://ayeeshatoooo.substack.com/p/the-eid-paradox).

A blogger who quit religious practice describes breaking her Ramadan fast without the guilt she expected: "I didn't feel guilty at all, because I did not see the point any more" — she "couldn't convince myself it was worth doing" — reported via a Patheos guest post on "failing Ramadan." (Sourced via search snippet; the original page returned a 403 on direct fetch, so treat the exact wording as a paraphrase of the indexed excerpt rather than a verified direct quote.)

A convert's post captures the isolation version of the same emptiness — not doubt about God, but doubt about whether she was doing it right at all: "Why does everyone else seem so confident? Why does this feel both beautiful and lonely?" She was fasting completely alone — no Muslim family, mosques closed for lockdown, "preparing iftar for one" — and says what got her through wasn't resolving the loneliness but finding other reverts in the same position, via an online community called Solace UK — [Solace UK, "My First Ramadan as a Revert"](https://www.solaceuk.org/2026/03/02/my-first-ramadan-as-a-revert-belonging-in-a-blessed-month/).

On the Jewish side, a Yom Kippur essay describes fasting becoming entangled with an eating disorder at 16 — the public performance of religious commitment masking "an entirely different story" internally — and a second account of someone who fasted yearly for atonement until an undiagnosed illness forced her to stop, adding the extra burden of hiding her illness from extended family rather than explaining why she'd stopped — both via [Hey Alma, "It's Complicated: Fasting on Yom Kippur"](https://www.heyalma.com/high-holiday-guide/its-complicated-fasting-on-yom-kippur/).

What these accounts have in common, stated plainly rather than softened: the fast itself did not automatically produce feeling. In every case, either something intervening (chores, illness, exhaustion, distraction, disorder) filled the space that was supposed to open up, or the addition people are told to make (extra worship, tahajjud, tafsir) arrived too late in the month to register, or the person simply stopped believing the mechanism would work at all. None of the writers report the emptiness resolving through more willpower — the ones who do describe a shift describe a *change in what filled the time*, not gritting through harder.

## 3. Converts and reverts — most articulate on the mechanism

Because it isn't automatic for them, converts describe the "why" question directly instead of assuming it.

A writer who's been Muslim 30+ years, converting as an adult, frames her sustained practice not as habit but "grit" — she names it as the trait that separates converts who "make it and stay the course," and is explicit that Ramadan is "a traditionally lonely time for many converts" rather than the communal ideal often assumed — [Grace Song, "Why Be A Convert"](https://gracesong.substack.com/p/why-be-a-convert).

A Muslim writer's most-quoted line across her own worst and best Ramadans is a purpose statement, not a discipline statement: "when you know why you're doing something, it becomes easier." Her worst Ramadan (working full-time through a hot summer, breaking fast at 9pm surrounded by eating colleagues) she attributes explicitly to "lacking understanding of *why* she was fasting" — not to the physical difficulty — [Zeynab Mohamed, "A Love Letter to Ramadan"](https://culturevulture.substack.com/p/a-love-letter-to-ramadan).

The revert-in-lockdown account above found the opposite of what she expected: not despair at having no inherited traditions to lean on, but that the stripped-down version — "just me, my hunger, my thirst, and my Lord" — became "profoundly intimate" precisely because there was no cultural performance to hide inside.

## 4. Specific practices that recur — what people actually name

Beyond the theme-level pattern, here are the concrete, named practices, with source:

- **Quran completion on a schedule (khatm)** — a woman raced her father through the Qur'an via a shared spreadsheet, aiming to finish the full text by month's end — [sabrgirl.substack.com](https://sabrgirl.substack.com/p/ramadan-series-reflections).
- **Tahajjud / night prayer** — see §1 and §4 sources above; the single most-detailed practice account in this research.
- **Deleting or fasting from social media** — four independent accounts (Bustle, Vice, embracingthechaosblog, Andy Bachert), all crediting it with reclaimed time, reduced anxiety, or a specific spiritual moment.
- **Journaling / decluttering the schedule beforehand** — "decluttering my schedule, and my life really, in preparation for Ramadan allowed me to focus" — credited with revealing capacity for consistency the author didn't think she had — [roadtoihsan.substack.com](https://roadtoihsan.substack.com/p/ramadan-reflections-1).
- **A specific dua repeated deliberately** — "Allahumma imla' qalbee bihubbik" (fill my heart with Your love), repeated three times, credited with restoring a post-Ramadan spiritual low — [sabrgirl.substack.com](https://sabrgirl.substack.com/p/iman-the-ebbs-and-flows).
- **Fasting the tongue, not just food** — a recurring theme (not a first-person story but repeatedly invoked): a writer who "refuses to fast from food" as dogma instead names watching people fast from food while gossiping and mocking others as the thing that convinced him the food-only version misses the point entirely — [thefunkstop.substack.com](https://thefunkstop.substack.com/p/on-ramadan-and-why-i-refuse-to-fast).
- **Redirecting physical hunger toward a specific external referent** — a diarist redirects each hunger pang toward imagining Gaza's famine conditions, explicitly using discomfort "as a bridge to empathy" — [webzetetic.substack.com, Day 15](https://webzetetic.substack.com/p/my-ramadan-fast-day-15).
- **Sitting with discomfort rather than distracting from it** — the secular water-faster's only named "practice," and the one clearest counter-example to the addition thesis — [madhusavara.com](https://www.madhusavara.com/kitchen-blog/my-7-day-water-fast-experience-mental-clarity-inner-strength/).

## Sections that are thin

Iʿtikāf (last-ten-nights seclusion) turned up only as background description, never as a first-person account — flagged rather than papered over. Breaking fast with others vs. alone is attested as a *tension* (convert loneliness; one revert finding solitude unexpectedly intimate) but not as a controlled comparison from one person who tried both. Charity/sadaqah is heavily covered in prescriptive writing but no verified first-person "sadaqah specifically changed my fast" account surfaced — cut rather than invented. Reddit (r/islam, r/exmuslim, r/converts, r/intermittentfasting) could not be reached directly — the platform blocked this tool's fetches outright, including through several proxy front-ends tried — so despite being a priority source, no reddit.com content is quoted here; independently sourced blog, Substack, and Medium material covers the same ground instead.

## For the fast

Ordered by how often the accounts above actually credited each one — not by prescription:

1. **A clear "why" held in mind, not assumed** — named directly as the difference between the worst and best Ramadans in more than one account (Zeynab Mohamed; gentlyrooted's "it's not about you" reframe).
2. **Night prayer / tahajjud, kept small and irregular rather than performed at full intensity** — the single most-detailed and most consequential specific practice found, credited with sustained relational and emotional change, not just a peak feeling.
3. **Cutting social media / phone specifically** — four independent accounts, the most-replicated concrete practice in this research, consistently credited with reclaimed time and reduced anxiety even when not fully sustained afterward.
4. **Redirecting the hunger itself toward something outside yourself** (empathy for others suffering, gratitude, prayer) rather than just enduring it — named explicitly in at least three accounts as the mechanism, not the symptom.
5. **Moderation over intensity — a few habits done consistently, not total transformation attempted at once** — the burnout account's explicit lesson, echoed by the revert accounts describing added practices that arrived "too late and too thin."
6. **A specific, repeated dua or line held onto through low points**, rather than general "praying more."
7. **Reading Qur'an on a concrete, trackable schedule** (a khatm goal, a shared tracker) rather than "reading more when I can."
8. **Finding or naming community for the specific form of isolation you're in** — not generic community, but people in the same structural position (other reverts, other people fasting alone).
9. **Un-distracted stillness during the hardest hours, rather than gritting through them** — the weakest-attested but most interesting outlier: at least one account credits *not adding anything at all* — just refusing to fill the emptied space with a substitute — as the thing that produced clarity.
10. **Honest naming of what actually filled the time when the intended practice didn't happen** — the negative accounts converge less on "I didn't try hard enough" and more on "something else filled the space I meant to fill" (chores, illness, distraction) — suggesting the useful question isn't just "what did I add" but "what filled the gap by default, and did I notice."
