# Death + Transition Cluster: mwt, wfy, qbr, mlk, barzakh

## Headline Finding

The Quran's death vocabulary is strikingly minimalist and strategically vague about the in-between. Four roots — mwt (165 occ), wfy (66 occ), qbr (8 occ), mlk (206 occ) — form the full death-to-resurrection arc, and the word barzakh appears exactly once in an eschatological sense [23:100]. The Quran specifies: death happens, souls are taken by angels, bodies go to graves, and resurrection will occur. What fills the interval between grave and resurrection is almost entirely absent — that is hadith territory. The text's silence is structural, not accidental.

## 1. mwt — The Lemma Table and the Tasting-Death Formula

**Stats:** 165 occurrences, 8 lemmas, 143 ayahs, 53 surahs. POS: N 96, V 60, ADJ 9.

| Lemma (Arabic) | Gloss | Count | POS |
|---|---|---|---|
| مَوْت (mawt) | death — noun | 50 | N |
| مَّاتَ (māta) | he died — verb | 39 | V |
| مَّيِّت (mayyit) | dead — noun/adj | 38 | N/ADJ |
| أَمَاتَ (amāta) | He caused to die | 21 | V |
| مَيْتَة (maytah) | dead-thing / carrion | 6 | N/ADJ |
| مَّيْت (mayt) | dead (short form) | 5 | ADJ/N |
| مَمَات (mamāt) | death (abstract) | 3 | N |
| مَوْتَت (mawtat) | a dying | 3 | N |

**Observations.** The dominant lemma is *mawt* (the noun, 50×) — death named as object or state. The verb *māta* (39×) is second; causative *amāta* (21×) marks God as the one who causes death, not death as an autonomous event. *Mayyit* (38×) covers both the dying person and the dead body/person.

**The tasting-of-death formula.** The phrase "kullu nafsin dhāʾiqatu al-mawt" — every soul shall taste death — occurs in three places:

> **[3:185]** كُلُّ نَفْسٍ ذَآئِقَةُ الْمَوْتِۗ وَإِنَّمَا تُوَفَّوْنَ أُجُورَكُمْ يَوْمَ الْقِيَٰمَةِ
>
> Saheeh: "Every soul will taste death, and you will only be given your full compensation on the Day of Resurrection."
>
> Khattab: "Every soul will taste death. And you will only receive your full reward on the Day of Judgment."

Identically repeated in [21:35] and [29:57]. Three-fold, spread across early-Medinan and Meccan surahs.

**Why TASTING (dhwq)?** The root *dhwq* names sensory experience of a substance — what enters you and registers on the body. The Quran uses it for: tasting the Fire [3:106], tasting punishment [5:95], tasting the sweetness of faith [49:14]. Death is placed in this experiential category — not viewed from outside as biological termination, but felt from inside as an event that comes *through* the person. The formula insists on universality (every *nafs*, not every body) and on a phenomenological dimension. Two further notes: (a) the verb is feminine singular *dhāʾiqah* — because *nafs* is grammatically feminine; (b) in all three instances, the tasting-formula is immediately followed by a return-to-God statement ("thumma ilaynā turjaʿūn") or a compensation-statement, making death a threshold, not an endpoint.

**Negative absence.** The Quran uses *māta* and *mawt* for physical death but almost never specifies mechanism, location in the body, or the dying experience. The hour of death (when *mawt* actually arrives) is described in [23:99] — "until, when death comes to one of them, he says: Lord, send me back!" — but only from the perspective of the dying person's plea, not the mechanics of dying.

## 2. wfy from the Death Side (Cross-ref + Extension)

Full baseline: concept-wfy.md (in notes/teleportation/1-roots/) established that *tawaffā* (Form V, 24×) is the Quran's clinical death-verb — "to take to oneself in fullness." Sleep is partial-*tawaffā* ([39:42] — the locus classicus). Death is full-*tawaffā*.

**Extension from the death side.** Three patterns not fully unpacked in the teleportation file:

**a. Eschatological wfy.** At the eschaton, the root appears in its Form II direction: *yuwaffā kullu nafsin mā kasabat* — "every soul will be paid-in-full for what it earned" [2:281, 3:25, 3:185, 16:111, 39:70]. This is the closing bracket: *tawaffā* (Form V) at death = God *takes* the soul fully; *waffā* (Form II) at resurrection = God *pays* the deeds fully. The same root brackets the entire arc from death to judgment.

**b. Angels as agents at death.** [6:61]: *tawaffathu rusulunā* — "Our messengers took him." [7:37]: *rusulunā yatawaffawnahum* — "Our messengers take them in death." These verses call the death-agents *rusul* (messengers), not *malāʾika* (angels) — a naming that elevates their status as divine envoys rather than merely angel-workers.

**c. The soul's destination after wfy.** [32:11] ends with *thumma ilā rabbikum turjaʿūn* — "then to your Lord you will be returned." The Quran says where the soul goes: to God. It says nothing about what happens between that return and resurrection. The post-death intermediate state is a blank.

## 3. The Angel of Death — Single vs. Plural

This is the cluster's sharpest grammatical tension.

**The single angel:** [32:11] alone names *malak al-mawt* in the singular:

> **[32:11]** قُلْ يَتَوَفَّىٰكُم مَّلَكُ الْمَوْتِ الَّذِي وُكِّلَ بِكُمْ ثُمَّ إِلَىٰ رَبِّكُمْ تُرْجَعُونَ
>
> Saheeh: "Say: 'The angel of death will take you who has been entrusted with you. Then to your Lord you will be returned.'"
>
> Khattab: "Your soul will be taken by the Angel of Death, who is in charge of you. Then to your Lord you will all be returned."

The verb *wukkila* — "was entrusted / commissioned" — marks this as a delegated role. The angel of death is not autonomous; he executes a divine commission.

**The plural angels:** [16:28], [16:32] — *tatawaffāhumu al-malāʾika* (the angels take them). [4:97] — the wrongdoers: *tawaffāhumu al-malāʾikatu ẓālimī anfusihim*. [6:61] and [7:37] — *rusulunā* (Our messengers, plural). [8:50] — angels striking faces and backs. [6:93] — the angels with outstretched hands at the moment of death.

**The coherent picture.** Both framings coexist: one chief angel (*malak al-mawt*) is commissioned; a team of angels (*al-malāʾika*, *rusulunā*) carries out the collection. The Quran does not resolve this tension — tradition (naming the angel Azrāʾīl) is post-Quranic elaboration. The text asserts both singular responsibility and plural execution.

**The reception-differentiation pattern.** [16:28] vs [16:32] is the most striking angel-death contrast in the corpus: same verb (*tatawaffāhumu al-malāʾika*), opposite outcomes — the wrongdoers receive a rebuke; the pure receive salutation and invitation to paradise. The mechanism of soul-taking is identical; the moral state of the departing person determines the angels' reception.

## 4. The Grave (qbr) — Quranic Minimalism

**Stats:** 8 occurrences, 3 lemmas, 8 ayahs, 8 surahs. POS: N 7, V 1.

| Lemma | Gloss | Count | Location |
|---|---|---|---|
| قَبْر (qabr) | grave / pl. al-qubūr | 6 | 9:84, 22:7, 35:22, 60:13, 82:4, 100:9 |
| أَقْبَرَ (aqbara) | He provided a grave for him | 1 | 80:21 |
| مَقَابِر (maqābir) | graveyards (pl.) | 1 | 102:2 |

**The full inventory:**

- [9:84] — prohibition against praying over the grave of a hypocrite. The grave appears as a social-ritual site, not a spiritual location.
- [22:7] — "Allah will resurrect those in the graves" (yabʿathu man fī al-qubūr). The grave as container for those awaiting resurrection.
- [35:22] — "those in the graves" cannot hear. The grave signals irreversible departure from the living realm.
- [60:13] — disbelievers have despaired of the hereafter like the disbelievers have despaired of "the companions of the graves." The grave-dwellers as a class beyond reach.
- [80:21] — *thumma amātahu fa-aqbarahu* — "then He causes his death and provides him a grave." The causative *aqbara* is unique: God is the one who *makes burial possible* — an act of divine dignity, not just a human custom.
- [82:4] — *wa-idhā al-qubūru buʿthirat* — "when the graves are scattered/overturned." The apocalyptic moment of resurrection, the grave as holding-vessel that is broken open.
- [100:9] — *idhā buʿthira mā fī al-qubūr* — "when what is in the graves is scattered." Same eschatological frame.
- [102:2] — *ḥattā zurtumu al-maqābir* — "until you visit the graveyards." The competitive worldliness that distracts people "until" death.

**The structural finding.** The Quran treats the grave as a container and a waiting-place, never as a punishment-site. The torture-in-the-grave (*ʿadhāb al-qabr*) concept is entirely hadith-origin. Quranic qabr is architecturally neutral: God places you there [80:21]; you wait there [22:7, 82:4, 100:9]; you come out at resurrection. The moral drama happens before death and after resurrection — the grave is the pause between them.

## 5. al-Barzakh — The One Eschatological Verse

The word *barzakh* (barrier / partition) occurs 3 times:

> **[23:100]** وَمِن وَرَآئِهِم بَرۡزَخٌ إِلَىٰ يَوۡمِ يُبۡعَثُونَ
>
> Saheeh: "And behind them is a barrier until the Day they are resurrected."
>
> Khattab: "And there is a barrier behind them until the Day they are resurrected."

Context [23:99–100]: the dying person begs to return and do good; the reply is: "No — it is merely a word he is saying; and behind them is a *barzakh* until the day of resurrection." The *barzakh* is placed between the moment of death and the resurrection. It is described only by its function (it blocks return) and its duration (until the resurrection). The Quran says nothing about its content — no questioning, no reward, no punishment, no consciousness, no state.

**The spatial barzakh:** [25:53] and [55:20] use *barzakh* for the partition between fresh and salt water — a geographic/physical barrier. The eschatological *barzakh* in [23:100] borrows exactly this image: an impermeable divide. The transfer from cosmological to eschatological is a direct semantic extension — the same barrier that keeps two seas from mixing keeps the dead from re-entering life.

**Data-grounded finding.** The Quran produces exactly one verse on the in-between state between death and resurrection. The entire elaborated tradition of *barzakh* as a realm with experiences, rewards, punishments, questioning by Munkar and Nakīr — that is post-Quranic. The Quran's barzakh is a partition-word borrowed from geography, used once in the eschatological sense, and left undescribed.

## 6. mlk — Angels in the Death-to-Resurrection Process

**Stats:** 206 occurrences, 10 lemmas. Key division: *malak/malāʾika* (angel sense, ~88× noun), *mulk* (dominion/kingship, 48×), *malaka* (to own/possess, verb 44×), *malik* (king, 15×). The angel sense (*malak*) is the dominant noun form.

**Angel sense in the eschatological context:**

- **At death:** [6:93] — the angels with outstretched hands demanding souls from the dying unjust. [8:50] — angels striking faces and backs. [16:28], [16:32] — paired reception at death.
- **At the Throne-bearing:** [69:17] — eight angels carry the Throne on the Day of Resurrection. [39:75] — angels surrounding the Throne at judgment.
- **As guardians:** [6:61] — *ḥafaẓa* (guardian angels) sent over humans, replaced by death-taking *rusul* when death arrives.
- **Hell's keepers:** [74:30] — *ʿalayhā tisʿata ʿashar* — "over it [the Fire] are nineteen." Angels as wardens, not punishment-givers.
- **Prostration to Adam:** [2:30–34], [7:11] — the original angelic commissioning that frames all subsequent angel-human relations.

**The angel-of-death title.** *Malak al-mawt* appears only in [32:11]. The Arabic constructs the title as a possessive: "the angel *of* death" — the angel that belongs to/is defined by death. Elsewhere the Quran calls the same agents *rusulunā* (Our messengers) or *al-malāʾika* (the angels) — no consistent title, deliberately unspecified.

**Angel-kingship disambiguation.** The *mulk* lemma (48×) is formally and semantically distinct from *malak*: *mulk* = dominion, *malak* = angel. They share a root but operate in entirely separate semantic registers. The Quran never conflates them — contexts always disambiguate. The research risk is assuming an angel wherever *mlk* appears; the lemma table cuts this immediately.

## 7. The Death-to-Resurrection Arc: Specified vs. Hadith-Elaborated

**What the Quran specifies:**

1. Death arrives (*māta* / *jāʾa aḥadahum al-mawt*) — experienced from inside as a tasting.
2. Angels take the soul (*tawaffā* / *tawaffathu rusulunā*) — the death-operation.
3. The soul returns to God (*ilā rabbikum turjaʿūn*) — direction stated, state unstated.
4. Body placed in a grave (*fa-aqbarahu* / *man fī al-qubūr*) — the body's resting place named.
5. A *barzakh* blocks re-entry to the world — duration: until the resurrection.
6. Resurrection (*yabʿathu man fī al-qubūr*) — God raises those in the graves.

**What is NOT specified in the Quran:**

- The state of the soul between death and resurrection (conscious? asleep? present before God? awaiting?)
- What the *barzakh* is like from the inside
- Any questioning of the dead in the grave
- Any punishment or reward between death and the Final Judgment
- The name of the angel of death (Azrāʾīl is not Quranic)
- The number of angels involved in any individual death
- What happens to disbelievers' souls between death and hell

Every item in the second list is present in the hadith tradition. The Quran's arc is a skeleton; tradition supplies the flesh.

## 8. Negative Space

The Quran narrates a complete death-to-resurrection arc using five vocabulary clusters (mwt, wfy, qbr, barzakh, bʿv) and says almost nothing about the interval between. The *barzakh* (one eschatological verse), the grave's interior (never described), and the soul's intermediate state (direction only: "to your Lord") are the three major blanks. The minimal vocabulary is not poverty — it is a consistent Quranic pattern of naming the bookend events precisely (death: tawaffā; resurrection: baʿth) while leaving the middle structurally empty.

## 9. Verses for Direct Study

- **[32:11]** — *malak al-mawt* + *wukkila* + return-to-Lord. The single angel-of-death verse; the soul's destination formulated.
- **[39:42]** — sleep and death as dual operations of *tawaffā*. The Quran's most explicit mechanism statement.
- **[23:99–100]** — the dying person's plea + barzakh. The only text that positions the barzakh as a post-death barrier.
- **[16:28] and [16:32]** — paired angel-taking scenes. Same verb, opposite receptions. The Quran's most vivid death-judgment through the angels.
- **[80:19–21]** — the human being's creation sequence ending in *amātahu fa-aqbarahu*. God causes death and provides burial — divine dignity-of-burial formula.

## 10. Rabbit Holes

- **The three kullu nafsin dhāʾiqatu al-mawt occurrences.** Each has a different second clause: [3:185] — compensation on Judgment Day; [21:35] — testing with good and evil; [29:57] — return to Us. The formula is invariant; the trailing context changes. Map what the Quran wants the "tasting" claim to lead into each time — three different theological purposes for the same statement.
- **The *rusulunā* naming at death.** [6:61] and [7:37] call the death-taking angels *rusulunā* (Our messengers) rather than *malāʾika* (angels). Only [32:11] uses *malak al-mawt*. What does the *rusul*-framing add? Does it suggest a different class of being — or is it a deliberate reminder that death is a divine message, not a mechanical event?
- **The *aqbara* causative [80:21].** God "makes burial happen" — the causative form attributes burial itself to divine will, not human custom. This is the only Quranic verse where burial is framed as a divine act rather than a human duty. What does this imply for the theological status of the body after death?
- **Barzakh as cosmological-to-eschatological transfer.** The word appears in water-geography ([25:53], [55:20]) and then once in the death-context ([23:100]). The semantic transfer is striking — what happens linguistically when a spatial barrier becomes a temporal-eschatological barrier? The Quran makes this jump without explanation; mapping the conceptual load the word carries from each context illuminates the eschatological claim.
- **The 19 angels of hell [74:30].** *ʿAlayhā tisʿata ʿashar* — no names, no description, just a number. The next verses [74:31] explain the number is a trial for disbelievers and a confirmation for believers. Investigate why a number (not a description) is the chosen eschatological marker — and whether the 19 has any structural resonance with the 8 Throne-bearers [69:17].
