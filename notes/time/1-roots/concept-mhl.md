# مهل (mhl): Respite That Becomes Molten — Two Words Behind One Root

## Headline finding: the Quran's six occurrences of root mhl split cleanly into two non-overlapping vocabularies — *muhl* (molten metal, eschatological, 3×) and *mahhil/amhil* (give respite, prophetic-imperative, 3×) — and the two clusters never appear in the same surah, the same lemma family, or the same theological register

The root **mhl (م-ه-ل)** is the cleanest disambiguation case in the Quran's small-time vocabulary. Six total occurrences. Three of them are the noun *al-muhl* — molten brass / dross / boiling-tar — used three times for hellfire imagery, all in the formula *ka-l-muhl* ("like molten metal"). The other three are imperative verbs from a Form II / Form IV verb meaning "to grant time, give respite" — *mahhilhum*, *fa-mahhili*, *amhilhum*. **These are two distinct lexemes that happen to share consonants.**

But here is the analytical pivot: classical Arabic lexicographers (Ibn Manẓūr, al-Zamakhsharī) treat these not as two separate roots but as **one root with a metaphorical bridge**. The *muhl* meaning of "slow, viscous, drawn-out" extends naturally to "to do something slowly, to grant a slow passage of time." Molten metal is slow because it pours; respite is slow because it stretches a deadline. **The slowness is the bridge.** Whether the Quran intends this etymological unity is itself the question.

Three structural facts make this root distinctive:

1. **The respite-imperative formula** — *fa-mahhili al-kāfirīna amhilhum ruwaydan* ([86:17]) — packs **two different mhl-imperatives into a single ayah**. The Quran uses Form II (*mahhili*, "respite them") and Form IV (*amhilhum*, "grant them respite") in adjacent words. This is a hapax doubling.
2. **The molten-metal noun is identical across all three occurrences** — *ka-l-muhl* (ك-ٱلۡمُهۡل), with the same kāf-of-similitude, in three different eschatological scenes. This is **formulaic**, not free composition.
3. **All six occurrences are Meccan.** The respite/molten doublet is entirely a feature of the Meccan vocabulary. After the hijra, neither sense reappears. This is structurally significant: Meccan rhetoric needs both the threat of *muhl* and the prophetic *mahhil*; Medinan legislation needs neither.

## Stats

- **Occurrences:** 6
- **Lemmas:** 3
  - `muhol` (مُهْل) — the noun (molten / dross), N×3
  - `mah~ili` (مَهِّلِ) — Form II imperative ("respite them"), V×2
  - `>amohilo` (أَمْهِلْ) — Form IV imperative ("grant them respite"), V×1
- **Ayahs:** 5 (because [86:17] holds *both* *mahhili* and *amhilhum*)
- **Surahs:** 5 — Al-Kahf 18, Al-Dukhān 44, Al-Maʿārij 70, Al-Muzzammil 73, Al-Ṭāriq 86
- **Revelation:** Meccan ×6 (100%)
- **POS distribution:** N×3, V×3 (perfectly split)

This perfect split — three nouns, three verbs, three molten, three respite — is itself a compositional signature. The Quran uses the root in **paired symmetry**.

The disambiguation is morphological, not just contextual:

- **Cluster A (the noun)** is always *muhl*, always definite (*al-muhl*), always in the prepositional phrase *ka-l-muhl* (with the *kāf* of comparison). Same form, same vowel pattern, same surrounding grammar in all three occurrences.
- **Cluster B (the verbs)** are imperatives, addressed to the Prophet, with object suffix *-hum* ("them") referring to disbelievers. Same form, same morphological marking, same addressee, same object — across all three.

These are **two parallel formulaic systems** sharing consonants. The fact that they sit under the same root entry in a lexicon is a fact about Arabic morphology; the fact that they do completely different work in the Quran is a fact about Quranic rhetoric.

## Cluster A — the molten verses

### [18:29] — the drink of the wrongdoers in Sūrat al-Kahf

> ... وَإِن يَسْتَغِيثُوا۟ يُغَاثُوا۟ بِمَآءٍۢ **كَٱلْمُهْلِ** يَشْوِى ٱلْوُجُوهَ بِئْسَ ٱلشَّرَابُ وَسَآءَتْ مُرْتَفَقًۭا

> "...and if they call for relief, they will be relieved with water *like al-muhl*, which scalds the faces. Wretched is the drink, and evil the resting place."

The first molten use. Embedded in an extended parable: the *wrongdoers* (*al-ẓālimīn*) are surrounded by a fire whose walls (*surādiq*) enclose them; when they cry for water (*yastaghīṯū*), they are given *māʾun ka-l-muhl*. The verb *yashwī* ("scalds, roasts") makes the temperature explicit.

### [44:45] — the food of the sinful in Sūrat al-Dukhān

> **كَٱلْمُهْلِ** يَغْلِى فِى ٱلْبُطُونِ

> "*Like al-muhl*, it boils in the bellies."

The shortest, densest molten verse. Three Arabic words. *Al-muhl* is now in the *belly* (*buṭūn*), not yet drunk. The verb *yaghlī* ("boils") replaces *yashwī* — the heat is now internal, not external. Read with [44:43–46] for the full *zaqqūm*-tree feeding cycle.

### [70:8] — the sky in Sūrat al-Maʿārij

> يَوْمَ تَكُونُ ٱلسَّمَآءُ **كَٱلْمُهْلِ**

> "On the Day the sky shall be *like al-muhl*."

The third molten use. The simile has now scaled up from the drink ([18:29]) to the food in the belly ([44:45]) to **the entire sky** ([70:8]). The molten formula is the same; the referent expands cosmically.

### Translator divergence on *muhl*

| Verse | Saheeh | Pickthall | Khattab | Arberry |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 18:29 | "murky oil" | "molten lead" | "molten metal" | "molten copper" |
| 44:45 | "murky oil" | "molten brass" | "molten metal" | "molten copper" |
| 70:8 | "murky oil" | "molten copper" | "molten brass" | "molten copper" |

**Saheeh consistently reads *muhl* as "murky oil"** — following the Arabic lexicographical tradition that *muhl* is the dross / sediment of oil. **Pickthall, Khattab, Arberry all read it as molten metal**, but disagree on the metal: copper, brass, lead. Combined divergence at 44:45 is 0.7118 — extremely high. The translators cannot agree on whether *al-muhl* is a fluid (oil) or a metal (copper/brass/lead).

The classical lexica preserve both readings: *muhl* = (a) oil dregs or (b) molten metal. The Quran's repeated use of the simile (*ka-l-muhl*) without ever specifying the substance suggests the **vagueness is deliberate** — the point is the *viscous slowness and burning* shared by both substances, not the chemical identity.

## Cluster B — the respite verses

### [73:11] — the Muzzammil command

> وَذَرْنِى وَٱلْمُكَذِّبِينَ أُو۟لِى ٱلنَّعْمَةِ **وَمَهِّلْهُمْ** قَلِيلًۭا

> "And leave Me with the deniers, those of luxury, and *give them respite* a little."

Form II *mahhilhum* ("respite-them") + the qualifier *qalīlan* ("a little"). Allah is telling the Prophet to **let the deniers have a short while**. The respite is granted *to* the deniers, not *by* them.

### [86:17] — the Ṭāriq imperative pair (the load-bearing verse)

> **فَمَهِّلِ** ٱلْكَـٰفِرِينَ **أَمْهِلْهُمْ** رُوَيْدًۢا

> "So *respite* the disbelievers; *grant-respite-to-them* gently."

This is the only verse holding **both lemmas** of Cluster B. *Fa-mahhili* (Form II) + *amhilhum* (Form IV) in adjacent imperatives. The two morphological forms generate near-synonymous commands; their juxtaposition is **emphatic-paired**.

The qualifier *ruwaydan* (gently, slowly, a little while) intensifies the temporal slowness. The Prophet is told to **slow down, even in his prophetic urgency**, and to let the deniers have their interval.

This verse is also the one with the **highest divergence score** in the cluster (combined 0.7537). Translators handle the imperative-pair differently:

| Translator | Rendering |
|---|---|
| Saheeh | "So allow time for the disbelievers. Leave them awhile." |
| Pickthall | "So give a respite to the disbelievers. Deal thou gently with them for a while." |
| Khattab | "So bear with the disbelievers ˹O Prophet˺. Let them be for ˹just˺ a little while" |
| Arberry | "So respite the unbelievers; delay with them awhile." |

Notice **Khattab translates *fa-mahhili* as "bear with"** — relocating the verb from "give them time" to "be patient with them." This shifts the agent of waiting from the disbelievers (who are receiving time) to the Prophet (who is enduring them). The Arabic permits both readings; the English forces a choice.

## The poetic UNITY question

Are *muhl* (molten) and *mahhil* (respite) really one root or two?

### Arguments for unity (the lexicographers' view)

1. **Both involve slowness.** Molten metal pours slowly because of viscosity; respite extends a deadline slowly. *Mahl* in classical Arabic = "slowness, deliberation."
2. **Both involve heat or pressure.** Molten metal is hot; the respite extended to the *al-mukaddhibīn* is the "heat" of looming judgment slowly building. (This second is admittedly speculative.)
3. **Form II *mahhala* in classical Arabic** can mean "to draw out, to delay" — the same verb that drips a viscous fluid and the verb that extends a term.
4. **The Quran's own poetic logic.** Sūrat al-Ṭāriq ends [86:17] with *amhilhum ruwaydan*, then the next surah (al-Aʿlā) opens with the cosmological. Sūrat al-Maʿārij ([70:8]) describes the sky becoming *ka-l-muhl* on a Day — and the surrounding context is about the deniers being "delayed" before that Day arrives. **The same surah sequences both senses without flagging the transition.**

### Arguments for separation (the historical-linguistic view)

1. **Semitic comparative reconstruction:** *muhl* (molten substance) and *mahala* (to delay) appear to derive from distinct Proto-Semitic roots that converged in Arabic.
2. **The Quranic lemmas split cleanly.** No occurrence ambiguously bridges the two senses. They never share an ayah, a surah, or a syntactic frame.
3. **The morphological forms are different.** *Muhl* is a verbal noun (Form I); *mahhala/amhala* are derived stems (Form II/IV). The derived stems do not appear as nouns; the noun does not appear as a verb.

### The Quran's posture

The corpus does not adjudicate. **The Quran uses the consonant cluster m-h-l in two registers and lets the reader either unify them poetically or separate them lexically.** This is a recurring feature of small-time vocabulary in the Quran (compare [Esr](concept-Esr.md)'s pressing/whirlwind/cloud/age polysemy).

If we accept the unity reading, the rhetoric is striking: **respite is itself a form of slow, hot, dangerous fluid** — the disbelievers who are given time (*amhilhum ruwaydan*) are being filled with the same slow viscous substance that will eventually be served to them (*ka-l-muhl yashwī al-wujūh*). The respite is the *muhl*. The cup of grace is the cup of doom, drunk slowly. **This is istidrāj** — the doctrine of gradual luring — in lexical form.

## *Istidrāj* — respite as mercy or as trap

The respite-imperatives ([73:11], [86:17]) are addressed to the Prophet and concern the disbelievers. The theological doctrine they invoke is **istidrāj** — God *gradually drawing* the deniers toward their destruction by allowing them to enjoy the world a little longer.

Cross-reference texts on istidrāj include:
- **[7:182]** — *sa-nastadrijuhum min ḥaytḥu lā yaʿlamūn* ("We will gradually draw them in from where they do not know")
- **[68:44]** — same formula
- **[3:178]** — *innamā numlī lahum li-yazdādū ithman* ("We give them respite only that they may add to their sin")

The verb *numlī* in 3:178 is from a different root (m-l-w / m-l-y) but is **synonymous with *mahhala***. The Quran's two near-synonyms for "give respite" (*mahhala* / *amlā*) both carry the istidrāj implication. **Respite is never simply mercy in the Quran — it is always also a setup**.

This is the disambiguation's **theological payoff**. If *muhl* and *mahhil* are etymologically one, the lexical structure embeds the doctrine: the granting of slow time is itself the slow-pouring substance that will be drunk at the end. **Respite ages into the molten metal it always was.**

## Negative space

1. **No use of mhl in any Medinan surah.** The vocabulary is entirely Meccan. After the hijra, the Quran no longer needs either the molten threat or the respite-command.
2. **No verb-of-respite in third person** (*yumhilu* "He grants respite"). The verbs are exclusively imperatives. Allah does not narrate Himself granting respite — He commands the Prophet to grant it.
3. **No present indicative *yumhilu / yamhalu*.** Compare the rich morphological field for *ajjala* (stem-II of Ajl). *Mhl* is morphologically thin in the Quran.
4. **No nominal *muhla* ("a respite, an interval").** This common Arabic noun is absent. The respite is always verbal (an act commanded), never substantive (a thing held).
5. **No *muhl* in a non-eschatological context.** The molten metal never describes a worldly substance. Compare *zayt* (oil), *naḥās* (brass) — the Quran has other names for these in non-eschatological frames. *Muhl* is reserved.

## Rabbit holes

1. **The full *istidrāj* network.** Trace *imlāʾ* (m-l-w/y), *mahhala* (m-h-l), *istadraja* (d-r-j), and *naʿīm* (provision) across the corpus. Hypothesis: respite-vocabulary is the lexical infrastructure of the istidrāj doctrine, and the doctrine is more pervasive than the explicit *istadraja* formula suggests.
2. **The Cluster A → Cluster B compositional question.** Is the Quran exploiting consonant-cluster ambiguity deliberately when it places *amhilhum* near eschatological imagery (the very next surah after al-Ṭāriq is al-Aʿlā, with imagery of *ghuṯāʾan aḥwā* — "withered black [grass]")? Is the molten future implicit in the respite imperative?
3. **Why *qalīlan* / *ruwaydan* and not *ḥattā ḥīn*?** [73:11] uses *qalīlan* ("a little") and [86:17] uses *ruwaydan* ("gently/slowly"). The Quran's standard "for a while" formula (*ilā ḥīn*, *ḥattā ḥīn* — see [concept-Hyn](concept-Hyn.md)) is **not** used with *mahhala*. Why does the respite-imperative bypass the standard *ḥīn* formula? Hypothesis: *qalīlan* and *ruwaydan* are **affective qualifiers** ("a little, gently"), while *ḥīn* is **structural** ("until the unspecified end"). The respite-verb takes affective qualifiers because the imperative is to the *Prophet*, demanding patience, not naming a deadline.
4. **The kāf-of-similitude formulaic system.** *Ka-l-muhl* repeats three times. Build the full inventory of Quranic *ka-l-X* eschatological similes: *ka-l-jibāl* (like mountains), *ka-l-ʿihn* (like dyed wool), *ka-l-farāsh* (like scattered moths). Hypothesis: the *ka-l-X* formulaic register is the Quran's signature for cosmological-cataclysmic comparison.
5. **The Ibn Manẓūr question.** Read *Lisān al-ʿArab* on *m-h-l* and check whether classical lexicography unifies or splits the two senses. If unified, the unification is **pre-Quranic** and the Quran is exploiting an existing semantic bridge. If split, the unification is **post-Quranic** and the bridge was constructed by exegetes reading the Quran. Either way, the answer changes how we read the doublet.
6. **Cross-ref with [dhr](concept-dhr.md) and [Hyn](concept-Hyn.md).** All three roots are small (2, 35, 6 occurrences) and Meccan-heavy. Together they form the Quran's vocabulary for **non-fixed time**. *Dhr* is the refused fate-word; *ḥīn* is the indefinite-while-word; *mhl* is the slow-deliberate-respite-word. The trio handles every kind of time-that-is-not-an-*ajal-musamman*. Drawing the full division of labor among them is a structural project.
