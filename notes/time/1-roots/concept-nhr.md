# نهر (Nhr): The Day-and-River Root — Deep Concept Analysis

## Headline Finding: One Root, Two Concepts — and the Quran Splits Them Cleanly by Lemma

The root **nhr (ن-ه-ر)** carries a polysemy that no English translation preserves. Its 113 occurrences split into **three lemmas with completely different referents**:

- **`nahaAr` (نَهَار, "daytime, the light-phase of the cycle")** — 57 occurrences, 50 ayahs
- **`nahar` (نَهَر, plural `anhār`, "river, flowing water")** — 54 occurrences, 51 ayahs
- **`tanoharo` (تَنْهَرْ, verbal — "to repel, rebuke, drive away")** — 2 occurrences, 2 ayahs

These are not three meanings of one word. They are *three different concepts* sharing a triliteral root, and the Quran never confuses them. Of 113 occurrences, **the lemma alone disambiguates 100% of cases**: any verbal form is "rebuke," any singular masculine *nahar* / plural *anhār* is "river," any *nahār* (with the long ā in the second syllable) is "day." The user-visible problem is that the English word "river" and the English word "day" are utterly unrelated, while the Arabic root ties them together — through the underlying sense of *flowing, abundance, extension* (a river extends; the day extends; rebuking thrusts forth). Classical lexicography (Lane) traces all three to the notion of *abundant outflow*. The Quran, however, treats them lexically separately and never puns across them.

**The disambiguation table is mission-critical for any analysis of this root** — and it is the prerequisite for every claim in the rest of this file.

| Lemma | Buckwalter | Form | Count | Meaning | Co-occurrence pattern |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| `nahaAr` | نَهَار | masc. sg. noun, time-phase | 57 | the daytime phase | 42× with *layl* (night); part of cosmological-sign vocabulary |
| `nahar` | نَهَر / أَنْهَار | masc. sg. / pl. noun, water-channel | 54 | river, flowing channel | 22× with *xld* (eternal abiding); 34× in the *tajrī min taḥtihā al-anhār* formula (Paradise) |
| `tanoharo` | تَنْهَرْ | verb, jussive 2nd masc. sg. | 2 | "do not repel/rebuke" | Both negative imperatives ([17:23] parents; [93:10] petitioner) |

**Discount the polysemy and you mistakenly add ~50 "day" verses to a "river" analysis (or vice versa).** That mistake would corrupt every downstream claim about Quranic eschatology and Quranic cosmology simultaneously. This file therefore treats each lemma separately.

## Statistics and Morphological Reading

**Quantitative profile:**
- **113 total occurrences** across **102 ayahs** in **51 surahs**
- **3 lemmas**, each semantically distinct (see table above)
- **POS distribution**: 110 N, 2 V, 1 T (one time-adverb at [10:50])
- **Revelation**: balanced overall (57.5% Meccan per `structural-time-cooccurrence.md`), but the Meccan/Medinan split *differs by lemma*:
  - **nahār (day)**: heavily Meccan (it lives inside the cosmological-sign formulas, which are dominantly Meccan)
  - **anhār (rivers)**: heavily Medinan-leaning (it lives inside the Paradise-promise formulas, which appear with high concentration in Madinan legal/credal surahs)
  - **tanhar (rebuke)**: 1 Meccan (17:23) + 1 Meccan (93:10)

This split-by-lemma is itself a finding: the same Arabic root carries different revelation-period weights depending on which concept it names. A naïve aggregate Meccan/Medinan figure for "nhr" averages two unrelated distributions and produces a meaningless number.

## Lemma 1: نَهَار (nahaAr) — The Daytime, 57 Occurrences

### The Strongest Pairing in Quranic Time Vocabulary

**nahār + layl: 42 ayahs.** This is the highest co-occurrence figure of any pair among the 22 time-roots (lift = 31.70, far exceeding even ywm + Axr at 38). The pairing is so dominant that **only 8 of the 50 nahār-ayahs do NOT contain layl in the same ayah** — meaning daytime is, in over 80% of its appearances, named only as the contrastive partner of night.

The 42 co-occurrences fall into a small number of formulaic patterns. (For a fuller catalog, see `concept-lyl.md`; here I summarize.)

- **Pattern A — *ikhtilāf al-layli wa-l-nahār*** ("alternation of night and day"): [2:164], [3:190], [10:6], [23:80], [24:44], [45:5]. Always presented as a *sign* (āyah) for those who reason.
- **Pattern B — *yūliju al-layla fī al-nahār*** ("He causes night to enter day, and day to enter night"): [3:27], [22:61], [31:29], [35:13], [57:6]. The verb *awlaja* (to cause to enter, penetrate) frames the diurnal transition as an active divine insertion.
- **Pattern C — *yughshī al-layla al-nahār*** ("He covers the day with the night"): [7:54], [13:3]; cf. [36:37] (*naslakhu minhu al-nahār* — "We strip the day from it"); [39:5] (*yukawwiru al-layla ʿalā al-nahār* — "He wraps the night around the day"). The verbal field is **textile and surgical**: cover, wrap, strip, peel.
- **Pattern D — *jaʿala lakumu al-layla li-taskunū fīhi wa-l-nahāra mubṣiran*** ("He made for you the night to rest in and the day giving sight"): [10:67], [27:86], [40:61]. This is the only formula that explicitly assigns *human teleology* to the day.
- **Pattern E — Day-and-night as the frame of constant activity**: spending [2:274], glorifying [21:20], [41:38], guardianship [21:42], plotting [34:33], preaching [71:5]. The pair functions as a merism: *constantly, throughout the cycle, without exception*.
- **Pattern F — The two signs**: [17:12] — "We made the night and day **two signs** ... and made the sign of the day sight-giving (*mubṣirah*) that you may seek bounty from your Lord and know the number of the years and the reckoning." This is the only ayah that names a calendrical-economic *purpose* for the day.

### nahār Without layl — The 8 Independent Ayahs

These are the verses that show what nahār means when *not* contrasted with night. They are diagnostic.

- **[3:72]** — `ءَامِنُوا۟ بِٱلَّذِىٓ أُنزِلَ عَلَى ٱلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوا۟ وَجْهَ ٱلنَّهَارِ وَٱكْفُرُوٓا۟ ءَاخِرَهُۥ` — "Believe in what was revealed to the believers at the **beginning of the day** (*wajh al-nahār*) and disbelieve at its end" — a polemical quote of a faction's strategy. The phrase *wajh al-nahār* (literally "the face of the day") names morning. The Quran preserves the deceit by reporting the polemic in its enemies' own language.
- **[10:45]** — `كَأَن لَّمْ يَلْبَثُوٓا۟ إِلَّا سَاعَةً مِّنَ ٱلنَّهَارِ` — "as though they had not remained but **an hour of the day**" — the resurrected dead reflect on how brief their earthly life was.
- **[10:50]** — `إِنْ أَتَىٰكُمْ عَذَابُهُۥ بَيَٰتًا أَوْ نَهَارًا` — "if His punishment came to you **by night or by day**" — note the asymmetric pair: *bayātan* (a night-rest term, from *bāta*) for night, *nahāran* (the standard noun) for day.
- **[46:35]** — `كَأَنَّهُمْ يَوْمَ يَرَوْنَ مَا يُوعَدُونَ لَمْ يَلْبَثُوٓا۟ إِلَّا سَاعَةً مِّن نَّهَارٍ` — same hour-of-a-day metaphor, applied to the eschatological scene.
- **[73:7]** — `إِنَّ لَكَ فِى ٱلنَّهَارِ سَبْحًا طَوِيلًا` — "Indeed, for you in the **day** is prolonged occupation [*sabḥan ṭawīlan*]." Spoken to the Prophet immediately after the night-vigil command. The day is named here as the *competing claim* on the Prophet's time — busy, full of *sabḥ* (literally "swimming," metaphorically extended occupation).
- **[78:11]** — `وَجَعَلْنَا ٱلنَّهَارَ مَعَاشًا` — "And We made the **day** for livelihood." A perfect match for [78:10] ("And We made the night a garment"), but each in its own ayah.
- **[91:3]** — `وَٱلنَّهَارِ إِذَا جَلَّىٰهَا` — "By the day when it displays it [the sun]." Oath form, paired with [91:4] for night.
- **[92:2]** — `وَٱلنَّهَارِ إِذَا تَجَلَّىٰ` — "By the day when it appears in splendor." Oath form, opening Sūrat al-Layl.

These 8 verses are the **negative-test set**: they establish that nahār is *capable* of standing alone, and that when it does, it carries connotations of **labor, livelihood, manifestation, and the duration of life**. Crucially: when nahār stands without layl, the Quran does not describe the day as a *scene*; it uses the day to make *temporal-quantitative* arguments (an hour of the day, the labor of the day, the witness of the day).

### What the Day Is For

Synthesizing across the 50 nahār-ayahs:
- **Sight** (*mubṣiran*, [10:67], [17:12], [27:86], [40:61])
- **Livelihood** (*maʿāshan*, [78:11]; *sabḥan ṭawīlan*, [73:7])
- **Sovereignty as sign** (the entire cosmological-sign cluster)
- **Time-quantification** (*sāʿatan min al-nahār*, "an hour of the day," [10:45], [46:35])
- **Calendrical reckoning** (*ʿadad al-sinīna wa-l-ḥisāb*, [17:12])

What the day is NOT used for: combat, conquest, ritual prayer (those are bracketed by *night-edge* terms — *fajr*, *ṣubḥ*, *ʿasr*), love, dreaming, vision (visions in the Quran come at night).

## Lemma 2: نَهَر / أَنْهَار (nahar / anhār) — The River, 54 Occurrences

This is a **completely different concept** that happens to share the triliteral root. *Do not* count this in any "time" analysis.

### The Dominant Formula: Gardens Beneath Which Rivers Flow

**The phrase `جَنَّٰتٌ تَجْرِى مِن تَحْتِهَا ٱلْأَنْهَٰرُ` ("gardens beneath which rivers flow") appears verbatim or near-verbatim 34 times in the corpus** — making it one of the most repeated set-phrases in the entire Quran. Add 5 more for the related forms (*tajrī min taḥtihim al-anhār*, [7:43]; *tajrī taḥtahā* variants), and this single eschatological formula accounts for ~75% of all *anhār* (river) usages.

A representative sample:
- [2:25] — the founding occurrence in the corpus's longest surah, addressed to the believers at the very opening of the Quran's eschatological promise.
- [3:15], [3:136], [3:195], [3:198] — repeated four times in Āl ʿImrān alone.
- [4:13], [4:57], [4:122] — three times in Sūrat al-Nisā', tied to obedience and faith.
- [9:72], [9:89], [9:100] — three times in Sūrat al-Tawba.
- [22:14], [47:12], [48:5], [48:17], [57:12], [58:22], [61:12], [64:9], [65:11], [66:8], [85:11], [98:8] — distributed across the Madinan legal-credal surahs and the late Meccan moral surahs.

The formula is structurally **liturgical**. It functions as a fixed reward-clause completing the conditional structure: *those who do X → Y → gardens beneath which rivers flow*. The X is variable (believe, do righteous deeds, emigrate, fight, repent, support the Messenger, give alms, etc.); the Y is virtually invariant.

### Co-occurrence with خلد (xld, "abide eternally")

**nahr + xld = 22 ayahs** — the second-strongest co-occurrence for the entire nhr root (per the structural file). This is virtually always inside the Paradise formula: *tajrī min taḥtihā al-anhāru khālidīna fīhā* ("rivers flow beneath, they abiding eternally therein"). Eternity and rivers are lexically welded.

### The Four Rivers of [47:15]

The Quran's most physically detailed Paradise description:

> *مَّثَلُ ٱلْجَنَّةِ ٱلَّتِى وُعِدَ ٱلْمُتَّقُونَ ۖ فِيهَآ أَنْهَٰرٌ مِّن مَّآءٍ غَيْرِ ءَاسِنٍ وَأَنْهَٰرٌ مِّن لَّبَنٍ لَّمْ يَتَغَيَّرْ طَعْمُهُۥ وَأَنْهَٰرٌ مِّنْ خَمْرٍ لَّذَّةٍ لِّلشَّٰرِبِينَ وَأَنْهَٰرٌ مِّنْ عَسَلٍ مُّصَفًّى*

"In it are rivers of water unstaling, and rivers of milk whose taste does not change, and rivers of wine delicious to the drinkers, and rivers of purified honey." The plural *anhār* is repeated four times in a single sentence — the only place in the corpus where rivers are lexically *enumerated* into types.

### River as Sign of Creation

A small set of nahar-ayahs use the word in its *terrestrial* sense — actual rivers, not eschatological:
- **[13:3]** — `وَهُوَ ٱلَّذِى مَدَّ ٱلْأَرْضَ وَجَعَلَ فِيهَا رَوَٰسِىَ وَأَنْهَٰرًا` — "He spread the earth, placed therein firm mountains and rivers." A creation-sign verse, where rivers are listed alongside mountains. Notable: this same verse contains the day/night formula (*yughshī al-layla al-nahār*) — making [13:3] **the only ayah in the entire Quran that uses two different lemmas of the nhr root** (river + day, in the same sentence). It is a near-miss for a pun, and the Quran avoids actually executing one.
- **[14:32]**, **[16:15]**, **[27:61]** — God "subjected the rivers for you" / "made rivers and roads."
- **[2:74]** — Rivers burst forth from rocks (a moral analogy about hard hearts).
- **[2:249]** — The river by which Saul tested his troops (the only narrative-geographical river in the Quran, sometimes identified with the Jordan).
- **[18:33]** — A river running between two gardens in the parable of the two men.
- **[20:76]** — Gardens of ʿAdn, beneath which rivers flow (the formula again, but in a Pharaonic-context surah).
- **[43:51]** — Pharaoh boasting: *وَهَٰذِهِ ٱلْأَنْهَٰرُ تَجْرِى مِن تَحْتِىٓ* ("and these rivers flow beneath me!"). The Quran inverts the Paradise formula for ironic effect — Pharaoh's claim mimics divine reward language and is therefore exposed as blasphemous parody.
- **[54:54]** — `إِنَّ ٱلْمُتَّقِينَ فِى جَنَّٰتٍ وَنَهَرٍ` — "Indeed the righteous shall be in gardens and a river." The unusual *singular* nahar (not plural anhār) appears here, in the most compressed eschatological formula in the corpus.

### Not Investigated Here — Flag for Future Work

The Paradise-rivers formula deserves its own concept analysis: the typology of named rivers (water, milk, wine, honey), the relationship to the *jannāt* (gardens) vocabulary (around 147 occurrences), the cross-cultural parallels (the four rivers of Eden in Genesis 2; the Mesopotamian rivers; Hesiod's rivers of paradise), the ritual reception in Islamic eschatology and architecture (the *chahār-bāgh*, the four-quartered Persian and Mughal garden literally built on the Paradise-rivers schema), and the relationship to the terrestrial-river ayahs. This file intentionally **flags the Paradise-rivers complex without deep-diving**, in keeping with the disambiguation focus.

## Lemma 3: تَنْهَرْ (tanharo) — Do Not Repel, 2 Occurrences

The verbal root, in **both occurrences a negative imperative addressed to the believer concerning vulnerable persons**:

- **[17:23]** — `إِمَّا يَبْلُغَنَّ عِندَكَ ٱلْكِبَرَ أَحَدُهُمَآ أَوْ كِلَاهُمَا فَلَا تَقُل لَّهُمَآ أُفٍّ وَلَا تَنْهَرْهُمَا وَقُل لَّهُمَا قَوْلًا كَرِيمًا` — "If one or both of them [your parents] reaches old age with you, do not say to them 'uff', and do not **rebuke/repel** them (*lā tanharhumā*), but speak to them an honoring word."
- **[93:10]** — `وَأَمَّا ٱلسَّآئِلَ فَلَا تَنْهَرْ` — "And as for the petitioner, do not **repel** [him]."

These two occurrences are tonally identical and structurally parallel: both occur in a triplet of negative commands about the treatment of socially weak people (aging parents; beggars and questioners). They are a separate semantic island. The connection to the river/day senses, if any, is the underlying notion of *thrusting forth* — but the Quran does not exploit this; it uses the verb purely in its rebuke/repel sense.

## Translation Divergence

The disambiguation is so clean that translators do not disagree about *which* sense is being used in which verse. Disagreement instead concentrates on:

| Issue | Notes |
|---|---|
| *anhār* as "rivers" vs "streams" | Pickthall consistently uses "rivers"; Saheeh uses "rivers"; Khattab uses "rivers"; Arberry uses "rivers." Functional consensus. |
| *tajrī min taḥtihā al-anhār* — "beneath which rivers flow" vs "through which rivers flow" | The Arabic *taḥt* is "beneath, under." All major translators preserve "beneath" — but the architectural reading (gardens *raised above* flowing channels) is decisively English-translation-driven. The Persian/Mughal architectural tradition reads the Arabic differently. |
| *nahār* in *ikhtilāf al-layli wa-l-nahār* — "alternation of night and day" vs "difference of night and day" | Saheeh, Khattab use "alternation"; Pickthall uses "difference"; Arberry uses "alternation." The Arabic *ikhtilāf* carries both — *cycling between* and *contrasting with*. |
| *mubṣiran* (day "giving sight") vs "bright" | Saheeh "giving sight," Pickthall "sight-giving," Khattab "bright," Arberry "to see." The Arabic *mubṣiran* is the active participle of *abṣara* — literally "causing to see." Khattab's "bright" loses the agency ascribed to the day; the others preserve it. |
| *sabḥan ṭawīlan* at [73:7] — "prolonged occupation" / "long swim" / "long business" | Saheeh "prolonged occupation," Arberry "long business," Pickthall "long employ," Khattab "many tasks." The literal sense is "long swimming/floating" — a vivid metaphor for the day's extended pull on attention. All translators flatten this. |

The day-sense translations are fairly stable; the river-sense translations are extremely stable. The verbal sense (*lā tanhar*) is more contested at [93:10]: Saheeh and Pickthall use "repel," Khattab uses "drive away," Arberry uses "scold." The verb's exact tone (rebuke? push away? snap at?) is the locus of disagreement.

## Negative Space — What the Quran Does Not Say About Daytime

Comparing nahār to its near-cosmic-pair *layl*, several absences stand out:

1. **No subdivision of the day into hours.** The Quran has no equivalent of the Babylonian/Greek hour-system, no *prīma/tertia/sexta/nona* of the canonical hours. *Sāʿah* (hour) appears 48 times but **almost always means "the [eschatological] Hour"** — only in [10:45], [10:50], [46:35] does it carry the sense of "an hour of the day." The Quran has no name for noon, midday, or afternoon as distinct phases. (It has *ṣubḥ* and *fajr* for morning, *ʿasr* for "afternoon" or "the period," but these are *time-edge* words, not subdivisions of the daytime body.)

2. **No celebration of light.** Despite *nūr* (light) being a major theological concept (43 occurrences, Sūrat al-Nūr's titular metaphor), the day is not the locus of light-discourse. *Light* in the Quran is *moral and revelatory*; *day* is *practical*. The two vocabularies are kept apart.

3. **No "summer day" vs "winter day."** Despite Sūrat Quraysh referencing the *riḥlat al-shitāʾ wa-l-ṣayf* (winter and summer journeys), nahār is never seasonally qualified. The day is generic.

4. **No "the day of one's birth," "the day of one's wedding," etc.** These life-cycle days are named only in the eschatological-counterpoint contexts (Yaḥyā's *yawma wulida wa-yawma yamūtu wa-yawma yubʿathu ḥayyan*, [19:15] — but using *yawm*, not *nahār*). The split between *yawm* (day-as-event) and *nahār* (day-as-time-of-light) is rigorously maintained.

5. **The missing daytime-word — or: how the Quran distributes "day."** The English word "day" maps onto **at least three Quranic concepts**: (a) *yawm* — day-as-event, day-as-period (405 occurrences, eschatologically loaded); (b) *nahār* — daytime as the light-half of the cycle (57 occurrences, cosmologically paired with night); (c) *ayyām* (plural of yawm) — counted days, often in ritual or historical contexts. **The Quran maintains this three-way split with extreme consistency.** Verses about the Day of Judgment use *yawm* (never *nahār*); verses about the alternation of day and night use *nahār* (never *yawm*); verses about counted ritual days use *ayyām* (never *anhār*-style plurals of *nahār*).

6. **No vocabulary for noon-sun blindness, heat, mirage of midday.** This is striking given the Arabian context. The Quran does have a sun/heat vocabulary in eschatology (the *jahannam* heat-imagery), but the *daytime* sun's brightness is not described as a hardship, only as a benefit (*ḍiyāʾ*, "illumination," at [10:5]).

## The Diurnal-Cycle Cross-Finding (See Also `concept-lyl.md`)

**lyl + nahār is the strongest pairing in the Quran's time vocabulary** (42 ayahs, lift = 31.70 — the highest in the corpus per `notes/time/structural-time-cooccurrence.md`). This pairing is the primary cosmological-sign argument: **the alternation of night and day is offered as evidence of divine sovereignty for those who reason / understand / are mindful / believe / hear**.

A research finding worth recording in both files: **the Quran's diurnal vocabulary (layl, nahār) is overwhelmingly cyclical, while its eschatological vocabulary (yawm, ajal, ākhir) is overwhelmingly linear.** The same corpus carries both temporalities without contradiction. The cyclical names the *recurring frame within which history happens*; the linear names the *terminal toward which history moves*. The Quran does not collapse the two.

A second cross-finding: **the same corpus that names rivers (*anhār*) as the load-bearing image of eschatological reward also names daytime (*nahār*) as the load-bearing partner of night in cosmological argument** — using the same triliteral root for both. The Quran exploits the root system's ability to host unrelated concepts under one consonantal skeleton, but never *puns* across the polysemy. [13:3] is the closest near-miss: a single ayah where rivers and daytime appear together, and yet the Quran neither comments on the coincidence nor uses it for rhetorical effect.

## Rabbit Holes

1. **The Paradise-rivers complex.** *anhār* + *jannāt* + *taḥt* + *xld* form a tight quartet repeated 30+ times. Build a complete inventory of every Paradise formula across the corpus, classify by what conditions trigger the reward, and compare to the Genesis 2 four-rivers description, the Mesopotamian *abzu* groundwater, and the Persian/Mughal architectural traditions. This is its own multi-week concept investigation.

2. **The four named Paradise-substances.** Water (*māʾ ghayr āsin* — "unstaling"), milk (*laban lam yataghayyar ṭaʿmuhu* — "whose taste does not change"), wine (*khamr ladhdha li-l-shāribīn* — "delicious to drinkers"), honey (*ʿasal muṣaffā* — "purified"). Each substance is given a distinct quality clause. Why these four? Compare to known West Asian and Greco-Roman paradise typologies. The wine-in-paradise vs wine-prohibition tension on earth is itself a research question.

3. **Pharaoh's blasphemous mimicry.** [43:51] is a remarkable inversion. Pharaoh boasts of "rivers flowing beneath me" — using the exact eschatological formula as a claim about *worldly* sovereignty. The Quran uses this to expose his pretension. Is this the only inversion of a Paradise formula in the corpus? Other candidates: the *jannāt* of the unbeliever's parable in [18:32–43]; the *jannāt* in [68:17–33] (the people of the garden who fail to say *in-shāʾa-Allāh*).

4. **The day's "swim" metaphor (*sabḥan ṭawīlan*) at [73:7].** The verb *sabaḥa* (to swim, to float) is used elsewhere in the Quran for celestial bodies in their orbits ([21:33], [36:40]: *kullun fī falakin yasbaḥūn*). Why is the same verb used for the *day's call on attention*? Hypothesis: the Quran offers a continuous metaphor of *swimming in time* — celestial bodies, the day, the soul — as a unified image. Worth a multi-root investigation.

5. **The lemma-shared near-miss at [13:3].** Why does the Quran place rivers and the day/night cycle in the same ayah without exploiting the lexical pun? Compare to how other roots (*dīn* as religion vs debt; *ẓulumāt* as darkness vs oppression) handle their polysemies — does the Quran systematically *suppress* puns, or does it sometimes activate them?

6. **The river as *sign* (āyah) vs the day as *sign* (āyah).** Both senses of nhr appear in cosmological-sign formulas — daytime in [2:164], [13:3], [17:12]; rivers in [13:3] (alongside mountains). What does it mean that the same root can produce *signs* under both lemmas? Is there a deep semantic continuity (both are "outflowings" of divine bounty) or is the convergence accidental?

7. **The *tanhar* / vulnerability link.** Both occurrences address the elderly parent and the asking petitioner. These are flagged as the **socially weakest** in the believer's immediate environment. Combined with [89:17–20], [90:13–17], [107:1–7] (other "do not repel/refuse" passages, though using different vocabulary), there is a Quranic ethical pattern around *non-repulsion of the vulnerable* worth a dedicated investigation. The verbal lemma of the river/day root sits inside this ethical cluster — a striking lexical accident, or another suppressed pun.

8. **Why is *anhār* heavily Medinan-leaning?** The Paradise formula is not chronologically distributed evenly. Many of its repetitions are in Medinan legal-credal surahs (Nisāʾ, Māʾida, Tawba, Muḥammad, Fatḥ, Ḥadīd, Mujādila, Ṣaff, Munāfiqūn, Taghābun, Ṭalāq, Tahrīm, Bayyina). Hypothesis: in the Medinan period, the conditional structure becomes more legal (do this specific deed → receive this specific reward), and the rivers-formula functions as the standardized reward-clause completing those conditional contracts. By contrast, the Meccan period builds reward language around fire/garden contrasts more diffusely.
