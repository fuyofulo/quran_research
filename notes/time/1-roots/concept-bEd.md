# Concept Analysis: bEd (بعد) — "After / Far / Distant"

**Root:** bEd (بعد) | **Occurrences:** 235 | **Ayahs:** 223 | **Surahs:** 57 | **Lemmas:** 6
**Distribution:** Meccan 130 / Medinan 105 (close to even, with strong Medinan density in legal surahs)

---

## Headline Finding

The root bEd encodes a single concept refracted across two axes: **temporal distance** ("after") and **spatial distance** ("far"). Both share the deep semantic of *separation from a reference point*. Where qbl ("before") is asymmetric — naming only the prior — bEd is symmetric: it names both the *posterior in time* and the *remote in space*, and the Quran exploits this overlap to fuse moral, geographic, and temporal distance into one metaphor.

The headline pattern: **bEd is the Quran's "wreckage clock."** The most common construction (*min baʿdi mā…* — "after that which…") almost always introduces an act of breakdown, betrayal, or relapse: covenant broken *after its ratification*; calf worshipped *after Moses brought clear proofs*; disbelief *after belief*; truth concealed *after it was made clear*. The temporal "after" is the Quran's preferred slot for moral failure. **What happens after a clear sign is, by default, a falling-away.**

Conversely, the *spatial* sense (*baʿīd*, "far") loads moral and metaphysical weight: the wrongdoers are in *ḍalāl baʿīd* ("far error"), the disbelievers are called *min makānin baʿīd* ("from a faraway place"), Paradise is brought near *ghayra baʿīd* ("not far"). **Distance is itself an indictment.** The far-thing is the cursed thing. Closeness is grace; distance is loss.

The two axes meet in 11:89 — *wa-mā qawmu Lūṭin minkum bi-baʿīd* — "the people of Lot are not from you far away." This is *temporal-spatial unity in one clause:* they are not far from you in time (history) nor in space (geography). One word is doing two jobs that English splits.

**Key observation:** 199 of 235 occurrences (85%) are the bare adverbial noun *baʿd*. The minor lemmas split into two clusters: **distance-words** (baʿīd, buʿd, baʿudat, mubʿadūn, bāʿid = 36 occurrences) all encoding spatial/moral remoteness. **There is no Form V "to delay" verb** corresponding to qbl's taqabbala (acceptance). The bEd-acceptance-equivalent doesn't exist; the negative sense (delay/distance) cannot be made positive in this root. **Compare this to qbl: qbl has positive verbal forms (taqabbala = accept); bEd has only negative ones (bāʿid = make farther, an ingratitude).** This asymmetry is structural: "before" can be embraced; "after / far" cannot.

## Formula 1 — *min baʿdi mā…* — The "After-That-Which" Sequence of Failure

The single most distinctive bEd-construction is **min baʿdi mā** + clause: "after that which [event]." Almost every instance introduces a *moral breakdown that follows a clarification*.

### The pattern

**[2:27]** — Covenant broken after ratification:
> ٱلَّذِينَ يَنقُضُونَ عَهۡدَ ٱللَّهِ مِنۢ بَعۡدِ مِيثَٰقِهِ
> "Those who break the covenant of Allah after ratifying it…"

**[2:74]** — Hearts hardened after the sign:
> ثُمَّ قَسَتۡ قُلُوبُكُم مِّنۢ بَعۡدِ ذَٰلِكَ
> "Then your hearts hardened after that…"

**[2:92]** — Calf worshipped after clear proofs:
> ثُمَّ ٱتَّخَذۡتُمُ ٱلۡعِجۡلَ مِنۢ بَعۡدِهِ
> "Then you took the calf [in worship] after him [Moses]."

**[2:109]** — Apostasy attempted after belief:
> لَوۡ يَرُدُّونَكُم مِّنۢ بَعۡدِ إِيمَٰنِكُمۡ كُفَّارًا
> "[They] wished they could turn you back to disbelief after you have believed."

**[2:159]** — Concealment after revelation:
> ٱلَّذِينَ يَكۡتُمُونَ مَآ أَنزَلۡنَا مِنَ ٱلۡبَيِّنَٰتِ مِنۢ بَعۡدِ مَا بَيَّنَّٰهُ
> "Those who conceal what We revealed of clear proofs after We made it clear…"

**[3:86]** — Disbelief after testimony:
> كَيۡفَ يَهۡدِي ٱللَّهُ قَوۡمࣰ ا كَفَرُواْ بَعۡدَ إِيمَٰنِهِمۡ
> "How shall Allah guide a people who disbelieved after their belief…?"

### What this pattern reveals

The *min baʿdi mā* slot is structurally reserved for **the second move in a two-move moral sequence: clarification, then collapse.** In every instance the construction is:

> [some clear divine event: covenant, proof, sign, belief, revelation] → *min baʿdi…* → [a defection from it]

This is not narrative reporting — it is *grammatical accusation*. To say "after the proof came" is already to imply "and yet they failed." The temporal *baʿd* carries an *ought*: after-the-proof should be *acceptance-of-the-proof*; the verse only mentions *baʿd* when this expectation has been violated. **The temporal preposition has prosecutorial force.**

This is one of the Quran's most efficient rhetorical structures: **without saying "they failed," the verse uses *baʿd* to imply that what came next *was a failure of what should have come next*.** The translation suffers here — English "after" is morally neutral.

### The repentance-after counter-formula

The same *min baʿdi* construction is also used in a redemptive register, and the symmetry is part of the design.

**[3:89]:**
> إِلَّا ٱلَّذِينَ تَابُواْ مِنۢ بَعۡدِ ذَٰلِكَ وَأَصۡلَحُواْ
> "Except those who repent after that and correct themselves."

**[5:39]:**
> فَمَن تَابَ مِنۢ بَعۡدِ ظُلۡمِهِۦ وَأَصۡلَحَ
> "But whoever repents after his wrongdoing and reforms…"

**[7:153]:**
> ثُمَّ تَابُواْ مِنۢ بَعۡدِهَا وَءَامَنُوٓاْ إِنَّ رَبَّكَ مِنۢ بَعۡدِهَا لَغَفُورࣱ رَّحِيمࣱ‏
> "Then repented after them and believed — indeed your Lord, *thereafter*, is Forgiving and Merciful."

**[16:110]:**
> لِلَّذِينَ هَاجَرُواْ مِنۢ بَعۡدِ مَا فُتِنُواْ ثُمَّ جَٰهَدُواْ
> "For those who emigrated after they had been compelled, then struggled…"

**The *min baʿdi* slot can house either accusation (after-the-proof-they-failed) or redemption (after-the-failure-they-repented).** The grammar is neutral; the moral charge depends on what fills the after-slot. **The Quran's "after" is the slot of decision.** It is not a passive temporal marker; it is the place where the moral test happens.

7:153 is exceptional: it places *min baʿdihā* twice in one sentence — "they repented thereafter, and your Lord, thereafter, is Forgiving." The repetition of "after" amplifies *both* the human turning *and* the divine answering. **The structure encodes asymmetric mercy: a single moral pivot triggers a double divine response.**

## Formula 2 — Inheritance Law: "min baʿdi waṣiyyatin yūṣī bihā aw daynin"

A distinctive legal-procedural use of *min baʿdi* dominates Surah 4 (An-Nisaa). In the inheritance verses, the formula **"after any bequest he may have made or debt"** repeats nearly verbatim:

**[4:11]:** *min baʿdi waṣiyyatin yūṣī bihā aw daynin*
**[4:12]:** *min baʿdi waṣiyyatin yūṣīna bihā aw daynin* (twice)

This is the Quran's most procedural use of bEd: it specifies the *temporal-legal order of operations*. Inheritance happens *after* debts and bequests. The "after" here is not moral but algorithmic — a procedural clock. The same root that prosecutes apostasy here governs probate.

**The juxtaposition matters:** the same word that names the moment of failure (after the proof) names the moment of legal cleanup (after the debts). **The Quran's "after" works at two registers — courtroom-moral and courtroom-procedural — without distinguishing them lexically.** This is the legal mind of the Medinan period showing through: time is itself a regulator.

## Formula 3 — *baʿīd*: Distance as Moral Verdict

The 25 occurrences of *baʿīd* (far/distant) fall into three semantic clusters:

### (a) *Ḍalāl baʿīd* — "far error" (frozen formula)

**[14:3], [14:18], [22:12], [34:8], [42:18], etc.** — A repeating formula: *fī ḍalālin baʿīd* ("in extreme/far error"). Examples:

**[14:3]:** *ūlāʾika fī ḍalālin baʿīd* — "those are in far error"
**[42:18]:** *al-ladhīna yumārūna fī al-sāʿati la-fī ḍalālin baʿīd* — "those who dispute about the Hour are in far error"

**The translators converge on "extreme" or "far"** — but the Arabic is doing something subtler: **error is being *distanced* from truth.** Not "great" error, but error *at a distance*. The wrongdoer has *traveled* from truth. Distance is the metric of error.

### (b) *Min makānin baʿīd* — "from a faraway place"

**[34:52]:** *wa annā lahum al-tanāwush min makānin baʿīd* — "How can they reach [faith] from a place so far away?" (after death)
**[41:44]:** *yunādawna min makānin baʿīd* — "Those are being called from a distant place" (the disbelievers cannot hear the message)

**The "faraway place" is metaphysical-spatial:** it names the disbeliever's position relative to truth as *physical distance from the speaker*. This is the deep grammar of bEd — distance is *categorical exclusion*. The disbeliever isn't merely wrong; they are *too far away to be reached*.

### (c) *Ghayra baʿīd* — "not far" (the redemption-distance)

**[11:83]:** *wa-mā hiya min al-ẓālimīn bi-baʿīd* — "And these stones are not far from the wrongdoers" (the punishment is near)
**[11:89]:** *wa-mā qawmu Lūṭin minkum bi-baʿīd* — "The people of Lot are not from you far away"
**[50:31]:** *wa-uzlifati al-jannatu li-l-muttaqīna ghayra baʿīd* — "Paradise will be brought near to the righteous, not far"

**The negation is the redemption.** When bEd is negated, the relation reverses: closeness becomes available. The structure *not-far* is itself a pedagogy: the punishment that comes to others is *not far from you*; the paradise that seems remote is *not far*; the destroyed peoples are *not far*. **The Quran's preferred mode is to invoke distance and then collapse it.** The threat works by making the far suddenly near.

11:89's *minkum bi-baʿīd* is the single most striking case: the people of Lot — a destroyed community — are temporally and geographically distant. The verse asserts they are *not far*. **Their moral fate is not far.** Time and space collapse: ancient Sodom is here, now, available as warning.

## Formula 4 — *buʿd* — The Imprecation: "Away With…!"

The verbal noun *buʿd* appears 7 times. Six of them are in the same imprecative construction: **"buʿdan li-[people]"** = "May they be far!" / "Away with them!" Used at the end of destruction-narratives.

**[11:44], [11:60], [11:68], [11:95], [23:41], [23:44]:**
> فَبُعۡدًا لِّقَوۡمِ نُوحٍ / فَبُعۡدًا لِّعَادٍ / فَبُعۡدًا لِّثَمُودَ / فَبُعۡدًا لِّمَدۡيَنَ
> "So away with [the people of Noah / 'Aad / Thamud / Madyan]!"

**The imprecation literally wishes distance upon the destroyed.** Not "may they perish" or "they were doomed," but *away with them* — may they be *far*. **The bEd-root is doing the curse-work.** Destruction is rendered as *increased distance from God*. Hellish exclusion is, lexically, just being *far*. Heaven (in 21:101, *mubʿadūn*) is the inverse: the righteous are kept *far from Hell*. **Salvation and damnation are both phrased as forms of distance — but in opposite directions.**

The seventh *buʿd* is **[43:38]:**
> يَٰلَيۡتَ بَيۡنِي وَبَيۡنَكَ بُعۡدَ ٱلۡمَشۡرِقَيۡنِ فَبِئۡسَ ٱلۡقَرِينُ
> "Oh, I wish there was between me and you the distance of the two horizons — how wretched a companion!"

The damned addressing his Satan-companion on Judgment Day. *buʿd al-mashriqayni* — "the distance of the two easts" — is hyperbolic: the maximum imaginable separation. **The damnation-wish is *distance* — separate me from you by every available unit of spatial distance.**

## Formula 5 — The "Make Distant" Verb (bāʿid, baʿudat) — Distance as Sin

Only three verbal occurrences exist: *baʿudat* (it-became-distant) twice, and *bāʿid* (Form III imperative — "make distant!") once. The single Form III imperative is **morally indicting**.

**[34:19]:** *fa-qālū rabbanā bāʿid bayna asfārinā* — "But they said, 'Our Lord, lengthen the distance between our journeys!' and they wronged themselves…"

The people of Sheba prayed for *longer journeys*. Their prosperity (close, easy travel between fertile towns) annoyed them; they wanted *more distance*. God answered with destruction. **The single Form III "make distant" verb in the Quran is an act of ingratitude that triggers ruin.**

**To pray for distance is sin.** The same root that names the closeness of Paradise (*ghayra baʿīd*) names the request for distance that destroys Sheba. The semantic load: closeness is gift; the desire for distance is rebellion.

The two *baʿudat* occurrences:

**[9:42]:** The hypocrites would have followed you, *but the distance was too far for them* (*baʿudat ʿalayhim al-shuqqa*). The journey to fight at Tabuk seemed too long. Distance becomes their excuse for dereliction.

**[11:95]:** *baʿidat Thamūdu kamā baʿidat Madyan* — "Thamud was sent far, as Madyan was sent far." Destruction phrased as being-made-distant.

## Formula 6 — "wa min baʿdi dhālika" — The Quranic Sequencer

The phrase **wa min baʿdi dhālika** ("and after that…") functions as the Quran's most common narrative connector across episodes — particularly in Surah 2's recitation of Israelite history.

**[2:51]** — *thumma ittakhadhtum al-ʿijl min baʿdihi* (then you took the calf after him)
**[2:52]** — *thumma ʿafawnā ʿankum min baʿdi dhālika* (then We forgave you after that)
**[2:64]** — *thumma tawallaytum min baʿdi dhālika* (then you turned away after that)
**[2:74]** — *thumma qasat qulūbukum min baʿdi dhālika* (then your hearts hardened after that)
**[2:92]** — *thumma ittakhadhtum al-ʿijla min baʿdihi* (then you took the calf after him)

This sequence, all in Surah 2's Israelite chronicle, hammers a structural rhythm: **a divine intervention, then *min baʿdi dhālika* a defection.** The repetition is the indictment. **History, in this narrative, *is* the *baʿd*-rhythm.** Each clear act of God is followed by an *after* in which the people fail. The temporal preposition becomes the Quran's clock for cyclical betrayal.

**The Quran does not narrate Israelite history as a sequence of events; it narrates it as a sequence of *afters*.** The verb *thumma* ("then") combined with *min baʿdi dhālika* ("after that") creates a *double-after* — emphasizing that what came after came *significantly* after, *despite* the prior. **In Quranic narrative grammar, *after* is the primary connector and *despite* is its secondary meaning.**

**Striking finding:** **"Amn" (believe) and "byn" (clarity, made-clear) are the most semantically loaded co-occurring roots after Alh.** This confirms the *min baʿdi mā* pattern — the slot is reserved for "after [the clarifying / believing event]." The Quran's "after" is overwhelmingly *after-the-clarification*. **There is no neutral *baʿd* in the Quran.** Every "after" is laden with the question: *after the proof, what?*

**The presence of *jyA* (came, 34 ayahs)** confirms the pattern: *baʿd* almost always sits next to a *coming*-verb. Time is structured as *what-came* + *what-came-after*. The *came* is the reference point; the *baʿd* is the response.

**The presence of *rHm* (mercy, 25 ayahs)** is significant: mercy lives *after* — after relapse, after sin, after wrongdoing. This is the redemptive *baʿd* — the slot where mercy is allowed to operate. **In the Quranic economy, mercy is structurally *posterior* to sin.** It would not be mercy if it preceded the sin.

## Negative Space — What bEd Doesn't Say

1. **No bEd-derivative names "during."** Like qbl, bEd is partner to a *binary temporal grammar*. The Quran has *qabl* and *baʿd* but no native term for *while* or *in the midst of*. **Time is conceived in episodes with thresholds, not as continuous flow.** The ongoing present is structurally absent.
2. **No positive Form V verb (*tabaʿʿada*).** Compare qbl, which has Form V (*taqabbala* = accept) as a positive divine action. There is no equivalent positive Form V for bEd. **You cannot "actively make-yourself-distant" in a praiseworthy sense.** Distance is always negative or punitive.
3. **No bEd-derivative names "the future" as such.** It names *what comes after a specific event* — not the future-in-general. The Quranic "after" is always relative to something. Time has no abstract forward direction; it only has nodes and what-comes-after-them.
4. **The spatial *baʿīd* never names "high" or "deep."** Distance is exclusively *horizontal* — far across a plane. There is no *vertical* distance encoded by bEd. Heaven-vs-earth is a different spatial axis (above/below), not a far/near axis.
5. **No collective noun for "later generations" derived from bEd.** Compare *al-awwalūn* (the ancients, from Awl). The "people-after-us" or "those-who-come-later" do not exist as a noun-class. **The Quran does not name posterity.** It names *the ancients* (al-awwalūn) but not *the descendants*. The arrow of time is biased toward looking back.
6. **No verb "to delay" in moral-acceptance sense.** Delaying acceptance, deferring judgment — these are handled by Axr-root verbs (*akhkhara*, *yastakhirūn*). The bEd-root cannot generate "to put-after." **bEd is positional, not actional.** It marks where things are, not what one does to put them there.

## Translation Divergence — Where bEd Bleeds

bEd-bearing verses cluster in mid-divergence range. Notable patterns:

1. **The redundant *baʿd* in Pickthall.** Pickthall almost always renders *min baʿdi dhālika* as "even after that," inserting "even" to load the moral charge that the Arabic carries by context alone. Saheeh, Khattab, and Arberry are more literal: "after that." **Pickthall is *interpreting* the prosecutorial force of bEd; the others let the reader infer it.**

2. **2:74 (*qasat qulūbukum*)** — "your hearts hardened after that." All four agree on the verb. The divergence is on the prefix: Saheeh "Then" / Pickthall "Then, even after that" / Khattab "Even then" / Arberry "Then." Pickthall's double-emphasis is doing rhetorical work.

3. **34:19 (Sheba's prayer for distance)** — *bāʿid bayna asfārinā*. Translators split: Saheeh "lengthen the distance"; Pickthall "Make the stage… longer"; Khattab "Make the distances of our journeys longer"; Arberry "prolong the stages of our travel." **Three of four make distance explicit; Arberry uses "prolong," losing the spatial sense entirely.** The spatial *bēd* is hard to translate when the context is journeys-through-time.

4. **34:52 (the disbelievers' belated faith) — *min makānin baʿīd*.** Saheeh "from a place far away"; Pickthall "from afar off"; Khattab "from a place so far-off ˹from the world˺"; Arberry "from a place far away." **Khattab inserts the bracketed gloss "from the world"** — committing to a metaphysical reading. The other three preserve the metaphor. **Khattab's editorial brackets are a translation of the *implicit*, but the implicit *is* the verse's pedagogy.**

5. **2:48 (no help on the Last Day) — *wa-lā hum yunṣarūn*** alongside *lā yuqbalu minhā shafāʿa*. The verses joining qbl (acceptance) and bEd (distance from help) reveal the translation challenge: each English version flattens the parallel structure. **English needs more words to mark what Arabic marks with rhythm.**

6. **50:3 (resurrection mocking)** — *dhālika rajʿun baʿīd* ("That is a far-return"). Saheeh "distant return"; Pickthall "far return"; Khattab "Such a return is impossible"; Arberry "a far returning." **Khattab translates *baʿīd* as *impossible* — making explicit what the Arabic uses spatial metaphor to imply.** The disbelievers don't say "impossible"; they say "far." **The far *is* the impossible, in their grammar.**

7. **42:18 (those who dispute about the Hour) — *fī ḍalālin baʿīd*.** All four use "extreme" or "far." But "far" is the lexically closer translation: *ḍalāl* itself means "wandering/being-lost"; *baʿīd* is the *measurement* of how lost. **English idiom prefers "extreme error"; Arabic logic is "far-distant error" — error has been *traveled into*.**

## Verses for Direct Arabic Study

1. **[2:51–74]** — The Israelite-chronicle in Surah 2, with its rhythmic *thumma… min baʿdi dhālika*. Read aloud to feel the *after-after-after* hammering. **This is the densest bEd-passage in the Quran.**
2. **[2:109]** — *yaruddūnakum min baʿdi īmānikum kuffāran… min baʿdi mā tabayyana lahum al-ḥaqq*. The verse uses *min baʿdi* twice in one sentence: after-belief, after-clarification. The double-after is rhetorical: the People of the Book are doubly indicted.
3. **[3:86–90]** — The disbelief-after-belief / repentance-after sequence. Verses 86 ("disbelieved after their belief"), 89 ("repented after"), 90 ("disbelieved after belief"). The same construction running both ways — moral failure and moral recovery — within five ayahs. **This is bEd as the slot of decision.**
4. **[5:39]** — *fa-man tāba min baʿdi ẓulmihī wa-aṣlaḥ*. The cleanest grammatical example of redemptive *baʿd*: after-the-wrong, the turn.
5. **[11:89]** — *wa-mā qawmu Lūṭin minkum bi-baʿīd*. The verse where temporal "not far" and spatial "not far" fuse perfectly. Worth memorizing as the canonical bEd-fusion verse.
6. **[34:19]** — The Sheba imprecation, *bāʿid bayna asfārinā*. The single most morally pointed bEd-verb. Read it for *why* praying-for-distance is a sin: ingratitude for closeness.
7. **[43:38]** — *buʿd al-mashriqayni* — "the distance of the two easts." The damned wishes maximum distance from his Satan-companion. Read for the *aesthetic* of distance: how Arabic poetry uses *east-to-east* to image infinite separation.
8. **[50:31]** — *uzlifati al-jannatu li-l-muttaqīna ghayra baʿīd*. Paradise brought-near, *not far*. The double movement — drawn-near, made-not-far — is the redemptive inverse of 11:89.
9. **[4:11–12]** — The inheritance verses. The procedural *min baʿdi waṣiyyatin*. Worth reading to feel how the moral *baʿd* and procedural *baʿd* are *the same word*. The Quran does not distinguish.

## Cross-cutting — qbl / bEd as Architecture

The qbl/bEd binary is the backbone of Quranic temporal grammar. They are asymmetric in several ways:

1. **Frequency:** qbl 294, bEd 235. Roughly equal but qbl wins by 25%. The Quran tilts slightly toward looking back.
2. **Polysemy:** qbl has more lemmas (13 vs. 6) and more semantic registers (acceptance, direction, kinship, approach). bEd is leaner — distance and "after" only.
3. **Moral charge:** qbl is *neutral-to-positive* (the prophets-before, the qibla-faced, the prayer-accepted). bEd is *neutral-to-negative* (after-the-proof failure, the far-error, the curse-of-distance).
4. **Verbal range:** qbl has Form V *taqabbala* (positive acceptance) and Form IV *aqbala* (positive approach). bEd has only Form III *bāʿid* (negative request for distance) and Form I *baʿudat* (passive distance). **The "before" can be ritually engaged; the "after" can only be undergone or wrongly desired.**
5. **Body-grammar:** qbl is *front-oriented* (face, qibla, accept-from-front). bEd has no body-grammar — it is purely positional. **One can *face* before; one cannot *face* after.** The "after" is something one is *in*, not something one *acts toward*.

The two together form the Quran's binary temporal vocabulary. **The Quran does not have a continuous time; it has two fields — qabl and baʿd — separated by an event.** Every event is a *threshold* between a *before* and an *after*. The middle — the present — is unnamed.

## Rabbit Holes — Questions for Deeper Digging

1. **Is the *baʿīd*/*qarīb* (far/near) pair as architecturally important as the qabl/baʿd pair?** The qrb root (near, close) is a major Quranic concept. Map *qarīb*-*baʿīd* exhaustively. Where do they pair? Where is each used solo? What does the absence of *baʿīd* in a context where *qarīb* is expected reveal?
2. **Does Surah 2's *thumma… min baʿdi dhālika* rhythm appear in other surahs?** Is the cadence specific to the Israelite-chronicle, or does it occur in other historical recitations (Surahs 7, 11, 26)?
3. **The 9:42 *baʿudat ʿalayhim al-shuqqa* (the distance was too far for them).** Is this the only verse where *physical* distance functions as moral excuse-making? Compare with other "they were too tired / too poor / too sick" deflection-verses.
4. **Inheritance verses (4:11–12):** Why are they Medinan, why so procedurally exact, why does *min baʿdi waṣiyyatin* repeat verbatim? Compare with hadith literature on the order-of-operations in Islamic estate law. Is the verse *creating* the procedure or *codifying* an existing one?
5. **Why is Paradise *ghayra baʿīd* (not far) but Hell described with *buʿd* (away with them)?** The distance-grammar is asymmetric: Paradise is approached *from far*; Hell is *cast far*. Is this an aesthetic choice or a theological one (Paradise as *home*, Hell as *exile*)?
6. **The single Form III *bāʿid* (34:19) and the Sheba destruction.** Sheba is mentioned only briefly in the Quran. Is this verse the entirety of Sheba's narrative, or are there other passages? What does it mean that the only *act* of bEd-as-verb in the Quran is *requesting distance* and being destroyed for it?
7. **The 50:3 *rajʿun baʿīd* (a far return).** The disbelievers' mockery of resurrection: "That is a far return." Is this the only Quranic verse where the *disbelievers themselves* use bEd? Worth checking — if so, *baʿīd* may be marked as *the disbelievers' word* in metaphysical contexts.

## Final Observation — The Wreckage Clock

The bEd-root is the Quran's *wreckage clock*. Its grammar is the grammar of consequence: *what came after* a moment of clarity, a covenant, a sign. By default, what comes after is failure. The Quran's "after" is morally and rhetorically *not* a neutral temporal marker — it is the slot where the test of faith plays out, and the verses repeatedly show it failing.

But the same slot, *baʿd*, also houses the redemption. *After* the failure, repentance. *After* the wrongdoing, mercy. The "after" is the temporal threshold of *both* damnation and salvation. **The drama of Quranic time happens in the *baʿd*-slot.** That is why the slot is so heavily filled, why the construction *min baʿdi mā…* is one of the highest-frequency phrases in the corpus, why the same root governs apostasy and repentance.

And spatially — *baʿīd* — the same root names the metaphysical-moral state of being *too far*. The disbeliever is in *ḍalāl baʿīd*; the punishment is *ghayra baʿīd*; the Satan-companion is to be wished *as far as the two easts*. **Distance is the metric of separation from God.** Closeness is grace; distance is loss; *and the Quran's grammar permits no middle ground*. There is no *somewhat far*. *Baʿīd* is binary: in error or not in error, called from a faraway place or here.

The Quran's universe has, in its grammar, two fields — *qabl* and *baʿd* — and two distances — *qarīb* and *baʿīd*. The middle, the gradient, the continuous flow — these are not lexicalized. **Salvation is not a journey along a continuum; it is a transition between fields.** And the *baʿd* — the after — is where every transition is judged.
