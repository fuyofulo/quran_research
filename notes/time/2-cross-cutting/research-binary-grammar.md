# The Binary Grammar of Quranic Time — Phase-2 Cross-Cutting Investigation

**Cross-cutting Phase-2 synthesis on the Quran's missing-middle vocabulary.** Built on prior concept files (qbl, bEd, Awl, Axr, Hyn, Ajl, ywm) and on direct corpus searches against `data/morphology/words.jsonl`.

---

## 1. Headline — Binary, not continuous

**The Quran's temporal grammar is binary.** It lexicalizes endpoints (*qabl* / *baʿd*; *awwal* / *ākhir*; *ajal*; *wqt*; *al-dunyā* / *al-ākhirah*) with formidable density, but it does not lexicalize **middle**, **during**, **in process**, **halfway through**, **mid-stream**, or **continuously-unfolding**. The vocabulary that would do this work in Greek, Latin, English, Hebrew, Sanskrit — *medius*, *μέσος*, *mitten*, *betweenwhile*, *meanwhile*, *during*, *while*, *throughout* — is either absent from Arabic, or present only in non-temporal (spatial, moral, social) senses, or replaced by **endpoint constructions that approximate duration by naming its bookends**.

What follows is corpus-grounded verification, then implications. The headline rests on five corpus facts:

1. **`wsT`** (the canonical "middle" root in classical Arabic) appears **5 times in the entire Quran**. None of them is temporal-during. It is moral-medial (2:143), ritual-positional (2:238), legal-average (5:89), social-mediating (68:28), and spatial-penetrative (100:5).
2. **`xll`** (the root of *khilāl* "through, in the middle of" and *khalīl* "intimate friend") appears 13 times. Of the 8 *khilāl* occurrences, **zero are temporal**. All eight are spatial — "through the dwellings," "amongst you," "between them," "rains from clouds."
3. **`bayna`** appears 266 times as a preposition, but it is **spatially / relationally between**, not **temporally during**. The strongest temporal use is *bayna yadayhi* ("between his hands" = before-him), which the Quran uses 41 times as a quasi-temporal idiom — but the idiom collapses *between* into *before* (the past is "between [his/their] hands"). The Quran's *between* still does not give a *during*.
4. **`mdd`** root contains *muddatihim* (their term) exactly **once** — at 9:4, in a treaty law clause, glossed *ilā muddatihim* ("until their term"). The one *mudda* in the Quran is **bound to *ilā* (until)**, an endpoint preposition. Even when *mudda* (duration) appears, it is grammatically conscripted to be an endpoint.
5. **`Amd`** root yields *amad* (a stretch) exactly **4 times** — and three of the four are bracketed by endpoint-talk (*amadan baʿīdan* = "a far term," 3:30; *al-amad* "the term grew long" causing hearts to harden, 57:16; "I do not know whether what you are promised is near or my Lord will set for it a *amad*," 72:25; "for how long they tarried — an *amad*," 18:12). Even Quranic Arabic's vocabulary for "stretch of time" is rare and mostly negative-distancing.

By contrast: *qabl* root = ~242 occurrences; *baʿd* root = ~235; *awwal* root = 170; *ākhir* root = 250; *ajal* (the named endpoint) = 56; *yawm* (the day-as-event) = 405. **The endpoint-and-event vocabulary outweighs the duration vocabulary by orders of magnitude.**

The Quran lives in a temporal grammar of **before / after** and **first / last** — not of **during**. It names **edges**, not **interiors**. It encodes **decisions**, not **processes**.

## 2. Verification — the missing-middle search

### 2.1 `wsT` (وسط) — the "middle" root

Five total occurrences, five distinct lemmas. None temporal-during.

| Verse | Form | Lemma | Sense |
|---|---|---|---|
| **[2:143]** | *wasaṭan* | *wasaṭ* | "We made you an *ummatan wasaṭan*" — a **median / middle / balanced** community (moral, not temporal) |
| **[2:238]** | *al-wusṭā* | *wusṭā* | "the **middle prayer**" — ritual-positional, naming the *middle* of the day's prayers (positional in the sequence, not temporal-during) |
| **[5:89]** | *awsaṭi* | *awsaṭ* | "the **average** of what you feed your families" — legal-economic median |
| **[68:28]** | *awsaṭuhum* | *awsaṭ* | "the **most just / most balanced** of them said…" — social-mediating |
| **[100:5]** | *fa-wasaṭna* | *wasaṭ* (V) | "and they **penetrate to the middle of** [the enemy]" — spatial-military |

This is the entire corpus of *wsṭ*. The closest the root gets to temporal use is 2:238, *al-ṣalāt al-wusṭā*, which names the **middle prayer** — but here *wusṭā* is a **positional adjective in a series of named prayers**, not a temporal *during*. The Quran knows there is a middle *of a sequence of named prayers*; it does not know there is a middle *of an unfolding stretch of time*.

The absence of an *al-awsaṭ* divine name is striking. **57:3** gives us four divine names in pairs: *al-awwal*, *al-ākhir*, *al-ẓāhir*, *al-bāṭin*. Two temporal endpoints, two ontological endpoints. There is no *al-awsaṭ*, no *al-aṯnāʾ*, no *al-mutawāṣil*. **God brackets time at both ends and being at both faces; He does not occupy a middle.**

### 2.2 `xll` (خلل) — the root of *khilāl*

Thirteen occurrences across three lemmas: *khilāl* (8), *khalīl* (4), *khulla* (1).

The **eight *khilāl* occurrences are all spatial**:

- **[9:47]** — *wa-la-awḍaʿū **khilālakum*** "they would have run **amongst** you" (sowing discord between people)
- **[14:31]** — *min **khilāl*** "from amid…" (giving secretly)
- **[17:5]** — *fa-jāsū **khilāla** ad-diyār* "they penetrated **through** the dwellings"
- **[17:91]** — ***khilālahā*** "**through** it" (gardens with rivers running through)
- **[18:33]** — *baynahumā* + *khilālahumā* — "between the two [gardens]" + "**through** the two of them"
- **[24:43]** — *yakhruju min **khilālihi*** "comes out from **its midst**" (rain from clouds)
- **[27:61]** — *jaʿala **khilālahā** anhāran* "made rivers **through** it" (the earth)
- **[30:48]** — *yakhruju min **khilālihi*** "comes out from **between** it" (rain from clouds again)

**Zero temporal uses.** *Khilāl* in classical Arabic poetry can mean "in the course of, during"; the Quran does not use it that way. Every *khilāl* in the corpus refers to **physical penetration / spatial-between** — never to temporal-during.

The remaining lemmas are non-temporal: *khalīl* (intimate friend, of Abraham at 4:125), *khulla* (close friendship, 2:254, 43:67). These are derivatives of *being-among / interpenetrated*, but they are **social**, not temporal.

### 2.3 `byn` (بين) — *bayna*, "between"

The largest of the candidate roots: 523 morphological occurrences, 266 of them as the preposition *bayna*. Structural verification is needed because *bayna* is the single most plausible candidate for "during" — it is the canonical "between" word.

**Three uses of *bayna*, none of which is "during":**

1. **Spatial-between** — *bayna ʾiṣbiʿayni min aṣābiʿi al-Raḥmān* (the heart between two fingers of the Merciful, hadith — but Quranic equivalents abound): *bayna al-samāʾi wa al-arḍ*, *baynahum*. By far the dominant use.
2. **Relational-between** — *yaḥkumu bayna al-nās* (judges *between* people, 2:213), *aṣlaḥa bayna al-ḏāt* (reconcile *between*), *farraqa bayna* (separate *between*). Hundreds of uses. The *bayna* of judgement and separation.
3. **Quasi-temporal *bayna yadayhi*** — "between his hands" = "before him" (in front). The Quran uses this idiom **41 times**, often of scripture (*muṣaddiqan li-mā bayna yadayhi* — "confirming what was *before* it"). Crucially, this is **not "during the time of" but "before / in front of / in the presence of"** — the *between* gets re-projected into the *before*.

The closest the Quran gets to a temporal *bayna* is at **[3:30]** — *taw addu law anna baynahā wa baynahu **amadan baʿīdan*** ("[the soul] would wish that there were between her and it [her evil deed] **a far term**"). Here *bayna* takes a temporal complement (*amad*) — but **the construction is *bayna* + *endpoint-distance***, not *during the period of X*. The soul wishes the **gap** between her and her deed were great. The middle is named only as **a chasm**.

This is the rule: *bayna* in temporal contexts always describes a **gap**, never an **occupancy**. It separates two endpoints. It does not contain them.

### 2.4 `mdd` and *mudda*

The root *mdd* generates 32 occurrences — but the lemma *muddat* (duration) appears **exactly once**, at **[9:4]**, in the form *ilā muddatihim* ("until their term"). The verse is the law for honoring treaties:

> "Those of the polytheists with whom you contracted a covenant and who then did not fail you in anything nor support anyone against you — fulfill to them their covenant **until their term**."

The lone Quranic *mudda* is:
- (a) Legal-treaty, not eschatological;
- (b) Bound to *ilā* (until) — therefore named **as an endpoint**, not as a duration.

The other *mdd* lemmas are: *madda* (V) "to extend, stretch out" (mostly of God stretching the earth — 13:3, 15:19, 50:7); *amadda* (V) "to reinforce, supply"; *madad* "supplies, reinforcement"; *midād* "ink"; *mamdūd* "extended" (of paradisiacal shade and money). **The root encodes physical extension — never temporal duration.** The one duration-noun (*mudda*) is grammatically conscripted to be an *until*-endpoint.

### 2.5 `Amd` — *amad*

Four occurrences. All four are revealing:

| Verse | Phrase | Function |
|---|---|---|
| **[3:30]** | *amadan baʿīdan* | the soul wishes for **a far term** between her and her evil deed (the term is a *gap*) |
| **[18:12]** | *aḥṣā li-mā labithū **amadan*** | "to know which of the two parties was best at calculating **the stretch** they had stayed" — the Sleepers' duration as something **calculated retrospectively**, not narrated as it unfolded |
| **[57:16]** | *fa-ṭāla ʿalayhimu al-**amad*** | "and **the stretch grew long** upon them, so their hearts hardened" — duration as corrosive, but expressed via the verb *ṭāla* ("it grew long"), not as a temporal preposition |
| **[72:25]** | *yajʿalu lahu rabbī amadan* | "or whether my Lord will set for it **a [far] term**" — endpoint, again |

**The Quran's only word for "stretch of time" appears 4 times, and three of those four uses present the stretch as a problem** (a far gap one wishes for, a long stretch that hardens hearts, a possibly-far term). The fourth (18:12) is a retrospective calculation. **The Quran does not narrate from inside an *amad*; it describes one only from outside.**

### 2.6 `lbv` — *labitha* "to tarry, stay"

The verb *labitha* is the Quran's main verb for "having-stayed-for-a-while" — and it appears in 27 ayahs. But it is not a "during" verb. **It is a retrospective verb that characterizes the duration as already-completed**, and it **almost always appears with a phrase that minimizes the duration**:

- **[10:45]** — *kaʾan lam yalbathū illā **sāʿatan** min an-nahār* — "as if they had not stayed except an **hour** of a day"
- **[17:52]** — *in labithtum illā **qalīlan*** — "you stayed only **a little**"
- **[20:103]** — *in labithtum illā **ʿashran*** — "you stayed only **ten** [days]"
- **[20:104]** — *in labithtum illā **yawman*** — "you stayed only **a day**"
- **[23:113]** — *labithnā **yawman aw baʿḍa yawmin*** — "we stayed **a day or part of a day**"
- **[23:114]** — *in labithtum illā **qalīlan*** — same again
- **[30:55]** — *mā labithū ghayra **sāʿatin*** — "they did not stay other than **an hour**"
- **[46:35]** — *kaʾannahum yawma yarawna mā yūʿadūna lam yalbathū illā **sāʿatan** min nahār* — "as if they had not stayed except an **hour** of a day"
- **[79:46]** — *kaʾannahum yawma yarawnahā lam yalbathū illā **ʿashiyyatan aw ḍuḥāhā*** — "as if they had not stayed except an **evening or its morning**"

The *labitha* verses are eschatological **dismissals of duration**: at the resurrection, what felt like a long life will register as "an hour," "a day," "a part of a day." **The Quran's main duration verb exists to collapse durations to the punctual.** This is the binary grammar in its most explicit form: the long stretch *between* birth and resurrection is, from the resurrection-vantage, **a single hour**. The middle does not survive the eschaton; it gets compressed back to a point.

The two non-eschatological *labitha* verses are the loudest exceptions:

- **[2:259]** — the man who passes the ruined town: *labithta? labithtu yawman aw baʿḍa yawmin. bal labithta miʾata ʿām* — "How long did you stay? I stayed a day or part of a day. Rather, you stayed **a hundred years**." The dramatic point is that **the man's interior sense of duration is a day; the actual duration is a century**. Same compression, narrated from God's vantage instead of the resurrection's.
- **[29:14]** — Noah: *fa-labitha fīhim **alfa sanatin illā khamsīna ʿāman*** ("a thousand years minus fifty"). The Quran's longest narrated human duration. Note even here the duration is **expressed by subtraction** (1000 − 50 = 950) — a doubly endpoint-bracketed expression. The Quran does not narrate Noah's 950 years as 950 years; it names them as a **bounded interval delimited by two numbers**. There is no narrative interior.

**The one place where the Quran is *forced* to acknowledge a long duration (Noah, 950 years) is also the one place where it most explicitly avoids narrating the interior of that duration.** This is structurally consistent with the binary grammar.

## 3. The before/after architecture

The Quran builds its temporal scaffolding from **endpoints** — and uses sequence-of-endpoints to do the work other languages do with *during*.

### 3.1 *min qabli mā* / *min baʿdi mā* — causality through sequence

The construction *min qabli + verbal noun* / *min baʿdi + verbal noun* establishes **causal relations through temporal sequence**. From the qbl analysis: *min qabli an taʾtiya l-mawt* ("before death comes," 14:31), *min baʿdi mā jāʾahum al-bayyināt* ("after the clear signs came to them"). Repeatedly, the Quran's argument structure is:

> X happened. Then Y happened. Therefore Y was caused by / in response to X.

There is no parallel *fī aṯnāʾi mā* ("during the period that") construction. The Quran does not say "*during* the time the messengers were among them, they did Z." It says "*after* the messengers came, they did Z." **Causation runs through sequence-of-edges, not through occupancy-of-a-process.**

### 3.2 The simultaneity-substitutes

When the Quran wants to express something close to simultaneity, it uses one of four constructions — none of which is a true "during":

| Construction | Function | Geometry |
|---|---|---|
| ***yawma* + verbal clause** | "the day when X" — collapses an extended event into a frame | Frame-event, not interior |
| ***iḏā*** (409 occurrences as lemma) | "when X happens" — punctual conditional | Threshold, not interval |
| ***iḏ*** (153 occurrences as form) | "when X was the case" — narrative-past punctual | Snapshot, not interior |
| ***ḥīna* + verb** (~14 occurrences) | "at the moment when X" — circumstantial-temporal | Instant of an action, not its duration |

***Yawm*** is the load-bearing one. The Quran has dozens of compound *yawm*-formulas (*yawm al-qiyāma*, *yawm al-faṣl*, *yawm al-dīn*, *yawm al-ḥasra*, *yawm yunfaḫu fī al-Ṣūr*, etc.). Each one **packages a long process into a single named "day."** Resurrection, judgement, the trumpet-blast, the gathering, the reckoning — each is a *yawm*, never a *muddat al-X* ("the period of X"). The Quran refuses to distend its eschatology into a process; it always packages it as a day-frame.

***Iḏā*** and ***iḏ*** are punctual-conditional and punctual-narrative. They do not house *during*. They mark **the moment when** a switch flips.

***Ḥīna*** + verb (from the Hyn analysis) names **the moment of an action** — *ḥīna mawtihā* ("at the moment of her death," 39:42), *ḥīna tumsūn* ("when you reach evening," 30:17), *ḥīna taqūmu* ("when you arise [for prayer]," 26:218). It is the closest morphological cousin of "during" — and even it functions as an **instant-marker for a ritual-act**, not a process-narrator.

### 3.3 The First/Last binary and the missing middle

**[57:3]** is the cosmological signature:

> *huwa al-awwalu wa-l-ākhiru wa-l-ẓāhiru wa-l-bāṭinu*
> "He is the First and the Last, the Outward and the Inward."

Two binary pairs. **Four endpoints. No middle.** There is no *al-awsaṭ* (the median), no *al-mutawāṣil* (the continuously-unfolding), no *al-mustamirr* (the perduring). God brackets time and brackets visibility — He does not occupy a middle.

The *al-ūlā* / *al-ākhirah* binary scales this cosmology to the soul's life:

- **[28:70]** — *lahu al-ḥamdu fī **al-ūlā wa l-ākhirah*** — "His is the praise in the **first** and the **last**"
- **[53:25]** — *li-llāhi **al-ākhiratu wa-l-ūlā*** — "to Allah belong **the Last and the First**"
- **[92:13]** — *inna lanā la-l-**ākhirata wa-l-ūlā*** — same
- **[93:4]** — *wa-la-l-**ākhiratu khayrun** laka **min al-ūlā*** — "the Last is better for you than the First"

**There is no *al-wusṭā* (the median life)** between *al-ūlā* (this life) and *al-ākhirah* (the hereafter). The *barzakh* (purgatory-like state mentioned in 23:100) is the only thing that comes close — and it is named once and only as a **barrier** (*barzakh*: barrier, isthmus), not as a middle-state to inhabit. The Quran is bivalent: *dunyā* and *ākhirah*, period.

This binary maps exactly onto the **bivalent destinations**: jannah (paradise) and nār (fire). **No purgatory, no limbo, no neutral state.** The state-noun for both is *khulūd* (eternal abiding) — the same word for opposite outcomes (see eschatological-architecture analysis). The grammar of destination matches the grammar of time: two endpoints, no middle.

## 4. *ʿinda* (with/at) — punctual co-presence, not extended occupancy

The root `End` (عند, "with, in the presence of, at") appears in **189 ayahs**. Of these, **112 are *ʿinda llāh*** ("with Allāh") — the canonical formula for things-stored-with-God: the named term (*ajalun musamman ʿindahu*, 6:2), the knowledge of the Hour (*ʿilmu l-Sāʿati ʿinda llāh*, 7:187, 31:34, 33:63, 41:50, 43:85), provision (*rizquhā ʿinda llāh*), reward (*ajruhu ʿinda llāh*).

The grammar of *ʿinda* is **punctual co-presence**: "X is *with* Y," "X is *at* Y." It names a **location**, not an **interval**. When the Quran says *ʿinda llāh*, it does not mean "during God's keeping"; it means "in God's possession / at God's side." This is **spatial-relational projected into the temporal**: the ajal is *at* God's side; the Hour is *at* God's side. **God owns the moment; He does not occupy a process.**

Even *ʿinda* + temporal noun does not give us "during":

- *ʿinda ṣalāti l-ʿishāʾ* ("at the time of the ʿishāʾ prayer," 24:58) — instant of a ritual
- *ʿinda mawtihi* (variants) — the moment of dying
- *ʿinda l-mashʿari l-ḥarām* (2:198) — at the location, not during a stretch

The Quran's most frequent "with" preposition is, when projected onto time, **a moment-marker**, not a period-marker.

## 5. Duration vocabulary — what the Quran *does* have

The Quran does have words that name duration, but they are rare, mostly endpoint-bracketed, and morphologically thin:

| Word | Occurrences | Geometry | Quranic function |
|---|---|---|---|
| **ḥīn** | 35 | indefinite while | mostly *ilā ḥīn* / *ḥattā ḥīn* — "until a while" (15× endpoint-bound) |
| **amad** | 4 | a stretch | far term, calculated stretch, lengthening-stretch (3× presented negatively) |
| **mudda** | 1 | a term | *ilā muddatihim* (until their term) — endpoint-bound |
| **dahr** | 5 | aeon | the suppressed pre-Islamic fate-word, twice in 76:1 + 45:24 — abstract aeon |
| **ʿumr / ʿām** | ~30 | lifetime / year | always specified by a number, then bounded |
| **sinīn** | ~30 | years (plural) | always counted (e.g. 18:11 *sinīna ʿadadan* — "numbered years") |
| **labitha (V)** | 27 | tarried, stayed | retrospective; usually paired with a minimizing phrase |
| **ṭāla (V)** | several | grew long | always pejorative — "the term grew long" → corrosion |

Note the structure: **the more "duration-like" a word is, the more rarely it appears, and the more it is grammatically bound to an endpoint or a number.**

Compare the endpoint vocabulary:
- *qabl* / *baʿd* (~477 occurrences combined)
- *awwal* / *ākhir* (~420 combined)
- *ajal* (56) + *wqt* (13) + *Sāʿa* (~48) + *yawm* (405)

**The endpoint-and-event vocabulary outweighs the duration vocabulary by roughly 30-to-1.** This is a **lexical fingerprint**: the Quran is a corpus that overwhelmingly thinks in *whens*, not in *how-longs*.

The most striking single statistic: **the entire Quran contains the word *mudda* (duration / term) exactly once, and uses it as an endpoint.** A 78,000-word text has one occurrence of the most natural Arabic word for "a duration."

## 6. The Aʿrāf "between" exception — 7:46–49

The single strongest candidate for a Quranic "middle-state" is *al-aʿrāf*, the Heights, in [7:46–49]. The text:

> [7:46] *wa-baynahumā ḥijāb* — **"and between them [paradise and hell] is a partition"**
> *wa-ʿalā l-aʿrāfi rijālun yaʿrifūna kullan bi-sīmāhum* — **"and on the heights are men who recognize each [group] by their marks"**
> They greet the people of the Garden with *salām* but have not yet entered, **though they hope to**.
> [7:47] When their eyes turn toward the people of the Fire, they pray: "Lord, do not put us with the wrongdoers."
> [7:48] The people of the Heights call to those they recognize: "Your gathering and your arrogance availed you nothing."
> [7:49] [pointing at people now entering paradise] "Are these the ones you swore would not get God's mercy? Enter the Garden — no fear upon you."

**Structurally, this is the closest the Quran comes to a middle-state.** And the grammar is revealing. The verses use:

- **bayna-humā** ("between the two") — the *bayna* preposition, but as a **partition**, not an inhabited middle. The middle is **a barrier** (*ḥijāb*) with people **on top of it** (*ʿalā l-aʿrāf*), looking both ways.
- **al-aʿrāf** = "the Heights" (from *Erf*, "to recognize / to know" — same root as *yaʿrifūna*, "they recognize"). The location-name is built from the verb *to recognize*. **The middle position is defined by epistemology** — these are the *recognizers*, who can see both sides.
- The men on the Heights **are not in a middle state of suffering or reward** — they are in a state of **liminal anticipation**, hoping to enter paradise. The verses end (in 7:49) with the implication that they **do** enter.

**The Aʿrāf is not a Quranic *purgatory*. It is a *threshold* — a barrier on which a temporary cohort stands, looks both ways, and then crosses.** The grammar is consistent with the binary thesis: even the most middle-like passage in the Quran builds the middle out of a **partition + an act of recognition + a coming crossing**. The middle is not a state to dwell in; it is **a moment of looking before deciding**.

Classical commentators are divided on who the *aʿrāf*-people are: martyrs, prophets, those whose good and bad deeds are equal, etc. The **textual evidence does not support an enduring middle-state**; the verses describe a temporary epistemic-anticipatory position. The dunyā/ākhira binary survives.

The **other** "middle" candidate is *barzakh* (a barrier between this life and resurrection):

- **[23:100]** — *wa-min warāʾihim **barzakhun** ilā yawmi yubʿathūn* — "and behind them is **a barrier** until the day they are resurrected"
- **[25:53]** — *wa-jaʿala baynahumā **barzakhan** wa-ḥijran maḥjūran* — "He set between the two seas **a barrier** and an inviolable bar"
- **[55:20]** — *baynahumā **barzakhun** lā yabghiyān* — "between the two [seas] is **a barrier**, neither transgresses"

*Barzakh* is **always a barrier** — used twice for the seas (water/salt vs. fresh) and once for the post-death pre-resurrection state. **It is named only as the partition itself; it is never inhabited as a process.** "They are behind a *barzakh*" — passive, blocked, waiting. **The Quran does not narrate what happens *during* the *barzakh*.** It names the barrier and moves on.

The same grammatical move: middle = barrier, not occupancy.

## 7. Theological implications — orientation vs. process

The lexical fingerprint maps onto a deep theological commitment.

### 7.1 A theology of decision, not experience

**The Quran is orientation-obsessed, not process-obsessed.** Its central category is *which way are you facing* — toward Allāh or away. The eschatological reckoning (*yawm al-dīn*, *yawm al-ḥisāb*) is a **single-day audit** of accumulated decisions, not a graduated assessment of ongoing development. The verbs of salvation are punctual: *aslama* (he submitted), *tāba* (he turned), *āmana* (he believed), *kafara* (he covered/disbelieved). Each is a **decision-act**, modeled on a moment, not on a process.

Where Western religious vocabulary develops words for **gradual sanctification, mystical ascent, progressive illumination, dark nights of the soul** — process-vocabulary — the Quranic vocabulary instead develops words for **conditions that one is in or not in**: *muʾmin*, *kāfir*, *muslim*, *muḥsin*, *muttaqī*. Each is a state-name, not a process-name. **You either have *taqwā* or you don't. You don't have "developing taqwā."**

### 7.2 No purgatory because no middle

The mapping is structural:
- **No "during" vocabulary** → **no narrative of process between life and death**
- **No "middle" vocabulary** → **no cosmological middle-state**
- **Only *qabl/baʿd* and *awwal/ākhir*** → **only this life and the next**
- **Only jannah and nār** → **only blessing and torment**
- **Only *khulūd*** → **only eternal abiding** (in either)

This is one tightly integrated grammar. The lexical absence is the metaphysical commitment.

The contrast with later Islamic mystical theology is sharp. **Sufism develops a rich middle-vocabulary** — *maqāmāt* (stations along a path), *aḥwāl* (states), *sulūk* (the journey), *ṭarīqa* (the way), *fanāʾ* (passing-away into God). **None of these are Quranic categories.** They are post-Quranic developments that fill, polemically or pastorally, the **negative space the Quran left open**. The Quran's binary grammar is so stark that mystical, philosophical, and legal traditions **had to** invent middle-vocabularies to cover the human experience of process. The Quran itself refuses.

### 7.3 The aesthetics of decision

The recitative form follows the grammar. Quranic surahs do not narrate continuous stories with developing characters. They oscillate: *wa-iḏā* + scene; *wa-yawma* + scene; *fa-lammā* + scene. **The text is structurally a sequence of moments, each demanding orientation.** There is no Bildungsroman; the closest the Quran has to a continuous narrative is Surah Yusuf — and even that is presented as a chain of *iḏ*-introduced episodes, each a punctual scene.

The listener is positioned not as a witness to a process but as a **person being repeatedly addressed at the threshold of decision**. *Iḏā* + scene = "when X happens, what will you do?" The grammar is consistent with the rhetoric is consistent with the eschatology is consistent with the ontology. **All four levels enforce: there is no middle to inhabit; there is only the moment of facing.**

## 8. Comparison — Quranic vs. other time-grammars

Brief, schematic, in service of locating the Quran:

| Tradition | Time-grammar | Middle-vocabulary |
|---|---|---|
| **Aristotelian** (Greek) | continuous, divisible, infinite-by-division | *μέσος* (middle), *μεσότης* (mean state — virtue!), *διάρκεια* (duration), *χρόνος* (continuous time) |
| **Hebrew Bible** | episodic but with substantial *bayn* and *betōk* (in the midst of) | *betōk* (in the midst), *bēyn* (between — used temporally) |
| **Christian (Greek/Latin)** | *kairos* (decisive moment) + *chronos* (continuous time) | *during*, *meanwhile*; theology of pilgrimage and progress |
| **Buddhist** | impermanent flow, the eternal NOW, dependent origination | *kṣaṇa* (instant) + *santāna* (continuous stream); rich middle-vocabulary |
| **Modern Western** | continuous, measurable, calendrical | *during, throughout, while, mid-, half-, over the course of* |
| **Quranic** | **bivalent: endpoints and named events; no continuous middle** | **near-zero** (mudda 1×, amad 4×, ḥīn 35× as indefinite-while) |

Two observations stand out:

1. **The Hebrew Bible has a developed middle-vocabulary** that the Quran lacks. Hebrew *betōk* ("in the midst of") is used both spatially and temporally with high frequency; *bayn* is used temporally for "between [event A] and [event B]" with explicit interval-meaning. The Quranic *bayna* does the relational and spatial work of Hebrew *bayn* but **does not inherit its temporal-interval use**. This is not a Semitic-family limitation; it is a Quranic specificity.

2. **The Christian *kairos* / *chronos* split** is a useful contrast. *Kairos* names the decisive-moment; *chronos* names the continuous-flow. The Quran has **only *kairos*-vocabulary** — *al-Sāʿa*, *al-yawm*, *iḏā*, *ḥīna*, *waqt*, *ajal*. It has **no *chronos***. There is no Quranic word for "continuous time," for "the flow of time," for "history-as-process." Even *dahr* (the closest candidate) is the **suppressed pre-Islamic fate-word**, named twice in the Quran and explicitly polemicized at 45:24 (the disbelievers say *yuhlikunā illā ad-dahr* — "nothing destroys us but Time" — and the Quran rebukes them).

The Quran's binary grammar is **distinctive among major scriptural traditions**. It is not a feature of Semitic languages generally; it is a feature of this specific corpus.

## 9. Counter-examples and refinements

The thesis must survive its hardest cases.

### 9.1 The dual-time formulas — *bukratan wa-ʿashiyyan* / *al-ghuduww wa al-āṣāl*

The Quran has at least two doubled time-formulas:

- ***bukratan wa-ʿashiyyan*** ("morning and evening") — at [3:41], [19:11], [19:62], [40:55] — **4 occurrences**
- ***bukratan wa-aṣīlan*** ("morning and afternoon") — at [25:5], [33:42], [48:9], [76:25] — **4 occurrences**
- ***bi-l-ghuduwwi wa-l-āṣāl*** ("in the mornings and in the afternoons") — at [7:205], [13:15], [24:36] — **3 occurrences**

These are **endpoint-pair encodings of duration**: by naming the morning *and* the evening, the Quran encodes "the whole day" or "continuously." This is genuinely a way of naming a continuous interval — but it does so by **bookending it with two endpoints**. The grammar remains binary; the *interval* is implied by the *pair*.

Compare: English *"from dawn to dusk"* implies continuity; Greek would use *διὰ πάσης τῆς ἡμέρας* ("through all the day"). The Quran has no *throughout* construction; it builds *throughout* by **the dual-endpoint trick**. **The middle is implied by the absence; it is never named.**

This is a refinement of the headline, not a refutation. **The Quran *can* express duration, but it does so by naming the bookends, not by occupying the middle.** The grammar remains endpoint-based.

### 9.2 Surah 18 — the Sleepers (309 years)

The Sleepers' narrative ([18:9–26]) is the Quranic test case for **how to narrate a long duration**. The Quran's solution:

- [18:11] — *fa-ḍarabnā ʿalā āḏānihim fī al-kahfi sinīna ʿadadan* — "We struck their ears in the cave for **numbered years**"
- [18:12] — *thumma baʿathnāhum li-naʿlama ayyu l-ḥizbayni aḥṣā li-mā labithū **amadan*** — "then We raised them to know which of the two parties was best at calculating the **stretch** they had stayed"
- [18:19] — *labithtum? labithnā **yawman aw baʿḍa yawmin*** — "How long did you stay? We stayed a day or part of a day" (the sleepers' own subjective experience)
- [18:25] — *wa-labithū fī kahfihim **thalātha miʾatin sinīna wa-zdādū tisʿan*** — "they stayed in their cave **three hundred years and added nine**"

The Quran's strategy:
1. Initially names the duration as *sinīna ʿadadan* — **"numbered years"** — an explicitly counted-but-not-told quantity. The years are *enumerable*, but the Quran does not narrate them. The middle is **black-boxed**.
2. At the moment of waking, the sleepers themselves report subjective duration as *yawman aw baʿḍa yawmin* — "a day or part of a day" — the same minimization as the eschatological *labitha* verses. **The interior of the duration is collapsed to the punctual.**
3. Only at the end does the text reveal the count: 309 years. **The number is the endpoint; the interior is never narrated.** The 309 years are named only as *"they stayed"* — a single static verb, with a number attached.

**This is the binary grammar doing its hardest work.** A 309-year duration is named by (a) a counted-but-untold number at the beginning, (b) a subjective-collapse to "a day or part of a day" in the middle, and (c) a final number-revelation. **At no point is the duration narrated as a process.**

### 9.3 Surah 46:35 and the *labitha* dismissal

> *kaʾannahum yawma yarawna mā yūʿadūna lam yalbathū illā **sāʿatan min nahār*** — "as if, on the day they see what they were promised, they had not stayed except an **hour of a day**" [46:35]

This verse is the canonical eschatological compression. From the resurrection vantage, **the entire span of dunyā-life registers as "an hour of a day."** The Quran's grammar of duration finds its purest expression here: at the moment of judgement, **all that mattered was the orientation, not the time-spent.** The middle was never the substance; it was always vapor in retrospect.

This is the binary grammar's deepest theological function: **duration is unreal; orientation is real.** Time is a stage on which decisions are made, not a substance that accumulates.

### 9.4 Noah's 950 years

[29:14] — *fa-labitha fīhim alfa sanatin illā khamsīna ʿāman* — "he stayed among them a thousand years minus fifty."

The Quran's longest narrated duration. And yet:
- It is expressed as a **subtraction**: 1000 − 50 = 950. Two endpoint-numbers, with the total not given.
- The 950 years are named **only as *labitha*** — a static "stayed." **The interior of the 950 years is not narrated.**
- The Quran proceeds immediately to the flood: "the flood seized them while they were wrongdoers." The duration is **a frame** for an end-event, not a process to inhabit.

Again, even at maximum, the binary grammar holds. Noah's 950 years are **named at the bookends and bracketed by the terminating event**.

### 9.5 The *kulla ḥīn* exception — 14:25

> *tuʾtī ukulahā **kulla ḥīnin** bi-iḏni rabbihā* — "[the good tree] gives its fruit **every season** by the leave of its Lord" [14:25]

This is the closest the Quran comes to a "continuous" or "iteratively-during" expression. Even here:
- *Kulla ḥīnin* = **"every while"** — but *kull* + interval-noun produces an **iterative-distributive** ("every X"), not a continuous ("throughout X").
- Translator divergence: Saheeh "all the time"; Pickthall, Khattab, Arberry "every season." Three of four read it as periodic-iterative, not continuous.

The construction *kulla X* is the Quran's way of generating something close to continuity — by **sequenced repetition of a punctual marker**. *Kulla yawmin*, *kulla ḥīnin*, *kulla marratin*, *kulla nafsin*. The continuity is **emergent from iteration**, not lexicalized as occupancy.

## 10. Rabbit holes for Phase 3

The binary grammar opens at least eight further investigations:

1. **The *wsṭ*-as-divine-name absence.** Why do all four 57:3 attributes (al-awwal, al-ākhir, al-ẓāhir, al-bāṭin) come in pairs of endpoints, with no median? The 99 names tradition develops *al-Wāsiʿ* ("the Vast") — but this is spatial-extensiveness, not temporal-medianness. **The Quran resists naming God as the Mediator-of-time.** Trace this through the asmāʾ al-ḥusnā: are any of the 99 names temporally mediating? Hypothesis: **none are**. All are punctual or eternal.

2. **The *barzakh*-as-passive partition.** Compare the three *barzakh* verses ([23:100], [25:53], [55:20]) systematically. The same word names the (a) post-death waiting state, (b) the salt/fresh sea-divider, and (c) the inter-sea barrier. **All three are barriers, never inhabited middles.** Is *barzakh* the **maximally compressed Quranic "middle"** — named only as an obstacle?

3. **The Sufi middle-vocabulary.** Map the Sufi vocabulary of *maqāmāt*, *aḥwāl*, *sulūk*, *fanāʾ* against Quranic absences. Hypothesis: every major Sufi technical term **fills a Quranic vacuum** that the binary grammar created. Sufism is the **process-grammar that the Quran refused to lexicalize**.

4. **The *labitha*-collapse pattern as eschatological doctrine.** Catalog all 27 *labitha* verses. Hypothesis: every eschatological *labitha* (10:45, 17:52, 20:103, 20:104, 23:113, 23:114, 30:55, 46:35, 79:46) **minimizes** the duration. Every non-eschatological *labitha* (2:259, 12:42, 18:25, 29:14, 34:14) is **objectively long but never narrated from inside**. **The verb *labitha* has no "during" semantics; it is always retrospective and always either minimizing or bracketing.**

5. **The 8 dual-endpoint formulas.** Map *bukratan wa-ʿashiyyan* (4×) and *bukratan wa-aṣīlan* (4×) and *al-ghuduww wa al-āṣāl* (3×). Hypothesis: this is the Quran's **only** lexicalized way of producing pseudo-continuity, and it does so by **naming both endpoints of the day**. Test: does the Quran ever pair morning + middle-of-day + evening? Or only morning + evening? Predicted answer: only the bipoles. There is no Quranic *al-ḍuhā wa al-ʿaṣr wa al-maghrib* triple.

6. **The Kahf 309-year narrative as the canonical "how to handle a long duration."** Read [18:9–26] as a structural template for **how the Quran narrates duration** — by black-boxing the interior, collapsing subjective experience to the punctual, and revealing the count only as an endpoint-number. Compare to [29:14] (Noah) and [2:259] (the man at the ruin). Hypothesis: **all three follow the same template** — name a count, collapse the interior, frame as an event.

7. **The contrast with Hebrew biblical *betōk*.** Comparative-Semitic study: where Hebrew uses *betōk* for "in the midst of [the people / the time / the wilderness]," what does Quranic Arabic use? Likely *fī* + noun, but without the middle-emphasis. Test: do Quranic *fī*-constructions ever build "during"-meanings? *Fī sabīli llāh* is not temporal. *Fī al-arḍ* is spatial. *Fī yawmin* is frame, not interior. **Quranic *fī* is consistently locational/frame, not interior-of-process.**

8. **The decision-grammar as an aesthetic.** Phase-3 question: trace the **rhetorical implications** of the binary grammar. The Quran's recitation-style (oscillating threshold-scenes, *wa-iḏā* / *fa-lammā* punctuation, vocative *yā ayyuhā* address) is **aesthetically congruent** with the binary grammar. The text does not develop characters; it positions listeners. **The grammar of decision is the grammar of recitation.** This deserves a dedicated literary-rhetorical analysis.

## Final Observation — What the Absence Builds

The Quran's binary grammar is not a deficiency; it is **a positive theological commitment expressed lexically**. The text refuses to lexicalize *during* because it refuses to **let process be the substance of religious life**. The substance is **orientation**: at every *iḏā*, at every *yawma*, at every *ḥīna*, at every *qabl* and *baʿd*, at every *awwal* and *ākhir*, the listener is positioned **at a threshold** and asked **which way they face**.

The lexical absences map onto cosmological absences (no purgatory), eschatological absences (no graduated reckoning), and pastoral absences (no Bildungsroman of the soul). These are not gaps. They are **the shape that conviction takes when it commits to decision over experience**.

The Quran's clock has two hands: *qabl* and *baʿd*, *awwal* and *ākhir*. There is no third hand sweeping continuously. **There is only the moment in which the two hands meet — and that moment is always now.**
