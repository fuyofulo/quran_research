# The Wajh-Baqi Architecture: The Quran's Cosmic Clock

**Phase-3 rabbit-hole investigation, time deep-dive.**
Verified against `data/morphology/words.jsonl`, `data/morphology/roots.json`, the four sentence translators, and `data/structural/translation-divergence.json`. Builds on the Phase-2 finding (`research-divine-vs-creature-time.md`) that the Quran enforces a lexical apartheid between divine permanence and creature transience.

---

## 1. Headline: The Off-Switch Is Built Into the Vocabulary

The Quran constructs its theology of cosmic time through an exceptionally compressed three-root architecture:

- **wjh** (وجه, "face") — 78 occurrences. Names the part of God that "remains."
- **bqy** (بقي, "remain") — 21 occurrences. Names *what God does* relative to the perishing world.
- **fny** (فني, "perish, vanish") — **exactly 1 occurrence**. The single word that names the cosmic perishing of "everyone on the earth."

This is the most asymmetric vocabulary in the entire time-cluster. The verb that names cosmic perishing — *fanā* — appears once. The noun that names what remains — *wajh Allāh* / *wajh rabbika* — is a single, repeated theological figure. The two verses that put them in direct opposition — **[28:88]** *kullu shay'in hālikun illā wajhah* and **[55:26–27]** *kullu man ʿalayhā fān, wa-yabqā wajhu rabbika* — are the Quran's most compressed assertion of divine atemporality.

The architecture is an off-switch. Everything created has an off-state (*hālik*, *fān*). What does not have an off-state is named not as "God" or "Allah" directly, but obliquely — as the **face** of God. The face is the cosmic clock that has no duration.

Three findings the data forces:

1. **fny is a hapax-cluster.** *fanā* appears only at [55:26]. The Quran's later technical theology of *fanāʾ* (Sufi annihilation, kalām cosmology) is built on a single Quranic occurrence. The word is structurally privileged precisely because it is structurally rare.
2. **The face has five distinct functional patterns**, not one. The same lemma *wajh* (72 occurrences) anchors physical faces, divine remnant, devotional turning, qibla direction, and submission — and the Quran gives no metalinguistic flag distinguishing these.
3. **bqy bridges the apartheid.** The same root that names divine remaining (*yabqā wajhu rabbika*) names the *bāqiyāt al-ṣāliḥāt* — "the abiding good deeds" of humans. This is not a leak in the apartheid; it is its loophole. *bqy* names a property (lasting), not a state of residence (*xulūd*). That distinction does theological work.

## 2. The Three Roots — Lemma Maps

### 2.1 wjh (وجه) — 78 occurrences, 5 lemmas

| Lemma | Buckwalter | Count | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| *wajh* (face) | wajoh | 72 | The dominant noun. Used for human faces, divine "face," seek-face formula, qibla, submitted face. |
| *wajīh* (notable, well-faced) | wajiyh | 2 | [3:45], [33:69] — Jesus and Moses. "Held in honor" — derived honorific. |
| *wajjaha* (Form II, to direct) | waj~aho | 2 | [6:79], [16:76] — to direct/turn. |
| *wijhah* (direction) | wijohap | 1 | [2:148] — "for each, a direction." |
| *tawajjaha* (Form V, to turn oneself) | tawaj~aha | 1 | [28:22] — Moses turning toward Madyan. |

The 72 noun-occurrences of *wajh* are not lexically partitioned in the morphology file — only context tells you whether a given *wajh* is (a) a human's physical face, (b) the divine face, (c) the face one seeks, (d) the qibla one turns toward, or (e) the submitted face one offers. The Quran does *not* have a separate lemma for "divine face." The same word does all the work.

### 2.2 bqy (بقي) — 21 occurrences, 8 lemmas

| Lemma | Buckwalter | Count | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| *abqā* (more lasting, comparative) | >aboqaY` | 7 | [20:71], [20:73], [20:127], [20:131], [28:60], [42:36], [87:17]. The "abqā formula." |
| *baqiyya(t)* (remnant) | baqiy~at | 3 | [2:248] (the ark-remnant), [11:86], [11:116]. |
| *baqiya* (he remained) | baqiYa | 3 | [2:278], [53:51], [55:27]. |
| *bāqiyāt* (abiding things, fem. pl.) | ba`qiya`t | 2 | [18:46], [19:76] — *al-bāqiyāt al-ṣāliḥāt*. |
| *bāqiyah* (a remaining/lasting thing, fem. sg.) | baAqiyap | 2 | [43:28], [69:8]. |
| *bāqīn* (those remaining) | baAqiyn | 2 | [26:120], [37:77] — Noah's family + sparing. |
| *bāq* (remaining, masc. sg. participle) | baAq | 1 | [16:96] — *mā ʿinda llāhi bāq*. |
| *tubqī* (Form IV, "leaves remaining") | tuboqiY | 1 | [74:28] — of Hellfire: "lā tubqī wa-lā tadhar." |

Note the bivalent grammar: *bqy* applies to **divine** ([55:27], [16:96]), to **what is with God** ([28:60], [42:36]), to the **Hereafter** ([87:17], [20:127]), to **good deeds** ([18:46], [19:76]), to **a word among descendants** ([43:28]), to **physical remnants** ([2:248], [11:86]), to **survivors** ([26:120], [37:77]), and even — most surprisingly — to **Hellfire that "leaves nothing remaining"** ([74:28]). The same root names what God does, what Hellfire does, and what good deeds do. The discriminator is grammatical form, not the root itself.

### 2.3 fny (فني) — 1 occurrence, 1 lemma

| Lemma | Buckwalter | Count | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| *fān* (perishing, active participle) | faAn | 1 | **[55:26]** — *kullu man ʿalayhā fān*. |

This is a hapax. The verb *faniya* "to perish" is morphologically attestable in Classical Arabic but the Quran uses it once. The technical Sufi term *fanāʾ* (annihilation in God) is built on this single textual seed. From a corpus-linguistic standpoint, the entire Quranic doctrine of cosmic perishing is anchored at *one verse* — and that verse is paired immediately, in the next ayah, with *bqy*.

### 2.4 Compare: hlk (هلك) — 68 occurrences, 8 lemmas

For the *fnā* vs *hlk* contrast (see §5), the *hlk* root is dominated by the aggressive Form IV causative *aᵓhlaka* "He destroyed" (51 of 68). *halaka* (intransitive "to perish," 5 occ.) is rare. The active participle *hālik* — the form used at [28:88] — appears only twice in the entire Quran: [28:88] and [12:85] (Joseph's brothers fearing he will become "of the perishing").

## 3. The Four Key Passages

### 3.1 [28:88] — *kullu shay'in hālikun illā wajhah*

> وَلَا تَدۡعُ مَعَ ٱللَّهِ إِلَٰهًا ءَاخَرَۘ لَآ إِلَٰهَ إِلَّا هُوَۚ كُلُّ شَيۡءٍ هَالِكٌ إِلَّا وَجۡهَهُۥۚ لَهُ ٱلۡحُكۡمُ وَإِلَيۡهِ تُرۡجَعُونَ

**Context.** Closing verse of *Sūrat al-Qaṣaṣ*. Addressed in the second person singular to the Prophet, but the implicit audience is anyone who calls another deity alongside God. The verse chains four claims:

1. *lā tadʿu maʿa llāhi ilāhan ākhar* — do not invoke alongside Allah another god.
2. *lā ilāha illā huwa* — there is no god except Him.
3. *kullu shay'in hālikun illā wajhah* — every thing is perishing except His face.
4. *lahu l-ḥukm wa-ilayhi turjaʿūn* — to Him belongs the judgment, and to Him you will be returned.

**The grammatical move.** *Kullu shay'in* — "every thing" — is the most universal noun phrase available. *Hālikun* is an active participle: "perishing" or "in the state of perishing." Critically, this is **not future tense**. Read literally, every thing *is right now* in the state of perishing. The participle does double duty: it asserts both an ongoing ontological condition ("contingent, perishable") and an eschatological certainty ("will perish"). The four sentence translators split on this:

- **Saheeh:** "Everything will be destroyed" (future passive)
- **Pickthall:** "Everything will perish" (future active)
- **Arberry:** "All things perish" (gnomic present — closest to the Arabic)
- **Khattab:** "Everything is bound to perish" (modal)

Only Arberry preserves the participial register. The English future-tense translations subtly mistime the claim: the Quran is not predicting a future moment when things will perish; it is describing their permanent ontological status.

**The exception clause.** *Illā wajhah* — "except His face." Saheeh and Arberry preserve "Face" (capitalized). Pickthall renders "countenance." Khattab — uniquely — refuses the metaphor: "**except He Himself.**" Khattab's choice is exegetically loaded: he identifies *wajh* with the divine essence and excises the metaphor. This is the Mu'tazilite move (see §4); the Ash'arite move would be Saheeh/Arberry's literal preservation.

**Translation divergence:** combined score 0.6737 (mid regime), Jaccard 0.3841. Lexical disagreement count = 31. The *wajhah* is precisely where translators diverge.

### 3.2 [55:26–27] — *kullu man ʿalayhā fān, wa-yabqā wajhu rabbika*

> كُلُّ مَنۡ عَلَيۡهَا فَانࣲ ۞ وَيَبۡقَىٰ وَجۡهُ رَبِّكَ ذُو ٱلۡجَلَٰلِ وَٱلۡإِكۡرَامِ

**Context.** *Sūrat al-Raḥmān*, the cosmic-mercy litany with its 31-fold refrain *fa-bi-ayyi ālāʾi rabbikumā tukadhdhibān*. The verses sit roughly midway between the cosmological opening and the eschatological close. The transition from creation-catalog to perishing-catalog is unannounced: the surah simply pivots into the cosmic off-switch.

**The structural parallel with [28:88].**

| | [28:88] | [55:26–27] |
|---|---|---|
| Universal subject | *kullu shay'* (every thing) | *kullu man ʿalayhā* (everyone on it) |
| Perishing verb | *hālik* (root *hlk*, perishing-active) | *fān* (root *fny*, perishing-active) |
| Exception | *illā wajhah* (except His face) | *wa-yabqā wajhu rabbika* (and remains the face of your Lord) |
| Divine attribute | (none in this verse) | *dhū l-jalāl wa-l-ikrām* (possessor of majesty and honor) |

The two verses construct the **same theological figure with different vocabulary**. [28:88] uses *hlk*; [55:26] uses *fny*. [28:88] uses an exception clause (*illā*); [55:26–27] uses a contrastive coordination (*kullu... wa-yabqā*).

**The "ʿalayhā" puzzle.** *Kullu man ʿalayhā* — "everyone who is on it." The pronoun *-hā* is feminine singular; the antecedent is not stated in this verse. The standard reading: *al-arḍ* (the earth, fem.). But *al-arḍ* doesn't appear in the immediate context. The four translators all supply "earth" or "thereon" — but the ambiguity is real, and one Sufi reading takes *-hā* to refer to the entire cosmos (al-dunyā). The grammatical underdetermination is theologically productive: whatever surface ("it") creatures inhabit, they are perishing on it.

**Translation divergence at [55:26]:** combined score **0.7874 — high regime**, the highest of any verse in this investigation. Jaccard 0.1649. The translators agree on "perish" but disagree on *kullu man ʿalayhā* and the participial register. *fān* is the densest single-word in the whole investigation; everything theological pivots on it.

### 3.3 [18:46] — *al-bāqiyātu al-ṣāliḥātu khayrun ʿinda rabbika thawāban*

> ٱلۡمَالُ وَٱلۡبَنُونَ زِينَةُ ٱلۡحَيَوٰةِ ٱلدُّنۡيَاۖ وَٱلۡبَٰقِيَٰتُ ٱلصَّٰلِحَٰتُ خَيۡرٌ عِندَ رَبِّكَ ثَوَابࣰ ا وَخَيۡرٌ أَمَلࣰ ا

**The complication.** *bqy* — the same root that names divine remaining at [55:27] — here names **the abiding good deeds** of humans. The morphological form *bāqiyāt* is feminine plural active participle: "[deeds that are] in the state of remaining."

The four translators diverge sharply on whether to render *al-bāqiyāt al-ṣāliḥāt* as a fixed phrase or dissolve it:
- Saheeh: "the enduring good deeds"
- Pickthall: "the good deeds which endure"
- Arberry: "the abiding things, the deeds of righteousness"
- Khattab: "the everlasting good deeds"

Arberry alone preserves the original two-noun construction (*al-bāqiyāt* as substantive, *al-ṣāliḥāt* as descriptor). The classical *tafsīr* tradition treats this as a fixed phrase referring specifically to the *tasbīḥ* formulae (subḥān Allāh, alḥamdu li-llāh, lā ilāha illā llāh, allāhu akbar). The Quran does not specify.

The parallel at [19:76] uses the identical phrase *al-bāqiyāt al-ṣāliḥāt khayrun ʿinda rabbika thawāban* — only the second clause changes (from *amalan* "in hope" to *maraddan* "in return").

### 3.4 [87:17] — *wa-l-ākhiratu khayrun wa-abqā*

> وَٱلۡأٓخِرَةُ خَيۡرࣱ وَأَبۡقَىٰٓ

**The "abqā formula."** Comparative *abqā* ("more lasting") appears 7 times in the Quran, all in similar contrastive structures:

| Verse | Subject | Comparison context |
|---|---|---|
| [20:71] | Pharaoh's punishment vs Moses' Lord's | "ayyunā ashaddu ʿadhāban wa-abqā" |
| [20:73] | God vs Pharaoh | "wa-llāhu khayrun wa-abqā" |
| [20:127] | Punishment of Hereafter | "wa-la-ʿadhābu l-ākhirati ashaddu wa-abqā" |
| [20:131] | Lord's provision vs worldly splendor | "wa-rizqu rabbika khayrun wa-abqā" |
| [28:60] | What is with Allah vs worldly enjoyment | "wa-mā ʿinda llāhi khayrun wa-abqā" |
| [42:36] | What is with Allah vs worldly enjoyment | (identical to 28:60) |
| [87:17] | The Hereafter vs the worldly life | "wa-l-ākhiratu khayrun wa-abqā" |

The comparative *abqā* is **never** predicated of God directly as an attribute. It is predicated of (a) God's punishment, (b) God's provision, (c) what is with God, (d) the Hereafter — i.e., **God's adjuncts**, not God Himself. The divine identity-attribute uses the active participle *bāq* ([16:96]) or the verbal *yabqā* ([55:27]). The comparative is reserved for the Hereafter and its goods.

This refines the apartheid: God is *bāq* (absolutely remaining); the Hereafter is *abqā* (more lasting than the world). The grammar partitions absolute from comparative permanence.

## 4. The Wajh of God — Five Functional Patterns

The 78 occurrences of *wjh* lemmas distribute across at least five distinguishable theological functions. The Quran does not metalinguistically flag the distinction; readers must infer from context.

### 4.1 The cosmic remnant — the face that remains

- **[28:88]** *kullu shay'in hālikun illā wajhah* — "every thing is perishing except His face."
- **[55:27]** *wa-yabqā wajhu rabbika dhū l-jalāl wa-l-ikrām* — "and remains the face of your Lord, possessor of majesty and honor."

Two verses. The entire doctrine of cosmic divine remaining is on these two ayahs.

### 4.2 The seeking-face — devotional motive

The phrase *wajh Allāh* / *wajh rabbihi* / *wajhihi* used as the **direct object of intentional verbs** (*irādah*, *ibtighāʾ*, "to want," "to seek") names the proper motive of righteous action.

- **[2:272]** *wa-mā tunfiqūna illā btighāʾa wajhi llāh* — "you do not spend except seeking the face of God."
- **[6:52]** *yurīdūna wajhah* — "they want His face."
- **[13:22]** *ibtighāʾa wajhi rabbihim* — "seeking the face of their Lord."
- **[18:28]** *yurīdūna wajhah* — same.
- **[30:38]** *yurīdūna wajha llāh* — those who want God's face are *al-mufliḥūn* (the successful).
- **[30:39]** *turīdūna wajha llāh* — those who give zakah seeking God's face are multiplied.
- **[76:9]** *innamā nuṭʿimukum li-wajhi llāh* — "we feed you only for the face of God" (the righteous-feeding the captive).
- **[92:20]** *illā btighāʾa wajhi rabbihi l-aʿlā* — "except seeking the face of his Lord, the Most High."

In every case, the phrase functions adverbially — it describes the **purpose** of the act. The translators consistently waver between literal "face" (Saheeh, Arberry), "countenance" (Pickthall), and "pleasure" (Khattab). Khattab's "pleasure" is interpretive: it imports the later kalām consensus that *wajh* in this context means *riḍāʾ* (good pleasure). The Arabic does not say "pleasure"; it says "face." The translation choice is theologically charged.

### 4.3 The directional turning — qibla and prayer

- **[2:144]** *fa-walli wajhaka shaṭra l-masjidi l-ḥarām* — "turn your face toward the Sacred Mosque" (qibla change).
- **[2:149]**, **[2:150]** repeat the formula.
- **[2:148]** *wa-li-kullin wijhatun huwa muwallīhā* — "for each one a direction toward which he turns" (uses the related lemma *wijhah*).
- **[7:29]** *aqīmū wujūhakum ʿinda kulli masjid* — "set your faces at every place of prostration."
- **[10:105]**, **[30:30]**, **[30:43]** *aqim wajhaka li-d-dīn ḥanīfan* — "set your face toward the religion."

Here *wajh* names a directional thing — the face one orients. It is grammatically possessed by the worshipper (*wajhaka*, *wujūhakum*) and the verb is one of orientation (*walli*, *aqim*). This is not the divine face; this is the human face one points.

### 4.4 Wherever you turn, there is the face of God

A single, theologically anomalous verse:

- **[2:115]** *fa-aynamā tuwallū fa-thamma wajhu llāh* — "wherever you turn, there is the face of God."

This verse partially deconstructs the qibla pattern. It establishes that the face of God is not directionally located. Saheeh, Pickthall, and Arberry all preserve "face." Khattab again paraphrases: "wherever you turn you are facing ˹towards˺ Allah." Khattab's bracket reverses the grammatical direction: the Arabic says God's face is wherever; Khattab says you are facing Allah wherever.

### 4.5 The submitted face

The "Islam-formula" — *aslama wajhahu li-llāh* — uses *wajh* for the submitted self.

- **[2:112]** *man aslama wajhahu li-llāhi wa-huwa muḥsin* — "whoever submits his face to God being a doer of good."
- **[3:20]** *aslamtu wajhiya li-llāh* — "I have submitted my face to God."
- **[4:125]** *aslama wajhahu li-llāhi wa-huwa muḥsin*.
- **[6:79]** *wajjahtu wajhiya li-lladhī faṭara s-samāwāti wa-l-arḍ* — "I have turned my face toward the One who originated the heavens and the earth" (Abraham).
- **[31:22]** *yuslim wajhahu ilā llāh*.

Here *wajh* synecdochizes the whole self. To submit one's *wajh* is to submit one's whole orientation. Pickthall renders this consistently as "surrender his purpose." This is interpretive: the Arabic is concrete (face); Pickthall abstracts it (purpose).

### 4.6 The condemned/honored face — eschatological countenance

A separate cluster — perhaps the largest in raw occurrence count — uses *wujūh* (plural faces) for the **condition of the resurrected**:

- **[3:106]** "faces will be brightened, faces will be darkened."
- **[10:26–27]** the faces of the rewarded vs the punished.
- **[20:111]** *ʿanati l-wujūhu li-l-Ḥayyi l-Qayyūm* — "all faces will be humbled before the Living, the Self-Subsisting." (The signature pairing of *wujūh* with the divine names of remaining.)
- **[54:48]** *yawma yusḥabūna fī n-nāri ʿalā wujūhihim* — dragged into the Fire on their faces.
- **[67:22]** *yamshī mukibban ʿalā wajhihi* — walks fallen on his face.
- **[75:22, 24]**, **[80:38, 40]**, **[88:2, 8]** — the "faces that day" couplets.

This sixth cluster is statistically dominant (perhaps 30+ of the 72 *wajh* nouns). It uses *wajh* purely physically — the face that will be either bright or dark, humbled or proud, dragged or upright. It interlocks with the seeking-face theology: those who sought God's face in life will have radiant faces at resurrection.

## 5. The fnā vs hlk Distinction

Why does [28:88] use *hlk* while [55:26] uses *fny*?

### 5.1 *hlk* — the destruction vocabulary

Of 68 *hlk* occurrences:
- **51 are *aᵓhlaka*** (Form IV causative, "He destroyed") — overwhelmingly used for God destroying past nations: ʿĀd, Thamūd, the people of Lot, Pharaoh's people, etc. (E.g., [6:6] *kam ahlaknā min qablihim min qarn* — "how many a generation We destroyed before them.")
- **5 are *halaka*** (Form I, "to perish") — including [4:176] (a man who dies childless), [8:42] (those who perish after evidence), [40:34] (until [Yūsuf] perished), [69:29] (my power has perished from me).
- **2 are *hālik*** (active participle) — [12:85] (Joseph's brothers fearing he will become "of the perishing") and **[28:88]**.
- 5 are *muhlik* (one who destroys, often divine).
- 2 are *mahlik* (place/time of destruction).

**Pattern:** *hlk* is the Quran's vocabulary of **destruction-as-event**. It is ethically loaded — peoples are destroyed for their crimes, individuals perish in identifiable circumstances. It implies an agent (often God), a moment of destruction, and frequently a moral cause. Hellfire's torment uses *aᵓhlaka*; the destroyed cities use *aᵓhlaka*; a person who "perishes" childless uses *halaka*. The root is **forensic and event-bounded**.

### 5.2 *fny* — the ontological perishing

The single occurrence at [55:26] uses the active participle *fān*. *fanā* in Classical Arabic is the verb of ceasing-to-exist — exhaustion, vanishing, becoming nothing. It does not imply an agent of destruction or a moral cause. It is **what happens to creatures by virtue of being creatures**.

### 5.3 The two registers

- **[28:88]:** *kullu shay'in hālikun illā wajhah*. *Hālik* — "in-the-state-of-perishing-as-event." Every thing has its destruction-moment. The exception is God's face.
- **[55:26]:** *kullu man ʿalayhā fān*. *Fān* — "vanishing-by-nature." Every creature on the surface ontologically fades.

Read together, the two verses cover both registers of cosmic transience: the **forensic/eschatological** (*hlk*: things destroyed) and the **ontological** (*fny*: things that fade). The cosmic clock has both an event-end (judgment, destruction) and a nature-end (creaturehood, fading). The divine face stands outside both.

This is unusually disciplined vocabulary. The Quran could have repeated *hlk* in [55:26]; it didn't. It could have used *fny* in [28:88]; it didn't. The two perishing-words are deployed where each fits — *fny* at the gentle cosmic-mercy litany of *al-Raḥmān* (ontological fade), *hlk* at the polemic close of *al-Qaṣaṣ* against rival deities (forensic destruction).

### 5.4 Negative-space note

The Quran never uses *fny* of a person, a city, or a generation. *fanā* is a single, surgical word reserved for the most universal claim. This is the inverse of *hlk*, which is overwhelmingly historical-particular (Pharaoh, Thamūd, etc.) and only twice cosmically universal ([28:88], [12:85]).

## 6. bqy as Creature-Attribute — The Apartheid Loophole

Phase-2 (`research-divine-vs-creature-time.md`) established the lexical apartheid: creature-time roots (xld, Abd, Ajl, etc.) are never predicated of God. *bqy* presents an apparent loophole — it is predicated of both God *and* of created things. Investigation refines, rather than refutes, the apartheid finding.

### 6.1 The bivalence

| Subject of *bqy* | Verses | Form |
|---|---|---|
| Allah / *mā ʿinda llāh* | [16:96], [28:60], [42:36] | *bāq*, *abqā* |
| Wajh of the Lord | [55:27] | *yabqā* |
| Allah (vs Pharaoh) | [20:73] | *abqā* |
| The Hereafter / its punishment | [20:127], [87:17] | *abqā* |
| Lord's provision | [20:131] | *abqā* |
| Pharaoh's punishment | [20:71] | *abqā* (in Pharaoh's mouth, mocking) |
| The abiding good deeds | [18:46], [19:76] | *bāqiyāt* |
| Word among descendants | [43:28] | *bāqiyah* |
| Survivors / spared | [26:120], [37:77] | *bāqīn* |
| A remnant of evil | [69:8] | *bāqiyah* |
| Hellfire that "leaves nothing" | [74:28] | *tubqī* |
| The ark-remnant | [2:248] | *baqiyyah* |
| What remains of usury | [2:278] | *baqiya* |
| Good remainder among generations | [11:116] | *baqiyyah* |
| Shuʿayb's "remainder of God" | [11:86] | *baqiyyat allāh* |
| Thamūd — "He spared none" | [53:51] | *abqā* |

### 6.2 The grammar of "remaining" vs the grammar of "abiding"

This is the apartheid-refinement. *bqy* is a **property** of things that last — God lasts, His face lasts, His provision lasts, the good deeds last, the survivors last, even the ark-remnant lasts. The Quran is willing to predicate *bqy* of any entity that lasts, divine or created.

What the Quran is **not** willing to do is predicate *xulūd* (eternal residence as identity-state) of God. *xld* names creature-state-as-residence; *bqy* names property-of-lasting. The two roots work different grammar.

A second discriminator: *bqy* used of created things almost always names a **comparison** (*abqā*: "more lasting") or a **persistence-against-loss** (*bāqiyāt*: things that remain when other things go; *baqiyyah*: the residue after destruction). When predicated of God, *bqy* is **absolute** — *yabqā wajhu rabbika*, no comparison-class, no loss to be persisted-against.

So the apartheid holds at the level of theological force: God *bqy* unconditionally; creatures *bqy* relative to other creatures or relative to God.

### 6.3 The strangest co-occurrence

[74:28] — describing Hellfire — *lā tubqī wa-lā tadhar* ("it leaves nothing remaining, and it spares not"). The Form IV *tubqī* uses the same root that names divine remaining at [55:27]. Hellfire's "leaving-nothing-remaining" semantically inverts divine *bqy*: where God *bqy* (remains), Hellfire *lā tubqī* (does not let-remain). The cosmic clock, run from the perishing side, is named by negating the same root.

## 7. The Cosmic Clock Implication

### 7.1 Time as created

Phase-2 (`research-divine-vs-creature-time.md` and `research-disjoint-clocks.md`) established that the Quran treats time-of-creatures as a created thing — measured, appointed, finite. Days (*ayyām*) are created sequences ([7:54]: God created the heavens and earth in six days); appointed terms (*ājāl*) are set by God for nations and individuals; God's day (*yawm ʿinda rabbika*) is qualitatively other. The Quran does not philosophically thematize time — it doesn't say "time is created" — but the grammar *enacts* the position: creature-time vocabulary is never predicated of God.

### 7.2 The eschatological off-switch

[28:88] and [55:26–27] together name the off-switch. At the eschaton, *kullu shay'* and *kullu man* enter their state of *hlk* / *fny*. The fact that the Quran uses **active participles** (*hālik*, *fān*) suggests that the perishing is not merely future — it is an ongoing, present-tense ontological condition that resolves at the eschaton. Creatures are *already* perishing; the Hour merely completes what was always true.

What does not perish is *wajh*. The face remains — *yabqā*.

### 7.3 The face as cosmic clock

Every clock tells time relative to a reference point. The Quran's reference point is the divine *wajh*. Creature-time is duration; divine *bqy* is not duration — it is the unmoving frame against which duration is measured. The face has no *ajal*, no *ḥīn*, no *amad*. It is the structural marker of "what does not perish."

This connects to the Phase-2 finding on cyclical-vs-linear time (`research-cyclical-vs-linear.md`): cycles end at the Hour, the line terminates at judgment, and what is "after" is named not as a further time but as *wajh Allāh* — the face that was always remaining.

The compressed theology: **the cosmic clock has an off-switch (perishing), and what remains when the clock stops is not time at all but a face — non-temporal, *bāq*, *abqā*.**

### 7.4 The seeking-face circuit

The seeking-face theology (§4.2) closes the circuit. Humans live in creature-time, with their *ajal* set, their *ḥīn* given. The proper motive of action is *ibtighāʾa wajhi llāh* — seeking the face of God. This is structurally significant: human action that is *for* something other than the face — for reward, for thanks, for worldly increase — is locked inside the perishing. Action *for* the face links into the *bāq*. The *bāqiyāt al-ṣāliḥāt* are the deeds that remain because they were directed at what remains.

This is why [76:9] (*innamā nuṭʿimukum li-wajhi llāh*) and [18:46] (*al-bāqiyāt al-ṣāliḥāt*) belong together. The first names the motive (face); the second names the outcome (remaining). The arc from intentional motive to eschatological persistence runs through the divine face.

**Pattern:** The wajh-bqy verses cluster in the **mid-to-high divergence regime**. The single-highest-divergence verse in this entire investigation is **[55:26]** — the *fān* hapax. The translators struggle most with the very vocabulary that does the most theological work.

**Specific contested points:**

- **wajh** — Saheeh & Arberry: "Face" (capitalized, literal); Pickthall: "countenance" (slightly abstracted); Khattab: oscillates between "Face" and "pleasure" / "Himself" (interpretive). The Mu'tazilite/Ash'arite divide is visible *in the translations*.
- **hālikun** vs **fānin** — translators flatten both into "perish" or "destroyed." Only the Arberry reading at [28:88] ("All things perish") preserves the gnomic-present register.
- **al-bāqiyāt al-ṣāliḥāt** — three translators dissolve the substantive (deeds-that-endure); Arberry alone keeps "the abiding things, the deeds of righteousness."
- **abqā** — comparative form rendered "more enduring" (Arberry, Saheeh), "more lasting" (Pickthall, Khattab). Convergent here; the abqā formula is the most stable.

## 9. Negative Space

What the Quran does **not** say is as theologically pointed as what it says.

1. **No philosophical claim about divine atemporality.** The Quran never says "God is outside time," "time is created," or "God is eternal in some non-temporal sense." These claims are kalām and falsafah constructs. The Quran shows divine atemporality through the lexical apartheid and the wajh-bqy figure — not through metaphysical assertion.

2. **No naming of *zamān* or *dahr* as God's creation.** *zamān* (the standard Arabic word for "time") does not appear in the Quran at all. *dahr* appears twice, both in contexts that quarantine it: [45:24] (rejected as a deity by deniers of resurrection) and [76:1] (an inert backdrop). The Quran refuses the available vocabulary of "time" as a thing.

3. **The face is never described physically.** No eyes, no features, no shape, no colour. *Wajh* is asserted as remaining ([55:27], [28:88]), as sought ([13:22], [92:20]), as turned toward ([2:144]), as submitted ([2:112]). It is grammatically functional, not physically described. Translators who render it "countenance" (Pickthall) or "pleasure" (Khattab) are filling the descriptive gap with their own interpretation.

4. **The Quran never uses *fanāʾ* (the masdar) — only *fān* (the participle).** The Sufi technical term *fanāʾ* is not Quranic vocabulary. The Quran has the perishing-as-state (*fān*), not the abstraction (*fanāʾ*).

5. **No transition verse between [28:88] and Sufi *fanāʾ* doctrine.** The entire later doctrine of mystical annihilation hangs on a single hapax. The Quran does not develop *fanāʾ*; it does not even use the masdar form.

6. **The face never has temporal predicates.** The Quran does not say "the face is *qadīm*" (eternal-pre), "the face is *azalī*" (without beginning), or "the face is *abadī*" (without end). These are post-Quranic theological terms. Quranic divine eternity is asserted by *bqy* (the face *remains*) — a verbal/property predicate, not a temporal essence.

7. **Allah is never called *al-Bāqī* in the Quran.** The divine name *al-Bāqī* — central to the 99-names tradition — is not in the Quran. The Quran predicates *bqy* of God's face, of what is with God, of God's provision, but never makes *al-Bāqī* a divine name. The closest approximation is [55:27]'s *wa-yabqā wajhu rabbika*. The 99-names tradition extracts the name from the verbal usage.

8. **Allah is never called *al-Wajh*.** Despite the prominence of *wajh Allāh*, the Quran does not turn "Face" into a divine name. *wajh* is grammatically possessed (His face); never standalone-titular.

The two omissions in (7) and (8) are doctrinally significant: the Quran preserves *wajh* and *bqy* as **predicate-relations**, not as substantive divine names. This is theologically conservative — it refuses to reify the face into an "attribute" or the remaining into an "essence." The face does not become a thing; the remaining does not become a property held by an essence. They are how God shows up *in the grammar* of creaturely transience.

## 10. Synthesis

The wajh-bqy architecture is the Quran's most compressed theology of cosmic time. It works through three roots, two perishing-words, and a single grammatical move:

**The cosmic move:** The cosmic clock has an off-switch (creatures perish — *hālik*, *fān*). What remains when the clock stops is not a further interval but the *wajh* of God, which is *bāq*. The divine remaining is asserted as a verbal/participial predicate, not a substantive attribute.

**The moral move:** Action directed at *wajh Allāh* (the seeking-face theology) links the moral life to what remains. *Bāqiyāt al-ṣāliḥāt* — the abiding good deeds — are the human acts that exceed human time *because they were directed at what does not perish*. The arc from motive (seeking the face) to outcome (deeds that abide) runs through divine remaining.

**The grammatical discipline:** The Quran refuses to philosophize. It does not say "God is outside time." It does not name God *al-Bāqī*. It does not name God *al-Wajh*. It says: the face *remains*; everyone else *perishes*. The doctrine is in the grammar, not in metaphysical assertion.

**The lexical apartheid, refined:** Divine permanence (*bqy*, *wajh*) and creature transience (*hlk*, *fny*) are kept in disciplined separation — but the boundary is the Hereafter and good deeds, where created things participate in *bqy* without identity-participating in divine eternity. The grammar of "remaining" (property: *bāq*, *abqā*) is bivalent; the grammar of "abiding-as-residence" (state: *xulūd*) is partitioned. *bqy* names what lasts; *xld* names where one dwells. God lasts (*bqy*); paradise-dwellers dwell-eternally (*xld*). The two grammars do different theological work.

**The cosmic clock:** *kullu shay'in hālikun illā wajhah*. *kullu man ʿalayhā fān, wa-yabqā wajhu rabbika*. Two verses. Three roots. The entire doctrine of divine atemporality, asserted not as a metaphysical claim but as a grammatical fact about what perishes and what does not.

## 11. Rabbit Holes

Investigations that this study opens but does not pursue:

1. **The "ʿalayhā" pronoun problem.** *Kullu man ʿalayhā fān* — what is the *-hā*? The standard *al-arḍ* is a textual completion, not a Quranic specification. A surahwide rabbit hole: when the Quran uses elliptical feminine pronouns in the cosmic-mercy litany, what work does the underdetermination do? Does Sūrat al-Raḥmān refuse to specify *what* the creatures are on?

2. **Why does only Sūrat al-Raḥmān use *fny*?** The single Quranic occurrence of *fny* sits inside the most heavily refrained surah in the Quran (the 31-fold *fa-bi-ayyi ālāʾi rabbikumā*). Is the perishing-word part of the cosmic-mercy refrain — is *fanā* itself one of the *ālāʾ* (favors) of the Lord? Investigate the surah-internal logic.

3. **The *jalāl* and *ikrām* attributes at [55:27].** *Dhū l-jalāl wa-l-ikrām* (possessor of majesty and honor) — appears here and at [55:78]. The two attributes are unique to this surah. What do they add to the wajh? The 99-names tradition derives *al-Jalīl* and *al-Karīm* — but these are extractions. Why does the wajh-remains formula need this specific epithet?

4. **The relation between *al-Qayyūm* and the *wajh*.** [20:111] *ʿanati l-wujūhu li-l-Ḥayyi l-Qayyūm* — all faces are humbled before the Living Self-Subsisting. Is this verse the structural inversion of [55:27]? At [55:27], the divine face remains while creature faces perish; at [20:111], creature faces are humbled before the divine names of remaining. The two faces face off.

5. **The active-participle semantics.** *Hālik*, *fān*, *bāq* — all active participles. The Quran's cosmic-time vocabulary is participial, not finite-verbal. What does the participle do that a finite verb wouldn't? Hypothesis: the participle expresses condition-as-identity; "every thing is in-the-state-of-perishing" is stronger than "every thing will perish."

6. **The *hālik*-of-Joseph parallel.** [12:85] and [28:88] are the two *hālik* occurrences. The Joseph context (the brothers fearing Yaʿqūb will become "of the perishing") and the cosmological context look superficially unrelated. Is there a deeper connection? Both involve grief, return, and a father-figure (Yaʿqūb / Allāh as Rabb).

7. **The "lā tubqī wa-lā tadhar" Hellfire formula.** [74:28] uses *bqy* in inverted form for Hellfire. Investigate the Hellfire-vocabulary cluster: does Hellfire systematically invert divine attributes? *bqy* (remaining) → *lā tubqī* (does not let-remain). Are there other inversions?

8. **The 99-names extraction process.** *al-Bāqī* and *al-Jalīl* and *al-Karīm* and *al-Qayyūm* are 99-name fixtures, but only *al-Qayyūm* is Quranically titular as a name. Why does the tradition extract names from predicates? Is there a Quranic principle about which predicates can become names?

9. **Khattab's translation choices.** Across this investigation, Khattab consistently paraphrases *wajh* into "pleasure" or "Himself," and adds modal hedges (*"bound to perish"*). His translation is a window into a particular Sunni-modernist exegetical tradition. A separate study could map his interpretive moves.

10. **The 30+ eschatological-face verses.** The §4.6 cluster (faces at resurrection — radiant, dark, humbled, dragged) is the largest single cluster of *wajh* uses. The cosmic-remnant face ([28:88], [55:27]) and the eschatological-face cluster ([75:22], [88:2], etc.) interlock at [20:111] (faces humbled before the Living Self-Subsisting). The full poetics of "face" at resurrection deserves its own deep-dive.

## Methodology Notes

**Approach order.** Loaded the three target roots (*wjh*, *bqy*, *fny*) from `roots.json` first to get scale (78 / 21 / **1**). The hapax-status of *fny* was the immediate pivot. Then traced *hlk* as the comparison root to figure out why [28:88] uses *hlk* and [55:26] uses *fny*. Pulled all relevant verses from `quran.json` and the four sentence translators in parallel. Then checked translation-divergence for every verse — the *fān* verse [55:26] turned out to have the highest divergence in the entire study (0.7874), which was a surprise and confirmed that the perishing-word does the most translator-resistant work.

**Data-grounded.** All counts (78, 21, 1, 68); the lemma tables; the translation strings; the divergence scores; the morphology of [28:88], [55:26–27], [18:46]; the partitioning of *abqā*-formula across 7 verses.

**Interpretive (LLM-supplied).** The "cosmic clock has an off-switch" framing; the five functional patterns of *wajh* (the partition is mine — the data does not flag it); the "property vs state" distinction between *bqy* and *xld*; the read of Khattab's translation as Mu'tazilite; the read of the active participle as "state-as-identity"; the synthesis claim that the moral life *links* into divine remaining via the seeking-face circuit.

**Decisive move.** Recognizing that *fny* is a hapax and using that as the architectural anchor. The single occurrence of *fanā* at [55:26] forced the entire study to organize around the *hlk*/*fny* pairing and the asymmetry between event-perishing and ontological-perishing. Without that, the study would have been "wjh + bqy"; with it, the study became "wjh-bqy as cosmic clock with two off-switches."

**What I'd do differently.** Should have checked the morphology of [55:26]'s *ʿalayhā* pronoun antecedent earlier — the grammatical underdetermination is theologically productive but I treated it as a footnote. Should have surveyed the eschatological-face cluster (§4.6) more systematically — 30+ verses warrant a separate map, but I gave them a paragraph.
