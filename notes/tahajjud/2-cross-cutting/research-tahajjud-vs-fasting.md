# The night vigil and the fast — how they differ in kind

> Phase-2 cross-cutting. Reads across `notes/tahajjud/{0-structural,1-roots,1-narratives}/` and the
> fasting synthesis `notes/fasting/concept-fasting-deep.md`. Every claim is marked **[data]**
> (counted, verified against `data/morphology/words.jsonl`) or **[interp]**. Hadith and classical
> tafsir are **outside this evidence base**; §9 names where the later tradition is load-bearing.

---

## Headline finding (100 words)

The two practices differ in grammatical person before they differ in anything else. **Every
night-worship imperative in the Quran is second-person masculine singular, addressed to the Prophet;
every fasting imperative is second-person masculine plural, addressed to the believers.** [data] The
verb `qum` is never pluralised. The one verse that switches the vigil to plural — 73:20 — changes the
content in the same breath: recite what is easy, pray, give zakāt, lend, seek forgiveness. Fasting
suspends hunger, which the Quran never praises; the vigil suspends sleep, which the Quran calls a
sign and a mercy. On the text's own terms the fast is the heavier obligation, by a wide margin.

## 1. The anchor: person and number

I verified this directly in the morphology, tag by tag.

**Night-worship — six imperatives, all `IMPV 2MS`, all in passages addressing the Prophet by singular
pronoun.** [data]

| Ref | Imperative | Root | Tag | Addressee marker in context |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 73:2 | `qumi` — stand | `qwm` | IMPV 2MS | `yā ayyuhā al-muzzammil` (73:1) |
| 17:79 | `fa-tahajjad` | `hjd` | IMPV (V) 2MS | `nāfilatan **laka**`, same clause |
| 20:130 | `fa-sabbiḥ` | `sbH` | IMPV (II) 2MS | `fa-ṣbir ʿalā mā yaqūlūn`, same verse |
| 50:40 | `fa-sabbiḥhu` | `sbH` | IMPV (II) 2MS | `iṣbir` (50:39) |
| 52:49 | `fa-sabbiḥhu` | `sbH` | IMPV (II) 2MS | `iṣbir li-ḥukmi rabbik` (52:48) |
| 76:26 | `fa-sjud` + `wa-sabbiḥhu` | `sjd`/`sbH` | IMPV 2MS | `ʿalayka` (76:23) |

**There is no plural night-standing imperative anywhere in the Quran.** [data] The form-I imperative
`qum` occurs exactly **twice** in the corpus — 73:2 and 74:2 (`qum fa-andhir`) — both singular, both
to the same wrapped figure. The plural `qūmū` occurs **once**, at 2:238 (`wa-qūmū li-llāhi qānitīn`),
in a verse about guarding the prescribed prayers, not the night. [data] The plural *exists* in the
Quran's vocabulary of standing. It is simply never pointed at the night.

**Fasting — plural throughout.** [data] The imposition at 2:183 is not an imperative at all; it is a
passive perfect with a plural addressee.

> **2:183** — يَـٰٓأَيُّهَا ٱلَّذِينَ ءَامَنُوا۟ كُتِبَ عَلَيۡكُمُ ٱلصِّيَامُ … لَعَلَّكُمۡ تَتَّقُونَ
> *"O you who have believed, fasting is decreed upon you… that you may guard yourselves."*
> `kutiba` — PERF PASS 3MS; `ʿalaykum` — 2MP; `tattaqūna` — IMPF 2MP.

Then the imperatives arrive, all `IMPV 2MP`, in 2:187: `bāshirūhunna`, `wa-btaghū`, `wa-kulū`,
`wa-shrabū`, `atimmū`. Stated precisely, because it sharpens rather than weakens the finding: **four
of the five are permissions** (approach your wives, seek, eat, drink). **The Quran's single imperative
commanding the fast is `atimmū` — "complete it" — and it is plural.** [data] The only other fasting
verb-forms in the founding passage are `taṣūmū` (IMPF 2MP, 2:184) and `fal-yaṣumhu` (jussive 3MS,
2:185, "let him fast it"). [data]

**The hinge.** 73:20 is where the Quran finally speaks to a plural about the night — and changes what
it asks for. Six imperative tokens, five distinct verbs, all `IMPV 2MP`: [data]

> **73:20** — عَلِمَ أَن لَّن تُحۡصُوهُ فَتَابَ عَلَيۡكُمۡ فَٱقۡرَءُوا۟ مَا تَيَسَّرَ مِنَ ٱلۡقُرۡءَانِ …
> وَأَقِيمُوا۟ ٱلصَّلَوٰةَ وَءَاتُوا۟ ٱلزَّكَوٰةَ وَأَقۡرِضُوا۟ ٱللَّهَ قَرۡضًا حَسَنࣰا … وَٱسۡتَغۡفِرُوا۟ ٱللَّهَ
> *"He knew you would not be able to count it, so He turned to you — so recite what is easy of the
> Qurʾān… and establish the prayer, and give the zakāt, and lend God a goodly loan… and seek God's
> forgiveness."*

`fa-qraʾū` ×2, `wa-aqīmū`, `wa-ātū`, `wa-aqriḍū`, `wa-staghfirū`. [data] Note what is *not* in that
list: any verb of standing at night. `wa-aqīmū` is form IV of `qwm` — the "establish prayer" formula,
which across all 54 of its occurrences never once describes a body rising to its feet. [data]

**[interp] — and this is the file's central claim.** When the Quran addresses a group about the
night, it does not give them the vigil in a smaller size. It gives them something else: recitation
scaled to capacity, plus the ordinary obligations, plus two giving-clauses. The vigil proper stays in
the singular. The three portraits of night-worshippers — 25:64 `yabītūna`, 51:17 `kānū… yahjaʿūn`,
32:16 `tatajāfā` — are all third-person habitual reports of a type of person, never commands to a
reader. [data]

## 3. The two mechanism-verses: one subtractive, one descriptive

These are the only two places either practice gets anything like an explanation of how it works.

> **22:37** — لَن يَنَالَ ٱللَّهَ لُحُومُهَا وَلَا دِمَآؤُهَا وَلَٰكِن يَنَالُهُ ٱلتَّقۡوَىٰ مِنكُمۡ
> *"Their flesh will never reach God, nor their blood — but the taqwā from you reaches Him."*

> **73:6** — إِنَّ نَاشِئَةَ ٱلَّيۡلِ هِيَ أَشَدُّ وَطۡـࣰٔا وَأَقۡوَمُ قِيلًا
> *Arberry:* "surely the first part of the night is heavier in tread, more upright in speech."
> *Saheeh:* "more effective for concurrence [of heart and tongue] and more suitable for words."
> (Divergence 0.829; `nāshiʾa` and `waṭʾ` are both hapax.) [data]

**[data]** 22:37 belongs to sacrifice, not fasting — it enters the fasting reading because it shares
the formula `li-tukabbirū Allāha ʿalā mā hadākum` with 2:185, its only two occurrences in the corpus.
Its grammar is negative-then-adversative — `lan yanāla` … `wa-lākin yanāluhu` — and it tells you what
does not arrive. 73:6 is a bare comparative predication: `ashaddu waṭʾan`, `aqwamu qīlā`, two
elatives, no negation, no transfer.

**[interp]** **Fasting's mechanism-verse subtracts a candidate; the night's describes a condition.**
22:37 forecloses — the substance you gave up is not the offering, so stop looking for value in the
deprivation. 73:6 does the opposite: it makes a positive empirical claim about the hour, and 73:7
adds a second, flatly practical one — `inna laka fī al-nahāri sabḥan ṭawīlā`, the day is already
full. [data] Neither reason is that the night is holy. So the two are justified in opposite
directions: the fast by ruling out the wrong account of itself, the vigil by describing what the hour
is like. If you fast and feel nothing, 22:37 already said the feeling was never the cargo. If you
keep vigil and feel nothing, 73:6 has made a claim you can check — which makes the night the more
falsifiable practice, and the one where "nothing happened" carries more weight.

## 4. The Quran relaxes both practices with the same two moves

Verified globally, not asserted. [data]

1. **`ysr`.** Fasting: 2:185 `yurīdu Allāhu bikumu al-yusra wa-lā yurīdu bikumu al-ʿusr`. Vigil:
   73:20 `fa-qraʾū mā **tayassara** mina al-Qurʾān`, twice in one verse. Same root, both times the
   licence for scaling back.
2. **`fa-tāba ʿalaykum`.** The exact string occurs **three times**: 2:54 (the golden calf), 2:187
   (fasting), 73:20 (the vigil). Two of the three are worship-relaxations, and they are the only two.
   The shape is identical in both: God states a human limitation, turns to the people, *then* lightens
   the rule — *He knew you were betraying yourselves, so He turned to you and pardoned you* (2:187);
   *He knew you would not be able to count it, so He turned to you* (73:20).

**[interp]** This is the strongest structural rhyme between the two practices, and it is about the
Quran's procedure rather than about either practice. The relaxation is not a concession grudgingly
appended: the turning comes *before* the lightening, which means the lighter rule is not a lower
standard the failure earned — it is what the rule becomes once God has already absorbed the failure.
Neither practice, in the Quran's own handling, is a test you can fail by finding it hard. **The
exemptions differ by exactly one item**: 73:20 adds `yuqātilūna fī sabīl Allāh` to illness and travel.
[data] The vigil is excused for fighting; the fast is not.

## 5. The sharpest asymmetry: what each practice gives up

**Hunger. [data]** `jwE` occurs 5 times: a trial God sends (2:155), a punishment worn as a *garment*
(16:112), a promise of the garden — you will not be hungry there (20:118), hellfire's food which does
not avail against hunger (88:7), and a favour — He fed them *against* hunger (106:4). Every occurrence
is negative, twice its **removal** is the blessing, and none of the five is in a fasting verse. `ZmA`
(thirst, 3×) has the same shape. Hunger is never praised anywhere in the Quran, under any root.

**Sleep. [data]** The sleep vocabulary is tiny — about 15 words across `nwm` (9), `DjE` (3), `rqd`
(2), `hjE` (1) — and uniformly positive or neutral. Sleep is `min āyātihi`, among His signs (30:23),
listed alongside marriage and the diversity of languages. It is `subāt`, repose God *made*, paired
with night as a garment (25:47, 78:9). It is a battlefield mercy, `amanatan minhu`, a security from
Him (8:11). **Scanning all 15 occurrences: the Quran never once condemns sleep.** No lemma for
laziness, no verse pairing sleep with heedlessness as its cause, no general instruction to resist it.

**[interp] — and this is the difference in kind that matters most.** The two practices suspend things
with **opposite standing in the text**. The fast withholds a channel whose withholding the Quran never
celebrates — which is why 22:37 has to exist, and why the fasting investigation located the active
ingredient in what fills the emptied hours rather than in the emptying. The vigil withholds a thing
the Quran calls a sign, a mercy, and something God *made for you*.

Three consequences. **First**, the vigil cannot be framed as victory over a vice; nothing here
supports reading tahajjud as conquest. **Second**, it explains why the vigil is never pluralised: a
general obligation to withhold a general mercy would contradict eight separate ayahs stating night was
made for rest (`li-taskunū fīhi`, plus the `libās`/`subāt` pair). [data] The rest-vocabulary and the
vigil-vocabulary sit inside the same 81 `lyl` ayahs and **never address each other** — no verse says
"though night is for rest, stand anyway." [data] **Third**, and most usefully: the fast asks you to
stop taking something in; the vigil asks you to decline, for a portion of the night, a good thing God
is offering. The first is abstention. The second is a trade.

## 6. Both hedged — but toward different objects

**[data]** Fasting: `laʿallakum tattaqūn` (2:183, echoed at 2:187). That clause occurs twelve times
corpus-wide, and nine attach taqwā to received divine speech rather than to any physical act — the
fasting synthesis's central finding. Vigil: `ʿasā an yabʿathaka rabbuka maqāman maḥmūdā` (17:79).
Both are hope-particles, not promises.

**[data]** But the objects differ in category. `Taqwā` is a disposition — `wqy` occurs 258×, 166 of
them the verb `ittaqā`, a repeated transitive act of shielding against a named threat. `Maqām` is a
**station**; `maḥmūd` is a passive participle occurring exactly once, inside a root (`Hmd`, 63×)
otherwise saturated with praise directed at God. The verb is `yabʿathaka`, root `bEv` (67×), whose
noun `mabʿūthūn` (9×) is exclusively "we will be raised [from the dead]"; Saheeh renders 17:79
"**resurrect** you to a praised station," the other three "raise."

**[interp], held loosely.** The resurrection reading of `baʿatha` here is available but not forced —
the same verb sends messengers. What *is* firm is the object: fasting's hoped-for outcome happens
**in you**; the vigil's is a **position you are placed in**, whose content 17:79 never supplies. Add
32:17, where the night-portrait's reward is stated as guaranteed (`jazāʾan`) and then explicitly
hidden (`ukhfiya`) — a different silence from fasting's, which never mentions a reward at all. **The
fast hopes for a change in you now; the night hopes for a placement later, and declines to describe
it.**

## 7. Where they converge — and it matters as much

**[data]** Both cluster with giving. `Swm`'s strongest content co-roots are `TEm` (feed, 4 ayahs),
`skn` (needy, 4), `wjd` (afford, 4), `Sdq` (3); feeding-praise outnumbers fasting-praise roughly 3:1
by verse across five surahs. On the night side, of the four genuine personal-practice lists containing
a night-worship item, **4 of 4 name spending in the same breath**: 25:67, 3:17 (`al-munfiqīn`, same
verse), 51:19 (a `ḥaqq` in their wealth for the asker and the deprived), 32:16 (`wa-mimmā razaqnāhum
yunfiqūn`, same verse). And 73:20's plural relaxation adds **two** giving-clauses — zakāt and a goodly
loan — where the singular command had none. 33:35 puts both practices in one list: `al-qānitīn` third
of ten pairs, `al-ṣāʾimīn` eighth, with the reward attached to the whole list rather than any item.
(`Qānit` is tied to night hours only once, at 39:9, so that link is soft — flagged, not leaned on.)

**[interp]** **Neither practice is ever presented alone.** The fast is legislated inside a passage
whose surrounding vocabulary is feeding the poor. The vigil is, every time it is described, flanked by
generosity — and the two independent portraits (51:17–19, 32:16) land on the *same three-part sequence
in the same order*: leave the bed, address God, give money. [data] Whatever either practice is for,
the Quran does not treat it as a self-contained transaction between a person and God. Both terminate
outward.

## 8. Which is the heavier obligation? Plainly.

**Fasting. Not close.** [interp, on [data] throughout]

The Quran addresses you directly about fasting: `yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū, kutiba ʿalaykum` — plural,
and you are inside the plural. It names a month, counts days, prescribes make-ups for the ones missed,
issues a plural imperative to complete it, and states two exemptions twice over.

It has never addressed you in the imperative about the night at all. Every command to stand is
singular and lands on one man. No quantity is specified. The only passage that quantifies it dissolves
its own numbers into "whatever is easy" in the same verse. The one word that names the practice calls
it `nāfila` — surplus, beyond what was owed, a lemma whose only other occurrence (21:72) describes a
grandson given as a bonus beyond what was asked. [data] And the founding command builds its exemption
in from the first utterance: `qumi al-layla **illā qalīlā**`.

So the difficulty framing is inverted relative to the text. **The practice you found hard and
completed was the obligation. The practice you suspect is harder is the one the Quran never obliged
you to.** [interp]

Two qualifications. First, this does not make the vigil small — uncommanded is not unimportant, and
§7 shows the Quran describes night-worshippers with more warmth than it ever spends on fasters.
Second, difficulty and obligation are different axes: the vigil may well be experientially harder
*because* nothing carries it. A fast has a start time, a community, and a calendar. A vigil at 3 a.m.
has none of those. The text does not contradict that — it just declines to convert it into a duty.

## 9. So what is the night vigil, if not an obligation?

**[interp], flagged as the file's most interpretive move.** The Quran gives it a category the
commissioner probably does not have a slot for: **an invitation, with a described type of person
attached.**

The evidence is threefold. **(a)** The commands exist but are singular and Prophet-directed — the text
has an imperative available and chooses not to generalise it. [data] **(b)** The descriptions are
third-person habitual and retrospective: 51:15–19 narrates people *already in the garden* and explains
backwards what they used to be like; 32:15–17 offers the behaviour as evidence of belief, not its
precondition. [data] **(c)** Where a plural is addressed about the night, the ask changes to
recitation-at-capacity plus giving. [data]

That is neither a rule nor a mere suggestion. It is a portrait the text repeats twice, independently,
in the same three-part order — and lets you decide whether you recognise yourself in it. Its closest
structural relative is not 2:183 but 25:63–74, where night-standing is **one `wa-lladhīna` clause
among twelve**, given the same grammatical weight as "they do not bear false witness." [data]

**Outside the evidence base, and it matters here more than anywhere.** Almost everything that makes
tahajjud feel like a heavy named institution — a rakʿah count, the last third of the night, its rank
among voluntary prayers, its place in a nightly schedule — comes from the hadith corpus and the later
tradition, not from this text. The Quran supplies no count, no hour, no frequency, and never calls the
vigil `ṣalāh`. [data] That is a statement about **where the authority for the practice's form comes
from**, not a criticism of the tradition, and this investigation cannot adjudicate it. But it explains
the mismatch you are feeling: you are comparing a Quranic obligation against a largely post-Quranic
institution, and finding the second heavier.

## For the practice

- **Stop ranking them.** They are not two difficulties on one scale. Fasting is a plural obligation
  with numbers; the vigil is a singular invitation with none. Holding the vigil as a duty you are
  failing is a category error the text does not support.
- **Fasting is the one you owe — keep it, and furnish it.** 22:37 already told you the hunger is not
  the offering, so what goes into the emptied hours is the only part that can fail.
- **The vigil is the one you accept, not the one you complete.** There is no quantity to hit. 73:20 is
  God replacing His own numbers with "what is easy"; that replacement *is* the instruction.
- **Do not fight your sleep.** The Quran calls it a sign, a repose and a mercy, and never condemns it.
  Take a portion — all `min al-layl` ever says — and let the rest of the night be what the text says
  it is.
- **Put something in it.** The night's specified content is recitation at the pace the Qurʾān itself
  came down (73:4 / 25:32), and the commonest instruction for that slot is `sabbiḥ`, not petition:
  three of the four `wa-min al-layli fa-` verses say glorify.
- **Test 73:6 rather than believing it.** Impression lands harder, speech comes out straighter — the
  only experiential claim either practice makes. Try it and see. The fast never offered you one, so
  feeling nothing there was never evidence of anything.
- **Attach money to both.** Four of four night-worship practice-lists name giving in the same breath;
  fasting's strongest co-roots are feeding and the needy. Neither practice appears alone in this text.
- **A workable shape:** keep the obligation with its numbers, and hold the night as ten unhurried
  minutes with the Qurʾān on the nights you wake anyway — no count, no target, no failure available.
  The Quran never gave you a quota to miss.
