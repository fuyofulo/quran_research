# Sleep vocabulary — nwm, hjE, rqd, DjE (+ sbt/skn glances)

## Headline finding (100 words)

The Quran's sleep vocabulary is tiny — about 15 words total (`nwm` 9, `DjE` 3, `rqd` 2, `hjE` 1) against
a night-vocabulary in the hundreds. And it is overwhelmingly positive: sleep is named a **sign**
(30:23), a **mercy/security** (8:11, drowsiness before battle), and God-given **rest** (`subāt`, 25:47,
78:9). The same verb `tawaffā` — "to take a soul" — covers both death and sleep (39:42, 6:60): sleep is
death's nightly rehearsal, not its opposite. Against this, 51:17's "little sleep" is a **report** of an
already-rewarded people's past habit, not a command — grammatically unambiguous. The Quran never once
condemns sleep.

## 1. The full inventory

| Root | Lemma | Count | Sense |
|---|---|---|---|
| `nwm` | manām | 4 | sleep/dream (8:43, 30:23, 37:102, 39:42) |
| `nwm` | nawm | 3 | sleep (2:255, 25:47, 78:9) |
| `nwm` | nāʾimūn | 2 | asleep (7:97, 68:19) |
| `rqd` | ruqūd | 1 | asleep (18:18, Cave Sleepers) |
| `rqd` | marqad | 1 | sleeping-place (36:52, Cave Sleepers waking) |
| `DjE` | maḍājiʿ | 3 | beds/lying-places (3:154, 4:34, 32:16) |
| `hjE` | yahjaʿūn | 1 | to sleep (51:17) — hapax |

**Total: 15 word-occurrences across 4 roots.** For comparison, the broader night corpus (`lyl` alone)
runs to 92 occurrences, and worship-roots attached to night run into the hundreds. Sleep gets almost no
vocabulary of its own — it is mentioned, not elaborated. There is no verb "to sleep well," no noun for
insomnia, no idiom for napping. The Quran names sleep only when it is doing theological work: as sign,
as mercy, as death's analogue, or (once) as something minimized by an already-rewarded people. `DjE`
is worth flagging precisely: its lemma *maḍājiʿ* means "beds/places of lying down," not sleep itself —
3:154 uses it for death-beds, 4:34 for the marital bed, and only 32:16 links it to night-rising. It
belongs to this file because 32:16 is the "two portraits" verse, not because the root denotes sleep as
an act.

`sbt` (subāt, "repose") and `skn` (rest) appear only as qualifiers of sleep in 25:47 and 78:9 — not as
independent roots with their own occurrence counts in the concept files pulled here; they modify `nawm`
rather than naming a separate category. Treat them as descriptors, not a fifth vocabulary.

## 2. Sleep is a sign and a mercy — stated explicitly (data-grounded)

Four passages make this an assertion, not an inference:

- **30:23** — *wa-min āyātihi manāmukum bi-l-layli wa-l-nahār* — "among His signs is your sleep by
  night and day." Sleep sits in the same signs-list as marriage (30:21) and language diversity (30:22).
- **78:9** / **25:47** — sleep is *subāt*, paired with night as *libās* (garment) and day as *nushūr*
  (resurrection/going-forth). The vocabulary is deliberately gentle: clothing, repose, rising.
- **8:11** — battlefield mercy before the fight later called Badr (root `nEs`, drowsiness, not `nwm`,
  but grouped with this material because it is the same phenomenon): God covers the anxious army in
  sleep as *amanatan minhu* — "a security from Him."

**Panel 1 — 25:47**
> وَهُوَ ٱلَّذِي جَعَلَ لَكُمُ ٱلَّيۡلَ لِبَاسًا وَٱلنَّوۡمَ سُبَاتًا وَجَعَلَ ٱلنَّهَارَ نُشُورًا
> Saheeh: "And it is He who has made the night for you as clothing and sleep [a means for] rest and
> has made the day a resurrection." · Pickthall: "hath appointed the night as a cloak and sleep as a
> repose" · Khattab: "made the night a cover and sleep for rest" · Arberry: "night for a garment, and
> sleep for a rest"

**Panel 2 — 8:11**
> إِذۡ يُغَشِّيكُمُ ٱلنُّعَاسَ أَمَنَةً مِّنۡهُ
> Saheeh: "when He overwhelmed you with drowsiness [giving] security from Him" · Pickthall: "He
> caused the slumber to fall upon you as a reassurance from Him" · Khattab: "cast drowsiness over you
> as a reassurance from Him" · Arberry: "when He was casting slumber upon you as a security from Him"

**The tension, stated plainly (interpretive framing of data-grounded facts):** the Quran calls sleep a
sign and a mercy in these passages, and elsewhere praises people who take *little* of it (51:17). Both
are true statements about the text. This file does not resolve that — it maps it for the cross-cutting
agent. One structural note that bears on the resolution: the "sleep is a mercy" verses are all general
statements about sleep as a universal human/creaturely condition (everyone sleeps, and this is good);
51:17 is a specific report about what one already-rewarded group chose to do with their share of it.
The tension may be between the *baseline good* and a *supererogatory reduction* of that good — not
between two competing verdicts on sleep itself. That reading is offered, not proven.

## 3. Sleep as rehearsal for death — the `tawaffā` link (data-grounded, the strongest finding)

**Panel 3 — 39:42**
> ٱللَّهُ يَتَوَفَّى ٱلۡأَنفُسَ حِينَ مَوۡتِهَا وَٱلَّتِي لَمۡ تَمُتۡ فِي مَنَامِهَا فَيُمۡسِكُ ٱلَّتِي
> قَضَىٰ عَلَيۡهَا ٱلۡمَوۡتَ وَيُرۡسِلُ ٱلۡأُخۡرَىٰٓ إِلَىٰٓ أَجَلٍ مُّسَمًّى
> Saheeh: "Allah takes the souls at the time of their death, and those that do not die [He takes]
> during their sleep. Then He keeps those for which He has decreed death and releases the others for
> a specified term."

The verb is *yatawaffā* — root `wfy`, "to take in full, to collect what is due." It is the Quran's
standard verb for **dying** (used for the Prophet's death, for the death of disbelieving nations,
etc.), and here it is used for **sleep with no modification whatsoever** — same verb, same grammatical
subject (God), same tense. The only distinction the verse draws is *outcome*: some sleepers are kept
(they die), some are released (they wake). Sleep is not compared to death; it is described using
death's own vocabulary, differentiated only by what happens after.

6:60 reinforces this with the night specifically: *wa-huwa alladhī yatawaffākum bi-l-layli* — "He is
the one who takes you by night" — followed immediately by *thumma yabʿathukum fīhi* ("then He revives
you therein"), using *baʿth*, the same root used for resurrection. Night sleep is described with a
death-word going in and a resurrection-word coming out, nightly.

**What this implies (interpretive):** if going to sleep is, in the Quran's own terminology, indistinguishable
in kind from dying — a temporary surrender of the soul, returned only by God's choice — then staying
awake at night is not opting out of something neutral. It is declining a nightly small death. That
reframes the vigil: it is not primarily "resisting the urge to rest" but "choosing to remain answerable,
awake and reciting, at the hour when one would otherwise have handed the self over." This is the file's
single strongest theological point and should carry weight in synthesis.

## 4. 51:17 — command or report? (flat answer: **report**)

**Panel 4 — 51:17** (with 51:16 for grammatical context)
> ٥١:١٦ ءَاخِذِينَ مَآ ءَاتَىٰهُمۡ رَبُّهُمۡ إِنَّهُمۡ كَانُواْ قَبۡلَ ذَٰلِكَ مُحۡسِنِينَ
> ٥١:١٧ كَانُواْ قَلِيلًا مِّنَ ٱلَّيۡلِ مَا يَهۡجَعُونَ
> Saheeh 17: "They used to sleep but little of the night" · Pickthall: "They used to sleep but little
> of the night" · Khattab: "they used to sleep only little in the night" · Arberry: "Little of the
> night would they slumber"

The grammar is unambiguous: **`kānū` + imperfect verb (`mā yahjaʿūn`) is the Arabic habitual-past
construction** — "they used to / they would." It is the identical construction used one verse earlier
for *kānū ... muḥsinīn*, "they were doers of good" (51:16), and for *kānū āminīn* elsewhere in the
Quran. This is descriptive narration of a settled disposition, not an imperative verb form (which
would be `iqlilū min al-nawm` or similar — the Quran does not say that anywhere).

And critically: **the report follows the reward, it does not precede it as its condition.** 51:15
opens *"inna al-muttaqīn fī jannātin wa-ʿuyūn"* — the righteous are [already, as narrated] in gardens
and springs — before 51:16-18 lists, in the past tense, what they had been like: doers of good,
sparing sleepers, dawn-forgiveness-seekers, generous with wealth. **51:17 is one clause in a four-part
biographical sketch of people already being rewarded, not a legislative instruction to the reader.**
This distinguishes it sharply from 73:2's `qumi al-layl` — an actual imperative, second person, verb-
initial command.

## 5. The `qalīlan` problem — real or cosmetic divergence?

**Panel 5 — 73:2**
> قُمِ ٱلَّيۡلَ إِلَّا قَلِيلًا
> Saheeh: "Stand [in prayer] the night, except for a little"

Grammatically the two `qalīlan`s do different jobs. In 73:2, `qalīlan` is the object of an exception
particle (`illā`) attached to an **imperative verb** (`qum`, "stand") — it marks the portion *exempted*
from standing, i.e., the part you may spend asleep. In 51:17, `qalīlan` is an accusative of extent
modifying an **indicative habitual verb** (`yahjaʿūn`, "they sleep") inside a `kāna`-report — it marks
the portion of the night that *was in fact* spent sleeping. One is a legal exemption from a command;
the other is a biographical measurement of an outcome. **They are consistent in substance** (both
describe a night mostly spent awake, with a small remainder for sleep) but **different in grammatical
function** (command-with-exception vs. report-with-measurement).

The translator split noted in the brief — "little of the night did they sleep" (Saheeh, Pickthall,
front-loaded for emphasis) vs. "they used to sleep only little in the night" (Khattab, plain adverbial
order) vs. "Little of the night would they slumber" (Arberry, poetic fronting) — is a **word-order and
register choice, not a semantic disagreement.** This is confirmed quantitatively: 51:17's measured
translation divergence is **0.35**, far below 73:2's own divergence (0.70) and nowhere near the
corpus's genuinely contested verses (73:1 at 0.93, 73:7 at 0.92). **The divergence here is cosmetic.**

## 6. Negative space — does the Quran ever condemn sleep?

Scanning all 15 occurrences across `nwm`, `hjE`, `rqd`, `DjE`: **no.** The closest candidates are 7:97
and 68:19, where sleep is the state during which a divine reckoning falls on the heedless (townspeople
in 7:97, garden-schemers in 68:19) — but in both, the moral fault named is arrogance/false security and
withheld charity, never the act of sleeping itself; sleep is simply the hour of vulnerability the
narrative uses. 3:154 and 4:34 use `maḍājiʿ` for death-beds and marital estrangement respectively —
again not a critique of sleep as such. There is no lemma anywhere in this vocabulary meaning "laziness,"
no verse pairing sleep with heedlessness (`ghafla`) as its cause, no verse instructing believers to
resist or minimize sleep as a general rule. **The night vigil is never framed, anywhere in this
vocabulary, as a victory over sleep.** Whatever the vigil is for, it is not fighting something the
Quran calls bad.

## For the night

- Sleep is not the enemy here — the text never says so. Waking at night is not a war against a vice.
- The `tawaffā` link means a night vigil can be held, quite literally in the Quran's own words, as
  choosing to remain "collected" rather than surrendered — an act of staying present, not of endurance.
- 51:17 describes people, it does not command you. If your own practice can't sustain "little sleep,"
  you are not violating an instruction — you are simply not yet the settled pattern that verse reports.
- The exemption in 73:2 (`illā qalīlā`) is built into the very first command about night-standing. Rest
  is not the failure mode; it is engineered into the practice from its founding verse.
- Because sleep is called a mercy, treat your own sleep, on the nights you take it, as itself a form of
  the sign the Quran points to — not as ground lost to the vigil.
