# Rabbit hole: `maqāman maḥmūdā` (17:79) — what is actually promised

## Headline finding (100 words)

`maḥmūd` is a hapax — the Quran calls God `ḥamīd` seventeen times but uses the passive participle only
here, of a human. `maqām` (14×) is mostly a place of standing, but its two closest parallels (14:14,
55:46, `maqāma rabbih`) are a standing *before* God in judgment, not a rank. `baʿatha` is a resurrection
verb in the majority (37/67, 55%) of its uses, `yabʿathaka` among them — this is eschatological, not a
felt state after prayer. `ʿasā` is grammatically a hedge God uses fifteen times about His own action, never
resolved into flat promise. And every verb is singular: **You** cannot cleanly claim this station from the
grammar alone.

---

> **17:79** — وَمِنَ ٱلَّيۡلِ فَتَهَجَّدۡ بِهِۦ نَافِلَةࣰ لَّكَ عَسَىٰٓ أَن يَبۡعَثَكَ رَبُّكَ مَقَامࣰ ا مَّحۡمُودࣰ ا
> "And from part of the night, pray with it as additional [worship] for you; it is expected that your
> Lord will resurrect you to a praised station." (Saheeh)

## 1. `maḥmūd` — the only passive praise-word in the Quran

Root `Hmd`: 63 occurrences, 5 lemmas, entirely data-grounded from `data/concepts/Hmd-حمد.json`:

| Lemma | Gloss | Count | POS |
|---|---|---|---|
| `Hamod` (ḥamd) | praise (the act/noun) | 43 | N |
| `Hamiyd` (ḥamīd) | praiseworthy (of God) | 17 | ADJ (16), N (1) |
| `yuHomadu` | "they will be praised" | 1 (3:188) | V |
| `Ha`miduwn` | "those who praise" | 1 (9:112) | N |
| **`m~aHomuwd` (maḥmūd)** | **"praised"** | **1 (17:79)** | **N** |

`ḥamīd` is exclusively divine — every one of its 17 occurrences is a name of God ("and He is al-Ḥamīd,"
e.g. 14:1, 22:64, 31:26, 35:15, 42:28, 57:24, 85:8), an intensive active-sense adjective: God *is*
praiseworthy, inherently, whether or not anyone praises Him. `maḥmūd` is the opposite grammatical
shape — a passive participle. It does not describe a quality; it describes something **done to it**:
praised, by someone, at some point. The text never names who. Read straight, the passive voice leaves
the agent open — could be God praising the one who stood the vigil, could be the assembled creation on
the Day, could be both. That is not a gap to fill by inference; the ambiguity is the finding.
**Data-grounded: the passive/active contrast is structural. Who does the praising: unresolved.**

## 2. `maqām` — station, but of what kind

Root `qwm`, lemma `maqaAm`, 14 occurrences, all N:

| Ref | Sense |
|---|---|
| 2:125, 3:97 | `maqām Ibrāhīm` — a physical stone, place of prayer |
| 5:107 | legal/procedural position ("let two others stand in their place") |
| 10:71 | Noah's residence |
| **14:14** | **standing before God — "fears My station [khāfa maqāmī]"** |
| 17:79 | the term under study |
| 19:73 | rhetorical rank/status boast |
| 26:58, 44:26 | worldly rank ("treasures and honorable station," Pharaoh's people) |
| 27:39 | literal seat |
| 37:164 | angels' ordered cosmic rank |
| 44:51 | eschatological location |
| **55:46** | **standing before God — "fears the station of his Lord," two gardens** |
| 79:40 | standing before God — feared it, restrained the soul |

**Data-grounded: `maqām` across the corpus is not one thing.** It ranges over literal place (2:125,
10:71, 27:39), worldly rank (19:73, 26:58, 44:26), cosmic hierarchy (37:164), and — in three of its
fourteen occurrences (14:14, 55:46, 79:40) — a fixed idiom, `maqām(a) rabbih(i)`, "the station of his
Lord," always paired with *fearing* it and preceding eschatological reward. This idiom is a standing
**before** God, not a rank above others — the only recurring formula built on the noun.

> **55:46** — وَلِمَنۡ خَافَ مَقَامَ رَبِّهِۦ جَنَّتَانِ — "But for he who has feared the station of his
> Lord are two gardens." (Saheeh)

17:79 does not use the `maqāma rabbih` construction — no possessive pronoun anchors the station to God
as the one stood-before. It is simply `maqāman maḥmūdā`, unanchored. **Interpretive, but strongly cued
by the pattern:** given that `maqām`'s only repeated formula (14:14, 55:46, 79:40) treats it as a
position in the judgment scene — arrived at through fear of God, rewarded there — and given that
`yabʿathaka` (§4) is independently a resurrection verb, reading 17:79's station as *also* a position in
a judgment scene, not an earthly or angelic rank, is well supported, though not proven by `maqām` alone;
the word is too polysemous for that.

## 3. `ʿasā` — hedge or rhetorical softening?

Root `Esy` (ع س ي, distinct from `ESy` ع ص ي "disobey" — a documented case-collision pair), single
lemma `EasaY`, ~29 verbal occurrences found in the morphology data. Sorting by grammatical subject:

**God (Allah/Rabb) as explicit subject** — 15 occurrences: 4:84, 4:99, 5:52, 7:129, 9:102, 12:83,
17:8, **17:79**, 18:24, 18:40, 28:22, 60:7, 66:5, 66:8, 68:32.

**Human or impersonal subject** — the rest: 2:216 (×2), 2:246, 4:19, 7:185, 9:18, 12:21, 17:51 (the
resurrection, impersonal), 18:19b, 19:48, 27:72, 28:9, 28:67, 47:22, 49:11 (×2).

> **17:8** — عَسَىٰ رَبُّكُمۡ أَن يَرۡحَمَكُمۡ — "It may be that your Lord will have mercy on you."

**Data-grounded:** every `ʿasā rabbukum` / `ʿasā Allāhu` construction (mercy 17:8, victory 5:52,
forgiveness 4:99/9:102, better spouses 66:5, wiping sins 66:8, guidance 18:24/28:22, better than the
garden 18:40, reconciling enemies 60:7, patience rewarded 12:83) is treated by surrounding text as
worth hoping and acting toward, and none is ever negated or walked back. **Interpretive — genuinely
contested:** classical exegesis treats "ʿasā min Allāh wājibah" (an ʿasā from God is a certainty) as
near-consensus, since God does not idly speculate about His own action. But the word's morphology does
not change with subject — `ʿasā` is an ordinary hedge for humans (2:216, 9:18, 47:22). Honest position:
the never-falsified pattern supports a softened reading, but the grammar alone does not settle it; God's
`ʿasā` could remain a real, if very likely, contingency whose fulfillment depends on the vigil being kept.

## 4. `yabʿathaka` — which `baʿatha` is this

Root `bEv`, 67 total occurrences across all forms. Classified by sense (data-grounded, from
`data/concepts/bEv.json` cross-checked against Saheeh International per-ayah text):

| Sense | Occurrences | Share |
|---|---|---|
| **Resurrection / raising the dead / Day of Resurrection** | **37** | **55%** |
| Sending a prophet/messenger | 15 | 22% |
| Other (sending a king, arbitrator, animal, punishers, waking sleepers, an agent for a task) | 15 | 22% |

The resurrection sense covers the main verb in contexts like 6:36 ("the dead — Allah will resurrect
them"), 22:7 ("Allah will resurrect those in the graves"), 58:6/58:18 ("the Day Allah will resurrect
them all"), the noun `mabʿūthūn` (9×, disbelievers denying "we will not be raised"), and the noun
`baʿth` (4×, "yawm al-baʿth," the Day of Resurrection, e.g. 30:56). The messenger sense is the classic
prophetic formula ("We sent a messenger to every nation," 16:36). 17:79's `yabʿathaka` is grammatically
identical to the resurrection-sense majority — second person, future, God as agent, no object but the
addressee's own person.

**Data-grounded: `baʿatha` is majority a resurrection verb, at better than 2:1 odds against
messenger-sending alone.** Combined with `maqām`'s judgment-scene idiom (§2), this is the strongest
textual case in the file: **17:79 promises an eschatological station, not a felt spiritual state after
tonight's prayer.** Whatever the vigil does to a person now, the text's vocabulary places the *named*
reward on the Day, not before it.

## 5. Who is being addressed

Every verb from 17:78–79 is second-person singular: `aqim`, `tahajjad`, `bihi... laka`, `yabʿathaka`,
`rabbuka`. Nothing in the grammar marks the addressee as anyone other than whoever the surah's "you" is
throughout 17:73-80 — contextually Muhammad (surrounding verses concern threats to drive him from the
land, 17:76, and his own night-recitation being witnessed, 17:78). **Data-grounded: the addressee is
singular, in a passage otherwise addressed to the Prophet.**

Two things can both be true. Singular address to a named figure is the Quran's ordinary mode for
instructions later treated as general — 2:183's fasting command and 73:1-4's vigil command are also
direct address, and no one reads those as Muhammad-only. Separately, later tradition (tafsīr, ḥadīth on
shafāʿah) reads `maqām maḥmūd` confidently as the Prophet's unique station of intercession — flagged
here as **outside the Quran-only evidence base**, since nothing internal to 17:79 names intercession
and `maqām` elsewhere never carries that sense.

What a reader can honestly claim: the *practice* commanded is addressed the way most Quranic commands
are, and generalizing it fits how the rest of the corpus is read. What cannot be claimed: that the
reward-formula itself is textually promised to any reader — the grammar attaches it to the same "you"
as the command, with no extending clause of the kind found elsewhere (fasting's "as it was prescribed
for those before you," 2:183). That silence is worth weighting.

## 6. Compared to 32:17 — two refusals to specify

> **32:17** — فَلَا تَعۡلَمُ نَفۡسࣱ مَّآ أُخۡفِيَ لَهُم مِّن قُرَّةِ أَعۡيُنࣲ — "No soul knows what has
> been hidden for them of comfort for eyes, a reward for what they used to do."

32:17 sits with those "whose sides forsake their beds" (32:16) — explicitly night-vigil adjacent,
explicitly plural/general. Its reward is **promised and withheld**: named as real, described as hidden,
refused all content. 17:79's reward is the mirror image: **named and unexplained** — `maqāman maḥmūdā`
gives a label but no content for what the station is, who praises, or when it is experienced beyond
what `yabʿatha` implies about timing.

**Interpretive:** side by side, the pair forecloses any psychological or experiential expectation. 32:17
blocks it explicitly; 17:79 blocks it by omission — a bare label, singular address, on a resurrection
verb. Neither gives a worshipper a state to chase tonight; both push the reward onto the Day. 17:79 only
looks like an exception to "no reward is described" until its vocabulary is checked.

## For the night

- Don't treat `maqāman maḥmūdā` as a felt outcome to notice after tahajjud — `baʿatha` is a resurrection
  word in the majority of its uses; this is a Day-of-Judgment promise, not a mood.
- 17:79's grammar is singular, addressed to the Prophet; generalizing the *reward clause* to any reader
  is a step the text doesn't take, even though the *practice* it commands is reasonably read as general.
- `ʿasā` is a hedge that, everywhere else God uses it, is followed through — evidence, not proof; don't
  inflate it into a guarantee the grammar doesn't give.
- Read it beside 32:17, not alone: both night-reward verses refuse to describe the reward. Expecting a
  describable one from either asks the text for something it has twice declined to give.
- If the vigil is worth keeping, keep it as `nāfilatan laka` — extra, for its own sake — not for a
  station whose content, timing, and recipient the text leaves genuinely open.

For the night.
